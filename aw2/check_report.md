## Findings by item, most serious first

### 1. U2-P18, why-not C: made-up claim (factual error, not in the passage)
Current text: "Ibn Battuta says a stranger may marry if he wishes. It is not a rule for trade."
The adapted passage says nothing about marriage. The original Gibb text (p. 283) says that at a *hostelry* the keeper "purchases a slave-girl" if the visitor wants a concubine. That is not marriage, and the bank cut it on purpose.
**Replace with:** "why not C: Ibn Battuta says nothing about marriage. A visiting merchant could stay with a Muslim host or at an inn, and he could leave when he wished."

### 2. U2-P28, key D wording goes beyond the source
Current key: "(D) Salt mined in the Sahara sold for much more in Mali than near the mines."
The passage gives no price at or near Taghaza. It compares **Iwalatan (Walata)**, which the passage itself calls part of Mali ("the northernmost province") and which lies about 10+ nights south of the mines, with the **town of Malli** (the capital). A careful student could reject D. The explanation repeats the slip ("near the desert … in Mali").
**Replace option D with:** "(D) Salt from the Sahara mines sold for more the farther south it was carried."
**Replace explanation with:** "A load of salt sold for 8 to 10 mithqals at Walata, at the southern edge of the desert, and for 20 to 40 in the city of Mali, farther south. West Africa had gold but little salt, so salt was worth more the farther it traveled from the mines."
**Replace why-not B with:** "Taghaza is in the desert, far north of Mali's capital. Salt sold for up to four times as much in the capital as in Walata."
**Replace hint with:** "Compare the price of a load of salt at Iwalatan (Walata) and in the town of Malli."

### 3. U2-P06, key A includes a doubtful claim, and C uses a reused template
Current key: "(A) took longer and carried smaller loads, so it favored light, costly goods"
"Took longer" is neither supported nor safe. Sea voyages from the Gulf to China, with the waits for the monsoon (Ibn Battuta waited three months at Calicut), could take as long as Pegolotti's nine-month overland trip or longer. The explanation never backs up "took longer."
**Replace A with:** "(A) carried smaller loads at a higher cost, so it favored light, costly goods"
Option C ("run by a single trading company that controlled the whole route") is the same "single X controlled the route" distractor used in P29 B and E25 D (see item 14).
**Replace C with:** "(C) was carried out by Chinese merchants who traveled west to Tana to sell silk"
**Replace why-not C with:** "Pegolotti, an Italian, wrote this guide for European merchants starting from Tana. Merchants from many lands used the route."

### 4. U2-P11, distractor A is historically half-right
"(A) letting merchants ride the Emperor's post-horses for free." In the early Mongol Empire, merchants holding *paizas* did use the yam stations for free. Möngke later made commercial users pay because merchants abused it (Wikipedia, "Yam (route)"; Allsen). A well-read student could defend A.
**Replace A with:** "(A) giving merchants the right to take horses from anyone they met on the road"
**Replace why-not A with:** "Only messengers carrying a gerfalcon tablet could make other travelers give up their horses. Merchants could not."

### 5. Source P13, Source 2 (U2-P37–P39): dangling "this mosque"
The excerpt starts in the middle of Gibb's paragraph: "the veneration in which the Damascenes hold **this mosque**." In Gibb, "this mosque" is the Mosque of the Footprints, described in the sentences that were cut. Students will think it means "the Great Mosque" named two lines later.
**Replace the first sentence with:** "I saw a remarkable instance of the veneration in which the Damascenes hold [the Mosque of the Footprints*] during the great pestilence, on my return journey through Damascus in the latter part of July 1348."
**Then** take the asterisk off the later "made their way to the Mosque of the Footprints, and there …" Keep the footnote ("* a mosque just outside Damascus, highly honored by the city's people").

### 6. Source P16 (U2-P46–P48): dangling "These were the three chief routes"
The three routes are described in a passage that was cut (Gasquet, pp. 3–4: Baghdad to the Mediterranean; Baghdad to the Black Sea and Crimea; India by sea, then through the Red Sea and Egypt to Alexandria). P46 asks for the main argument of this paragraph.
**Replace the opening with:** "[Two caravan roads from Baghdad, one to the Mediterranean and one to the Black Sea, and a route from India by sea through Egypt] were the three chief routes by which communication between Asiatic countries and Europe was kept up …"

### 7. Source X7 / U2-E20: "before printing was invented" contradicts the rest of the bank
Smith & Karpinski (1911) say "some two hundred and fifty years before printing was invented." They mean Gutenberg. But P02's why-not says Chinese printers carved blocks "by the 800s," and P34–P35 teach Chinese block printing. E20's why-not C repeats the claim as fact.
**In the passage, replace with:** "… some two hundred and fifty years before printing was invented [in Europe]. …"
**Replace E20 why-not C with:** "C: Incorrect. The authors say this was about 250 years before printing came to Europe, so printing could not have spread the numerals then."

### 8. U2-P12, hint describes text that isn't in the passage, and gives the answer away

**Replace hint with:** "What job did the post stations do for the ruler? Look for a choice that did the same job in another empire."
