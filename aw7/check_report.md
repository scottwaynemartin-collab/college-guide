### 3. U7-P29 (and Source P10 chart note): [Major] The explanation misreads the data series; distractor B misdescribes the tariff

HSUS Series D 86 uses the standard BLS/Lebergott definitions, which count work-relief workers (WPA, CCC, and similar programs) as **unemployed**. Darby (1976, *Journal of Political Economy*; NBER w0088) shows that these series counted emergency workers as unemployed. Counting them as employed lowers the 1934–41 rates by 4–7 points.

So the 1933–37 drop in this chart is not the result of "programs such as the CCC and WPA hired millions." It reflects private-sector recovery, which the New Deal helped bring about through bank insurance, leaving gold, and deficit spending. The key can stay D, but its wording and the explanation must change.

Distractor B ("Smoot-Hawley Tariff, which opened American markets") describes a real law falsely and teaches a misconception. Use a true description that is simply the wrong cause.

```
- (B) the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, which raised taxes on goods brought into the country
- (D) New Deal spending and new laws that made banks and savings safer
KEY: D. Explanation: After Roosevelt took office in 1933, the government insured bank deposits, left the gold standard, and spent money on relief and public works. Businesses began to recover and hire again. These figures count people in work-relief jobs, such as the WPA, as unemployed, so the drop mostly shows private jobs coming back. Unemployment stayed high until war orders arrived.
  - why not B: The Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 raised taxes on imports. Other countries struck back, and world trade shrank, which made the Depression worse.
```

Add this sentence to the P10 chart note, after "...not counting people in the armed forces.":


### 4. U7-P43 (and P15 explanations): [Major] The key misstates what the passage shows

### 32. Length and absolute-word tells (h)
