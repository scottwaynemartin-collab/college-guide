## Unit 8, set X6
**Source 1**
> When the House met last Wednesday, we were all under the shadow of a great and imminent menace. War, in a form more stark and terrible than ever before, seemed to be staring us in the face. …
> … We did not go there to decide whether the predominantly German areas in the Sudetenland should be passed over to the German Reich. That had been decided already. …
> … The real triumph is that it has shown that representatives of four great Powers can find it possible to agree on a way of carrying out a difficult and delicate operation by discussion instead of by force of arms, and thereby they have averted a catastrophe which would have ended civilisation as we have known it. …
> … Ever since I assumed my present office my main purpose has been to work for the pacification of Europe, for the removal of those suspicions and those animosities which have so long poisoned the air. The path which leads to appeasement is long and bristles with obstacles. The question of Czechoslovakia is the latest and perhaps the most dangerous. Now that we have got past it, I feel that it may be possible to make further progress along the road to sanity. …
> … For a long period now we have been engaged in this country in a great programme of rearmament, which is daily increasing in pace and in volume. Let no one think that because we have signed this agreement between these four Powers at Munich we can afford to relax our efforts in regard to that programme at this moment.
— Neville Chamberlain, prime minister of Great Britain, speech to the House of Commons, October 3, 1938 (Text from Hansard, House of Commons Debates, 5th series, vol. 339, cols. 41–50 (October 3, 1938), UK Parliament, Open Parliament Licence v3.0; adapted)
**Source 2**
> … It was not for Serbia that we fought in 1914. It was not even for Belgium, although it occasionally suited some people to say so. We were fighting then, as we should have been fighting last week, in order that one great Power should not be allowed, in disregard of treaty obligations, of the laws of nations and the decrees of morality to dominate by brutal force the Continent of Europe. …
> … That is the deep difference between the Prime Minister and myself throughout these days. The Prime Minister has believed in addressing Herr Hitler through the language of sweet reasonableness. I have believed that he was more open to the language of the mailed fist. …
> … We are left with the additional serious commitment that we are guaranteeing a frontier that we have at the same time destroyed. We have taken away the defences of Czechoslovakia in the same breath as we have guaranteed them, as though you were to deal a man a mortal blow and at the same time insure his life. …
> … The Prime Minister has confidence in the good will and in the word of Herr Hitler, although when Herr Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles he undertook to keep the Treaty of Locarno, and when he broke the Treaty of Locarno he undertook not to interfere further, or to have further territorial aims, in Europe. When he entered Austria by force he authorised his henchmen to give an authoritative assurance that he would not interfere with Czechoslovakia. That was less than six months ago. … Do [his promises] mean that Herr Hitler will take "No" for an answer? He has never taken it yet. Or do they mean that he believes that he will get away with this, as he has got away with everything else, without fighting, by well-timed bluff, bluster and blackmail? …
— Duff Cooper, member of Parliament from Chamberlain's Conservative Party, explaining to the House of Commons why he had resigned from the cabinet, October 3, 1938 (Text from Hansard, House of Commons Debates, 5th series, vol. 339, cols. 32–39 (October 3, 1938), UK Parliament, Open Parliament Licence v3.0; adapted)
Glossary (Source 1): imminent menace = a danger that is about to happen; Sudetenland = the border regions of Czechoslovakia where many German speakers lived; the German Reich = Germany; averted = prevented; pacification = bringing peace; animosities = feelings of strong dislike; appeasement = keeping the peace by meeting the demands of a rival; bristles with = is full of
Glossary (Source 2): Serbia = the Balkan kingdom that Austria-Hungary attacked in 1914, starting World War I; Belgium = the neutral country Germany invaded in 1914, which brought Britain into the war; decrees of morality = the rules of right and wrong; sweet reasonableness = calm, polite argument; the mailed fist = the threat of force (a mailed fist is a fist in an armored glove); guaranteeing a frontier = promising to defend a border; at Munich, Britain offered to guarantee Czechoslovakia's new borders; the Treaty of Locarno = a 1925 treaty in which Germany accepted its borders with France and Belgium; bluster = loud, threatening talk

**E8-E15** [Topic 8.7 · LO J · Skill 3.C · Comparison · medium] Which of the following best describes a difference between the two sources?
- (A) Chamberlain wants Britain to stop building weapons, while Duff Cooper wants Britain to rearm.
- (B) Chamberlain praises settling the crisis by discussion, while Duff Cooper says Hitler respects only force.
- (C) Chamberlain trusts the League of Nations to protect Czechoslovakia, while Duff Cooper trusts Germany's written promises.
- (D) Chamberlain blames France for the crisis, while Duff Cooper blames Czechoslovakia.
KEY: B
Explanation: Chamberlain says the Munich meeting solved a hard problem "by discussion instead of by force of arms." Duff Cooper resigned from the cabinet in protest. He says Hitler gives way only to force, and he points to the promises Hitler had already broken.
Rationales: A: Incorrect. Chamberlain says Britain cannot "relax our efforts" on its "great programme of rearmament." He does not want Britain to stop building weapons. | B: Correct. Chamberlain praises solving the problem "by discussion instead of by force of arms." Duff Cooper says Hitler "was more open to the language of the mailed fist." | C: Incorrect. Neither speaker relies on the League. Duff Cooper distrusts Hitler's word and lists promises Hitler had broken. | D: Incorrect. Neither speaker blames France or Czechoslovakia. Duff Cooper says Britain has "taken away the defences of Czechoslovakia."


**E8-E16** [Topic 8.7 · LO J · Skill 4.B · Causation · medium] Chamberlain's view in Source 1 is best understood in the context of which of the following?
- (A) a British military alliance with the Soviet Union that protected Czechoslovakia
- (B) American promises to send an army to Europe if Germany attacked its neighbors
- (C) Germany's defeat by the Spanish Republic in the Spanish Civil War
- (D) widespread fear in Britain and France of another war like the First World War
KEY: D
Explanation: Chamberlain says war "in a form more stark and terrible than ever before" had seemed close. Many people in Britain and France feared another great war. Distrust of the Soviet Union and American isolationism also pushed them toward appeasement.
Rationales: A: Incorrect. Britain had no alliance with the Soviet Union in 1938. The Soviet Union was not invited to the Munich conference. | B: Incorrect. The United States followed a policy of isolation in the 1930s and made no such promise. | C: Incorrect. Germany helped Franco's Nationalists, who won the Spanish Civil War in 1939. | D: Correct. Memories of the millions killed in 1914–1918, and fear of bombing raids on cities, made many Britons and French people eager to avoid war.


**E8-E17** [Topic 8.7 · LO J · Skill 3.D · Causation · medium] Which of the following later developments most strongly supports Duff Cooper's doubts, in the fourth paragraph of Source 2, about Hitler's promises regarding Czechoslovakia?
- (A) The signing of the Nazi–Soviet Pact in August 1939
- (B) The German occupation of Prague in March 1939
- (C) The Italian invasion of Albania in April 1939
- (D) The German annexation of Memel in March 1939
KEY: B
Explanation: Duff Cooper lists promises Hitler had broken, including an assurance that he "would not interfere with Czechoslovakia." In March 1939 Hitler broke the Munich Agreement and took Prague. Britain and France then promised to defend Poland.
Rationales: A: Incorrect. The Nazi–Soviet Pact opened the way for Germany's attack on Poland. It did not bring the Czech lands under German rule. | B: Correct. In March 1939, less than six months after Munich, German troops occupied Prague and the rest of the Czech lands. Hitler broke the Munich Agreement, as Duff Cooper feared. | C: Incorrect. Italy, not Germany, invaded Albania. That did not affect Czechoslovakia. | D: Incorrect. Germany forced Lithuania to give up Memel. Memel was not part of Czechoslovakia.


**E8-E18** [Topic 8.8 · LO K · Skill 5.B · Causation · easy] Britain and France went to war with Germany less than a year after this debate in response to which of the following?
- (A) The German invasion of the Soviet Union
- (B) The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor
- (C) The German invasion of France
- (D) The German invasion of Poland
KEY: D
Explanation: Germany's attack on Poland was its first blitzkrieg. Tanks and planes crushed the Polish army in a few weeks. The Soviet Union invaded Poland from the east on September 17.
Rationales: A: Incorrect. Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, almost two years after Britain and France went to war. | B: Incorrect. Japan attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941. That brought the United States into the war. | C: Incorrect. Germany invaded France in May 1940, after Britain and France had already been at war with Germany for eight months. | D: Correct. Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and Britain and France declared war two days later.


## Unit 8, set P15
> Since England, despite her militarily hopeless situation still shows no sign of willingness to come to terms, I have decided to prepare a landing operation against England, and if necessary to carry it out.
> The aim of this operation is to eliminate the English homeland as a basis for the carrying on of the war against Germany, and if it should become necessary to occupy it completely. …
> 1. The Landing must be carried out in the form of a surprise crossing on a broad front approximately from Ramsgate to the area west of the Isle of Wight. … The preparations for the entire operation must be completed by mid-August.
> 2. To these preparations also belong the creation of those conditions which make a landing in England possible.
> a. The English air-force must morally and actually be so far overcome, that it does not any longer show any considerable aggressive force against the German attack. …
— Adolf Hitler, Führer and supreme commander of the German armed forces, General Order (Directive) No. 16, a top-secret order to the German military, July 16, 1940 (English translation by the U.S. prosecution staff at Nuremberg, Nazi Conspiracy and Aggression, vol. 3, pp. 399–400 (document 442-PS) (Washington: U.S. Government Printing Office, 1946); adapted)
Glossary: come to terms = agree to make peace; landing operation = an invasion by troops brought across the sea; from Ramsgate to the area west of the Isle of Wight = along England's southeastern and southern coast, facing France; morally and actually = in fighting spirit and in fact; aggressive force = power to attack

**E8-P43** [Topic 8.8 · LO K · Skill 1.A · easy] Hitler calls England's situation "militarily hopeless" in July 1940. Germany had just won its victories on the continent mainly by which method of war?
- (A) Trench warfare
- (B) Blitzkrieg
- (C) Naval blockade
- (D) Guerrilla warfare
KEY: B
Explanation: Blitzkrieg means "lightning war." Masses of tanks and planes broke through the enemy's lines and moved fast. In May and June 1940 Germany used it to defeat the Netherlands, Belgium, and France in about six weeks. British troops escaped from Dunkirk, but Britain now stood almost alone.
Why not: A: Trench warfare was slow fighting from fixed lines, as in the First World War. In 1940 the German army moved fast. | C: A naval blockade cuts off supplies by sea. Germany defeated France in a land campaign led by tanks and planes. | D: Guerrilla warfare is fought by small bands, often hiding among civilians. The German attacks were made by huge regular armies.
Hint: Think about how Germany defeated France in only about six weeks in 1940. What was that fast-moving kind of attack called?


**E8-P44** [Topic 8.8 · LO K · Skill 4.B · medium] Hitler's complaint that England "shows no sign of willingness to come to terms" is best understood in the context of which of the following?
- (A) Britain's refusal under Churchill to make peace after France fell
- (B) Britain's wartime alliance with the Soviet Union against Germany
- (C) the entry of the United States into the war on Britain's side
- (D) Britain's acceptance of German demands at the Munich Conference
KEY: A
Explanation: France signed an armistice with Germany on June 22, 1940. Hitler expected Britain to make peace too. Churchill's government refused to talk terms, so Hitler ordered plans for an invasion.
Why not: B: In July 1940 the Soviet Union was still bound to Germany by the Nazi–Soviet Pact. Britain and the Soviet Union became allies only after Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. | C: The United States entered the war in December 1941, after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. In 1940 it sold Britain supplies but stayed out of the fighting. | D: At Munich in 1938 Britain gave in to Hitler. In 1940 Britain did the opposite and refused to make peace.
Hint: The order is dated July 16, 1940. France had just surrendered. What did Britain's new government decide to do?


**E8-P45** [Topic 8.8 · LO K · Skill 5.B · Causation · medium] Which of the following best explains why Germany never carried out the landing operation ordered in the directive?
- (A) The United States entered the war against Germany in December 1941.
- (B) The Soviet army defeated a German army at Stalingrad in early 1943.
- (C) German troops were sent to help Italy in North Africa in early 1941.
- (D) The German air force failed to defeat the Royal Air Force in the Battle of Britain.
KEY: D
Explanation: Point 2a says the invasion depended on beating the British air force first. From July to October 1940, the German air force tried to destroy the Royal Air Force. British fighter pilots, helped by radar, held on. In September 1940 Hitler put off the invasion, and he soon turned his plans toward the Soviet Union.
Why not: A: The United States entered the war more than a year after Hitler had put off the invasion of Britain in September 1940. | B: The battle of Stalingrad came more than two years after Hitler had put off the invasion of Britain. | C: German troops went to North Africa in February 1941, months after Hitler had put off the invasion of Britain.
Hint: Point 2a names a condition that had to come first. Did Germany ever achieve it?


## Unit 9, set X1
> … The Three Powers note that the Polish Provisional Government in accordance with the decisions of the Crimea Conference* has agreed to the holding of free and unfettered elections as soon as possible on the basis of universal suffrage and secret ballot in which all democratic and anti-Nazi parties shall have the right to take part and to put forward candidates; and that representatives of the Allied Press shall enjoy full freedom to report to the world upon developments in Poland before and during the elections. …
> … The Three Governments have no doubt that in view of the changed conditions resulting from the termination of the war in Europe, representatives of the Allied Press will enjoy full freedom to report to the world upon developments in Roumania, Bulgaria, Hungary and Finland. …
> … The Three Governments, having considered the question in all its aspects, recognise that the transfer to Germany of German populations, or elements thereof, remaining in Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary, will have to be undertaken. They agree that any transfers that take place should be effected in an orderly and humane manner. …
> … The Czechoslovak Government, the Polish Provisional Government and the Control Council in Hungary are at the same time being informed of the above and are being requested meanwhile to suspend further expulsions …
> * The Crimea Conference was the meeting of the Allied leaders at Yalta in February 1945.
— Protocol of the Potsdam Conference, agreed by the leaders of the Soviet Union (Joseph Stalin), the United States (Harry Truman), and Britain (Clement Attlee), August 1, 1945 (Text from Foreign Relations of the United States: The Conference of Berlin (The Potsdam Conference), 1945, vol. II (Washington: U.S. Government Printing Office, 1960), document 1383, pp. 1491–1495 (U.S. government work, public domain); adapted)
Glossary: the Three Powers = the Soviet Union, the United States, and Britain; Polish Provisional Government = the temporary government of Poland, set up in 1945 to rule until elections were held; unfettered = free from limits; universal suffrage = the right of all adult citizens to vote; Allied Press = newspaper reporters from the Allied countries; Roumania = Romania; elements thereof = parts of those populations; the Control Council in Hungary = the Allied commission, chaired by the Soviet Union, that oversaw defeated Hungary

**E9-E01** [Topic 9.1 · LO A · Skill 4.B · medium] The agreements in the passage are best understood in the context of which of the following?
- (A) the Soviet blockade of West Berlin and the Western airlift that followed
- (B) debates over independence movements in the overseas empires of Britain and France
- (C) the victorious Allies' efforts to settle postwar Europe after Germany's defeat
- (D) the creation of the European Coal and Steel Community
KEY: C
Explanation: Germany surrendered in May 1945. At Potsdam, Stalin, Truman, and Attlee tried to settle the postwar order. Soviet armies already held most of Eastern Europe. The Western leaders asked for promises of free elections and a free press there, but they had little power to enforce them.
Rationales: A: Incorrect. The Berlin blockade began in 1948, three years after this agreement. | B: Incorrect. The passage is about Poland, Germany, and other states in Europe, not about colonies overseas. | C: Correct. In July and August 1945, the leaders of the Soviet Union, the United States, and Britain met at Potsdam, near Berlin, to decide the future of Germany and Eastern Europe. | D: Incorrect. Six Western European states founded the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951. It was not part of the Potsdam agreements.


**E9-E02** [Topic 9.3 · LO C · Skill 3.D · medium] Which of the following later developments most clearly shows that the hopes for free elections and a free press expressed in the passage were not met?
- (A) Communist parties took over Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, and Poland by 1949.
- (B) Finland kept a democratic government after the war.
- (C) Austria regained full independence as a neutral state in 1955.
- (D) Greece's government defeated communist rebels in a civil war that ended in 1949.
KEY: A
Explanation: From 1945 to 1949, communist parties backed by the Soviet Union took power in Poland, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria. They used rigged elections, arrests, censorship, and threats. The promises made at Yalta and Potsdam were not kept.
Rationales: A: Correct. Poland's communists won rigged elections in 1947. Bulgaria's communists executed the opposition leader Nikola Petkov that year. Romania's king was forced to give up his throne that December. Hungary's communists pushed out their rivals by 1949. | B: Incorrect. Finland is named in the passage, but it stayed a democracy. That shows a hope that was met, not one that failed. | C: Incorrect. Austria regained full independence in 1955 and stayed a democracy. It is not one of the countries named in the passage. | D: Incorrect. Greece is not named in the passage, and its government defeated the communist rebels. Greece stayed out of the Soviet bloc.


**E9-E03** [Topic 9.5 · LO E · Skill 1.A · medium] The "transfer to Germany of German populations" described in the passage best illustrates which postwar development?
- (A) ethnic cleansing after 1945
- (B) the return of Holocaust survivors
- (C) guest-worker recruitment
- (D) the flight of East Germans before 1961
KEY: A
Explanation: The passage asks Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary to "suspend further expulsions," which shows that Germans were already being driven out. The Allies said the transfers should be "orderly and humane." In fact, many expulsions were brutal, and many people died. It was one of the largest forced migrations in European history.
Rationales: A: Correct. Between 1945 and 1950, about 12 million Germans fled or were driven out of Poland, Czechoslovakia, and other states. Many died on the way. Driving out a people because of its nationality is called ethnic cleansing. | B: Incorrect. The passage describes Germans being moved out of Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary, not Jewish survivors going home. Many survivors who tried to return home met hostility, and many left Eastern Europe. | C: Incorrect. Guest workers came to West Germany in the 1950s and 1960s to fill jobs. The passage describes Germans being forced out in 1945. | D: Incorrect. Millions of East Germans moved west before the Berlin Wall went up in 1961. The passage describes something else: Germans driven out of Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary.
