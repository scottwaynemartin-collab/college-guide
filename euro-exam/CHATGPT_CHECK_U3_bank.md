### Source P1 (questions E3-P01–E3-P03)
> When the Parisians lost sight of their Broussel* they were like madmen, shouting through the streets that they were lost, that they would have their protector restored to them, that they would die, every one of them, cheerfully in his behalf. They assembled, they stretched chains across the streets, and in a few hours they erected barricades in every quarter of the town. …
> The Coadjutor of Paris [Jean-François-Paul de Gondi, afterwards Cardinal de Retz], who, from inordinate ambition, had inclinations that were far from wishing to endeavour to remedy these evils, was also sent. … he went out on foot with his hood and rochet; and mingling with the crowd he reproved the people, shouted peace to them, and pointed out the obedience they owed to the king … .
> The populace replied to what he said to them with respect for his person, but with audacity and anger against the idea of what they owed to the king. They demanded their protector, with protestations that they would never be pacified until he was returned to them; and without considering the respect they owed to the Grand-Master, the Maréchal de La Meilleraye, they flung stones at him, overwhelmed him with insults, and, in threatening him, uttered horrible imprecations against the queen and her minister. Against the latter they launched such insolence as deserved the gibbet if the king had been master … .
> … Kings flatter themselves readily; our regent did so, and being born with intrepid courage, she ridiculed the emotions of the populace and could not believe they would ever do her any serious harm.
> * Pierre Broussel, a judge in the Parlement of Paris, France's most important royal law court. He had been arrested that morning on the queen's orders.
— Madame de Motteville, a close companion and attendant of Queen Anne of Austria, the mother of King Louis XIV, memoirs describing Paris on August 26, 1648, first published in 1723 (Translated by Katharine Prescott Wormeley, Memoirs of Madame de Motteville on Anne of Austria and Her Court, vol. 1 (Boston, 1902), pp. 317–319; adapted)
> Words to know: barricades = barriers built across streets to block soldiers; coadjutor = a church official who helps an archbishop and will take his place; inordinate = too great; rochet = a white robe worn by church officials; populace = the common people; audacity = boldness; Grand-Master … Maréchal de La Meilleraye = a high-ranking royal army commander whom the queen had sent to calm the crowd; imprecations = curses; her minister = Cardinal Mazarin, the queen's chief minister; gibbet = a gallows used for hanging; regent = a person who rules in place of the monarch; here, Queen Anne, Louis XIV's mother; intrepid = fearless

**E3-P01** [Topic 3.1 · LO A · Skill 4.B · medium]  The events Motteville describes are best understood in the context of which of the following?

KEY: C. Explanation: In 1648 Louis XIV was nine years old. His mother ruled for him with Cardinal Mazarin, and they wanted new taxes to pay for war. Judges of the Parlement of Paris fought the taxes. When the queen had Broussel arrested, Paris rose up. This began the revolts called the Fronde (1648–1653).
  - why not A: Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, almost forty years after these riots of 1648.
  - why not B: The French Revolution began in 1789, more than a century later. This crowd is in Paris and is defending a judge, not attacking feudal dues.
  - why not D: France's war with the Dutch Republic began in 1672. In 1648 the crowd wanted the queen to free Broussel.
  - hint: Who was ruling France in 1648, and why might a judge of the Parlement have angered the queen and her minister?

### Source P2 (questions E3-P04–E3-P06)
> The state of monarchy is the supremest thing upon earth; for kings are not only God's lieutenants upon earth, and sit upon God's throne, but even by God himself they are called gods. …
> Kings are justly called gods, for that they exercise a manner or resemblance of divine power upon earth; for if you will consider the attributes to God, you shall see how they agree in the person of a king. God hath power to create or destroy, make or unmake at his pleasure, to give life or send death, to judge all and to be judged nor accountable to none, to raise low things and to make high things low at his pleasure, and to God are both soul and body due. And the like power have kings: they make and unmake their subjects, they have power of raising and casting down, of life and of death, judges over all their subjects and in all causes and yet accountable to none but God only. …
> I would wish you to be careful to avoid three things in the matter of grievances:
> First, that you do not meddle with the main points of government; that is my craft … to meddle with that were to lessen me. … I must not be taught my office.
> Secondly, I would not have you meddle with such ancient rights of mine as I have received from my predecessors … . All novelties are dangerous as well in a politic as in a natural body, and therefore I would be loath to be quarreled in my ancient rights and possessions; for that were to judge me unworthy of that which my predecessors had and left me. …
— James I, king of England and Scotland, speech to both houses of Parliament, March 21, 1610 (Text from The Workes of the Most High and Mightie Prince, James (London, 1616), as printed in James Harvey Robinson, Readings in European History, vol. 2 (Boston, 1906), pp. 219–220; adapted)
> Words to know: lieutenants = officers who act for a higher authority; attributes = qualities; accountable = required to explain one's actions; grievances = complaints; meddle with = interfere in; craft = skilled job; predecessors = the rulers who came before; novelties = new things; politic … body = the state, compared to a human body; loath = unwilling; quarreled in = challenged in

### Source P3 (questions E3-P07–E3-P09)
> Having by our late labours and hazards made it appear to the world at how high a rate we value our just freedom, and God having so far owned our cause as to deliver the enemies thereof into our hands, we do now hold ourselves bound in mutual duty to each other to take the best care we can for the future to avoid both the danger of returning into a slavish condition and the chargeable remedy of another war … . In order whereunto we declare:—
> [I.] That the people of England, being at this day very unequally distributed by Counties, Cities, and Boroughs for the election of their deputies in Parliament, ought to be more indifferently proportioned according to the number of the inhabitants … .
> III. That the people do, of course, choose themselves a Parliament once in two years … .
> IV. That the power of this, and all future Representatives of this Nation, is inferior only to theirs who choose them, and doth extend, without the consent or concurrence of any other person or persons, to the enacting, altering, and repealing of laws, to the erecting and abolishing of offices and courts, to the appointing, removing, and calling to account magistrates and officers of all degrees, to the making war and peace, to the treating with foreign States, and, generally, to whatsoever is not expressly or impliedly reserved by the represented to themselves: Which are as followeth.
> 1. That matters of religion and the ways of God's worship are not at all entrusted by us to any human power … .
> 4. That in all laws made or to be made every person may be bound alike, and that no tenure, estate, charter, degree, birth, or place do confer any exemption from the ordinary course of legal proceedings whereunto others are subjected. …
> These things we declare to be our native rights, and therefore are agreed and resolved to maintain them with our utmost possibilities against all opposition whatsoever … .
— An Agreement of the People, a plan for a new government presented to the General Council of Parliament's New Model Army, October 28, 1647 (Text from Samuel Rawson Gardiner, ed., The Constitutional Documents of the Puritan Revolution, 1625–1660, 3rd ed. (Oxford, 1906), pp. 333–335; adapted)
> Words to know: late labours and hazards = recent efforts and dangers, meaning the civil war; owned our cause = supported our side; chargeable = costly; boroughs = towns that elected members of Parliament; indifferently proportioned = fairly divided; Representatives = here, Parliaments; inferior only to theirs who choose them = lower only than the power of the voters; concurrence = agreement; magistrates = officials who enforce the law; impliedly reserved = kept back without being stated; tenure, estate, charter, degree = kinds of land holding, property, special legal grants, and rank; exemption = freedom from a rule others must follow

  - why not C: James II ruled from 1685 to 1688, long after 1647.
  - why not D: James I died in 1625. By 1647 his son, Charles I, had lost a civil war.

**E3-P08** [Topic 3.2 · LO B · Skill 1.B · medium]  Members of which group would most likely have opposed the Agreement of the People?

### Source P5 (questions E3-P13–E3-P15)
> Whereas the late king James the Second by the assistance of divers evil counsellors, judges and ministers employed by him did endeavour to subvert and extirpate the Protestant religion and the laws and liberties of this kingdom.
> By assuming and exercising a power of dispensing with and suspending of laws, and the execution of laws, without consent of parliament. …
> By levying money for and to the use of the crown, by pretence of prerogative, for other time and in other manner than the same was granted by parliament.
> By raising and keeping a standing army within this kingdom in time of peace, without consent of parliament, and quartering of soldiers contrary to law. …
> … do in the first place (as their ancestors in like case have usually done) for the vindicating and asserting their ancient rights and liberties, declare:
> That the pretended power of suspending of laws or the execution of laws by regal authority without consent of parliament is illegal. …
> That the levying money for or to the use of the crown by pretence of prerogative without grant of parliament for a longer time or in other manner than the same is or shall be granted is illegal. …
> That the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace unless it be with consent of parliament is against law. …
> That election of members of parliament ought to be free.
> That the freedom of speech and debates or proceedings in parliament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of parliament. …
> And that for redress of all grievances and for the amending, strengthening and preserving of the laws parliaments ought to be held frequently.
— The Bill of Rights, act passed by the English Parliament in December 1689; it repeated the Declaration of Right that Parliament had presented to William and Mary in February 1689 (Text from George Burton Adams and H. Morse Stephens, eds., Select Documents of English Constitutional History (New York: Macmillan, 1901), pp. 462–465; adapted)
> Words to know: divers = various; subvert and extirpate = undermine and wipe out; dispensing with and suspending of laws = excusing people from laws, or stopping laws from being enforced; levying = collecting taxes; pretence of prerogative = a false claim of special royal power; standing army = a permanent army kept in peacetime; quartering = housing soldiers in private homes; vindicating = defending; regal = royal; impeached or questioned = challenged; redress of all grievances = fixing all complaints

**E3-P15** [Topic 3.2 · LO B · Skill 1.A · easy]  The Bill of Rights best reflects which form of government?

- (B) Republican commonwealth
- (C) Enlightened despotism

**E3-E15** [Topic 3.6 · LO E · Skill 3.A · medium]  Which of the following best describes Queen Anne's main argument in the speech?
- (A) Britain should keep fighting until France gives up its colonies in North America.
- (B) The peace would keep France and Spain apart and win trade advantages for Britain.
- (C) The peace would place the Austrian Habsburg claimant on the throne of Spain.
- (D) Parliament alone should decide whether Britain makes war or makes peace.
KEY: B. Explanation: Anne says the peace keeps France and Spain apart, which was "the chief Inducement to begin this War." She says it creates "a real Balance of Power." She also lists new lands and trading rights for Britain.
  - A: Incorrect. Anne is announcing terms of peace, not calling for more war. Under these terms France kept Quebec and the rest of Canada, which it did not lose until 1763.
  - B: Correct. Anne says France and Spain will be "more effectually divided than ever" and lists gains such as Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, and the Asiento.
  - C: Incorrect. Anne accepts the Duke of Anjou as king of Spain, as long as he gives up all claim to the crown of France. The Habsburg claimant, Archduke Charles, did not get Spain. By late 1711 he had become Holy Roman Emperor himself.
  - D: Incorrect. Anne says making peace and war is "the undoubted Prerogative of the Crown." She informs Parliament but does not hand it the decision.

**E3-E16** [Topic 3.4 · LO C · Skill 5.B · Causation · medium]  The agreement described in the last paragraph most directly contributed to which of the following?

**E3-E17** [Topic 3.2 · LO B · Skill 4.B · medium]  Anne's concern for "the Protestant Succession … in the House of Hanover" is best understood in the context of which of the following?

**Source 1**
> The States bordering upon Poland have so often been involved in the disorders which have arisen during interregnums in that kingdom, that the experience of the past would in any case have led the neighboring powers to occupy themselves seriously with the affairs of that State the moment that the throne became vacant by the death of King Augustus III. …

**Source 2**
> … this unfortunate partition of Poland, which is costing me ten years of my life. … How many times have I refused to agree to it! > Words to know: partition = a division of a country's land among other states; pestilence = a deadly disease that spreads widely; accede to = agree to; imbitters = old spelling of embitters, makes bitter

**E3-E19** [Topic 3.6 · LO E · Skill 4.B · medium]  The "disorders" in Poland described in Source 1 are best understood in the context of which of the following?

### Source X8 (questions E3-E22–E3-E25)
> The great Reformer's predecessors were fully conscious of this condition of things, and did what they could to remedy it. … Their programme included the reorganisation of the armed forces, and, as an inevitable consequence, the improvement of the finances,—the development of the economic resources of the country,—and the encouragement of foreign commerce. They admitted the necessity of more direct intercourse and co-operation with foreign countries. …
> Then we come to Peter. What other, what new thing did he do? Nothing, or, at most, very little indeed. The programme above referred to was his programme; he enlarged it a little, added the Reform of the national customs; he modified the nature of the intercourse already in existence with the Western world; but he, too, left the foundations of the political edifice he had inherited, intact … . It was begun before his time,—what he did was to change the conditions under which it was to be carried on in future. The new factor was, in the first place, the endless war, which, for twenty long years, was to inspire, direct and drive him forward … . In the second place, we have the tastes, habits of mind, manias and fancies, which the gifted but whimsical ruler owed to his education, … and his intercourse with Europe. … Finally, the Reformer's personal temperament, which endued all his measures with qualities of violence, excess and hastiness, both painful and disconcerting to every one they affected, must be taken into account. What had been a peaceful evolution became, in consequence of these peculiarities, a revolution. …
> … Peter, in a few years, had performed the work of several centuries. It may be doubted whether this sudden bound across time and space was an unmixed benefit. …
> … The war made military reforms a first necessity, and these called forth financial measures, which, in their turn, made economic enterprises indispensable.
— Kazimierz Waliszewski, Polish-born historian who wrote in French, Peter the Great, 1897 (Translated by Lady Mary Loyd (New York: D. Appleton, 1897), pp. 395–397; adapted)
> Words to know: predecessors = the rulers who came before him; programme = British spelling of program, a plan; intercourse = dealings; contact; edifice = building; structure; whimsical = acting on sudden ideas; endued = filled; disconcerting = upsetting; an unmixed benefit = something wholly good; indispensable = absolutely needed

**E3-E22** [Topic 3.7 · LO G · Skill 3.A · medium]  Which of the following best describes Waliszewski's main argument?

- (D) Peter's reforms were driven mainly by the demands of Russia's merchants and towns.
KEY: C. Explanation: Waliszewski says earlier tsars had already planned to rebuild the army, the money system, and trade. Peter's long wars and fierce temper made those plans move fast and with force.
  - A: Incorrect. Waliszewski asks what new thing Peter did and answers, "Nothing, or, at most, very little indeed."
  - B: Incorrect. Waliszewski says Peter's measures were marked by "violence, excess and hastiness."
  - C: Correct. Waliszewski says of the earlier tsars' plan, "The programme above referred to was his programme." He says war and Peter's "violence, excess and hastiness" turned "a peaceful evolution" into "a revolution."
  - D: Incorrect. Waliszewski names war, Peter's own tastes, and his temper as the forces behind the reforms. He does not mention merchants' demands.

**E3-E23** [Topic 3.7 · LO G · Skill 3.D · hard]  Which of the following, if true, would best support Waliszewski's claim that "the war made military reforms a first necessity, and these called forth financial measures"?
- (A) Peter created a new tax on each male peasant to pay for his growing army.
- (B) Peter ordered nobles at court to shave their beards and wear Western clothes.
- (C) Peter replaced the head of the Russian Church with a council under state control.
- (D) Peter founded an Academy of Sciences to bring Western learning to Russia.
KEY: A. Explanation: Waliszewski says war came first. A bigger army needed money, and that led to new taxes. Peter's tax on male peasants, collected from the 1720s, was created to pay for his army.
  - A: Correct. A tax created to pay for the army shows a military need leading to a financial measure, just as the claim says. Peter's poll tax of the 1720s did this.
  - B: Incorrect. Rules about beards and dress reflect what Waliszewski calls Peter's "tastes" and "fancies," not the needs of war.
  - C: Incorrect. Replacing the patriarch with the Holy Synod increased Peter's control of the Church. It was not mainly a way to pay for war.
  - D: Incorrect. The Academy of Sciences, which Peter planned and which opened in 1725, aimed at learning. It does not show war leading to financial measures.

- (D) Johan de Witt of the Dutch Republic

  - A: Incorrect. William III also built up England's army, but he ruled within the limits of the Bill of Rights. He needed Parliament's consent for taxes and a peacetime army.
  - B: Correct. Like Peter, Louis XIV built a large standing army, expanded the central government, and brought the church and the nobles under royal control, while keeping final power in his own hands.
  - C: Incorrect. Stanisław II was an elected king who could not control Poland's nobles, and his country was partitioned.
  - D: Incorrect. De Witt led the Dutch Republic, a merchant republic without a king, from 1653 to 1672.
