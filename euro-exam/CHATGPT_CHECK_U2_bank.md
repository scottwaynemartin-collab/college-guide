### Source P1 (questions E2-P01–E2-P03)
> You may obtain letters of safe conduct from the vicar of our Lord Jesus Christ, by means of which you are able to liberate your soul from the hands of the enemy, and convey it by means of contrition and confession, safe and secure from all pains of Purgatory, into the happy kingdom. For know that in these letters are stamped and engraven all the merits of Christ's passion there laid bare. Consider, that for each and every mortal sin it is necessary to undergo seven years of penitence after confession and contrition, either in this life or in Purgatory.
> How many mortal sins are committed in a day, how many in a week, how many in a month, how many in a year, how many in the whole course of life! They are well-nigh numberless, and those that commit them must needs suffer endless punishment in the burning pains of Purgatory.
> But with these confessional letters you will be able at any time in life to obtain full indulgence for all penalties imposed upon you, in all cases except the four reserved to the Apostolic See. …
> Do you not know that when it is necessary for anyone to go to Rome, or undertake any other dangerous journey, he takes his money to a broker and gives a certain per cent—five or six or ten—in order that at Rome or elsewhere he may receive again his funds intact, by means of the letter of this same broker? Are you not willing, then, for the fourth part of a florin, to obtain these letters, by virtue of which you may bring, not your money, but your divine and immortal soul safe and sound into the land of Paradise?
— Johann Tetzel, German Dominican friar who preached an indulgence for Archbishop Albert of Mainz, sermon to parish priests, c. 1517 (Translated in Translations and Reprints from the Original Sources of European History, vol. 2, no. 6 (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania, 1895), pp. 9–10; adapted)
> Words to know: letters of safe conduct = papers promising safe passage; here, letters of indulgence; vicar of our Lord Jesus Christ = the pope; contrition = sorrow for sin; Purgatory = in Catholic teaching, a state after death in which souls are cleansed of sin before entering heaven; Christ's passion = the suffering and death of Jesus; mortal sin = in Catholic teaching, a very serious sin; penitence = punishment or acts done to make up for sin; well-nigh = almost; indulgence = a release from the punishment owed for sins, granted by the Church; Apostolic See = the papacy; broker = a banker who sends money from one place to another; florin = a gold coin

**E2-P01** [Topic 2.2 · LO B · Skill 3.A · easy]  Which of the following best describes the main argument of the sermon?
- (A) Buying an indulgence letter is a small price to pay to escape the pains of Purgatory.
- (B) Prayer and good works, not payments, are the way to escape Purgatory.
- (C) Christians must travel to Rome to confess their sins to the pope himself.
- (D) Parish priests should stop charging fees and give the sacraments to their people free.
KEY: A. Explanation: Tetzel says that letters bought for a small coin bring "full indulgence for all penalties." He uses fear of the "burning pains" of Purgatory to get people to buy.
  - why not B: Tetzel urges listeners to buy letters "for the fourth part of a florin." He says the letters bring "full indulgence for all penalties."
  - why not C: Tetzel mentions a trip to Rome only as a comparison with a banker's letter. He says the letters work "at any time in life," wherever a person is.
  - why not D: Tetzel asks priests to help sell the letters. He is not asking them to stop charging money.
  - hint: Look at what Tetzel asks people to buy and what he promises it will do for them.

**E2-P02** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 4.B · medium]  The sermon is best understood in the context of which of the following?
- (A) Charles V's need for money to defend Hungary and Vienna against the Ottoman Turks
- (B) the Council of Trent's need for money to found new seminaries for training young priests
- (C) the papacy's need for money for projects such as rebuilding St. Peter's Basilica in Rome
- (D) Francis I's need for money to pay for the wars of the French kings in Italy
KEY: C. Explanation: In 1515 Pope Leo X offered an indulgence to raise money for rebuilding St. Peter's Basilica. Archbishop Albert of Mainz shared in the money to pay off his own debts, and Tetzel preached the indulgence in Germany.
  - why not A: Charles V was not elected emperor until 1519, and his wars against the Ottomans in Hungary came later. Tetzel preached a papal indulgence.
  - why not B: The Council of Trent first met in 1545, almost thirty years after this sermon. In 1562 it ended the use of special preachers to collect money for indulgences.
  - why not D: King Francis I of France did fight in Italy in 1515. But Tetzel preached an indulgence granted by the pope, in Germany, and the money did not go to the French king.
  - hint: Think about who granted indulgences and why the Church wanted to raise money in the 1510s.

**E2-P03** [Topic 2.2 · LO B · Skill 5.B · Causation · easy]  Sermons like this one most directly provoked which of the following?
- (A) The Diet of Worms
- (B) The Council of Trent's reform decrees
- (C) The German Peasants' War
- (D) Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses
KEY: D. Explanation: In October 1517 Martin Luther wrote the Ninety-Five Theses. In them he attacked the way preachers like Tetzel sold indulgences. Printed copies spread across Germany within weeks.
  - why not A: The Diet of Worms (1521) was the assembly of the empire where Luther refused to take back his writings. It dealt with Luther, not directly with Tetzel's sermons.
  - why not B: The Council of Trent met from 1545 to 1563. It was the Catholic Church's answer to the whole Protestant Reformation, decades after this sermon.
  - why not C: The Peasants' War of 1524–1525 grew mainly out of peasants' complaints against their lords, not directly out of indulgence preaching.
  - hint: Which of these events was a written challenge to the way indulgences were sold?


### Source P2 (questions E2-P04–E2-P06)
> A Christian man is the most free lord of all, and subject to none; a Christian man is the most dutiful servant of all, and subject to every one. …
> And so it will profit nothing that the body should be adorned with sacred vestments, or dwell in holy places, or be occupied in sacred offices, or pray, fast, and abstain from certain meats, or do whatever works can be done through the body and in the body. … One thing, and one alone, is necessary for life, justification, and Christian liberty; and that is the most holy word of God, the Gospel of Christ … For the word of God cannot be received and honoured by any works, but by faith alone. Hence it is clear that, as the soul needs the word alone for life and justification, so it is justified by faith alone and not by any works. …
> Not that in the sense of corporeal power any one among Christians has been appointed to possess and rule all things, according to the mad and senseless idea of certain ecclesiastics. That is the office of kings, princes, and men upon earth. …
> Nor are we only kings and the freest of all men, but also priests for ever, a dignity far higher than kingship, because by that priesthood we are worthy to appear before God, to pray for others, and to teach one another mutually the things which are of God. …
— Martin Luther, German monk and professor of theology, Concerning Christian Liberty, a short book that he sent to Pope Leo X, 1520 (Translated in Henry Wace and C. A. Buchheim, First Principles of the Reformation (London: John Murray, 1883), pp. 104–107, 114–115; adapted)
> Words to know: dutiful = obedient; vestments = special robes worn by priests; sacred offices = church duties; abstain from certain meats = avoid certain foods, such as meat on fast days; justification = being made right with God; corporeal = bodily; physical; ecclesiastics = members of the clergy; mutually = with each other

**E2-P04** [Topic 2.2 · LO B · Skill 3.A · easy]  Which of the following best describes Luther's main argument in the passage?
- (A) People are made right with God by faith alone, not by outward religious works.
- (B) Christians must obey their priests in order to be saved.
- (C) Fasting and prayer earn the forgiveness of sins if they are done sincerely.
- (D) Christians are free from the rule of kings and princes in worldly matters.
KEY: A. Explanation: Luther writes that the soul "is justified by faith alone and not by any works." For him, Christian freedom is an inner, spiritual freedom.
  - why not B: Luther says all believers are "priests for ever" who can pray for and teach one another. He does not make salvation depend on obeying priests.
  - why not C: Luther says it will "profit nothing" to "pray, fast, and abstain from certain meats" as a way of being justified.
  - why not D: Luther says Christian freedom is not "corporeal power." Ruling is "the office of kings, princes, and men upon earth."
  - hint: Find the sentence that begins "Hence it is clear." What does Luther say the soul needs?

**E2-P05** [Topic 2.2 · LO B · Skill 1.B · medium]  Luther's claim that believers are "priests for ever" most directly challenged which feature of the Church of his time?
- (A) Its teaching that kings and princes received their power to rule from God
- (B) Its use of sermons and teaching to explain the Christian faith to the people
- (C) Its call for Christians to pray to God for the needs of other people
- (D) Its belief that clergy had a higher spiritual rank than other Christians
KEY: D. Explanation: This idea is called the priesthood of all believers. Luther says every Christian is a priest who may "appear before God," pray for others, and teach. This challenged the special spiritual rank of the clergy, although Luther still wanted pastors to preach and lead worship.
  - why not A: Luther does not challenge rulers' power. He says ruling is "the office of kings, princes, and men upon earth."
  - why not B: Luther wanted more teaching, not less. He says believers should "teach one another mutually the things which are of God."
  - why not C: Luther kept this practice. He says believers are worthy "to pray for others."
  - hint: Think about what set priests apart from other Christians in the Church of 1520.

**E2-P06** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 4.B · medium]  Luther's reliance on "the most holy word of God" is best understood in the context of which of the following?
- (A) the Council of Trent's ruling that the Latin Vulgate was the official Bible of the Catholic Church
- (B) Renaissance popes' use of art and grand buildings to display the Church's power
- (C) Christian rulers' efforts to keep religious unity by enforcing the laws of the Church
- (D) Christian humanists' efforts to study the earliest Christian texts
KEY: D. Explanation: Christian humanists such as Erasmus wanted scholars to study the oldest Christian writings. In 1516 Erasmus printed the New Testament in Greek. Luther used it when he translated the New Testament into German in 1522.
  - why not A: The Council of Trent made the Vulgate the Church's official Bible in 1546. That was twenty-six years after this book, and partly a reply to Protestants like Luther.
  - why not B: Popes such as Leo X paid for grand art and buildings in Rome. That does not explain why Luther turns to the word of God in Scripture.
  - why not C: Rulers punished heretics to keep their lands united in one faith. That explains how rulers treated reformers, not why Luther relies on Scripture.
  - hint: Which movement, active just before 1520, urged scholars to study the oldest Christian texts?

### Source P4 (questions E2-P10–E2-P12)
> 1. We, Charles V, by God's grace Roman emperor elect, ever august, king of Germany, Spain, the two Sicilies, Jerusalem, Hungary, Dalmatia, Croatia, etc., archduke of Austria, duke of Burgundy, etc., count of Hapsburg, Flanders, and Tyrol, … salute and tender our gracious good wishes to each and all of the electors, princes, … and all other beloved and faithful subjects of ours, or of the Empire. …
> 3. Therefore we hold that if it was the duty of any of our ancestors to defend the Christian name, much greater is the obligation on us, inasmuch as the unparalleled goodness of Almighty God has, for the protection and increase of his holy faith, endowed us with more kingdoms and lands and greater power in the Empire than any of our ancestors for many years. …
> 9. And although, after the delivery of the papal bull and final condemnation of Luther, we proclaimed the bull in many places in the German nation, … nevertheless Martin Luther has taken no account of it, nor lessened nor revoked his errors, … but like a madman plotting the manifest destruction of the holy Church, he daily scatters abroad much worse fruit and effect of his depraved heart and mind through very numerous books, both in Latin and German, composed by himself, or at least under his name, which are full of heresies and blasphemies. …
> 29. Consequently we command you, each and all, … that henceforth no one shall dare to buy, sell, read, preserve, copy, print, or cause to be copied or printed, any books of the aforesaid Martin Luther, condemned by our holy father the pope as aforesaid, or any other writings in German or Latin hitherto composed by him, since they are foul, harmful, suspected, and published by a notorious and stiffnecked heretic. …
— Edict of Worms, decree of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V issued after the Imperial Diet (assembly of the princes and cities of the empire) at Worms, May 1521 (English translation in James Harvey Robinson, Readings in European History, vol. 2 (Boston: Ginn, 1906), pp. 83–88; adapted)
> Words to know: emperor elect = emperor chosen by the electors, the princes who voted for the emperor; ever august = always majestic, a title of the emperors; salute and tender our gracious good wishes = send greetings; inasmuch as = because; unparalleled = never equaled; endowed = provided; papal bull = an official order from the pope; revoked = taken back; depraved = wicked; heresies = beliefs that go against official church teaching; blasphemies = statements that insult God or holy things; stiffnecked = stubborn

**E2-P10** [Topic 2.2 · LO B · Skill 2.A · easy]  Which of the following best describes the main purpose of the edict?
- (A) to call a church council that would judge Luther's ideas fairly
- (B) to let each German prince decide which religion his lands would follow
- (C) to brand Luther a heretic and forbid the reading of his books
- (D) to reform the sale of indulgences that Luther had criticized
KEY: C. Explanation: After Luther refused to take back his writings at Worms in April 1521, Charles V declared him an outlaw. The edict forbade anyone to buy, read, copy, or print his books.

  - why not B: The edict orders "each and all" to obey its ban on Luther's books. It gives princes no choice about religion.
  - why not D: The edict defends the Church against Luther. It says nothing about changing how indulgences were sold.

  - why not D: The Edict of Restitution (1629) ordered Protestants to give back church lands they had taken. It tried to undo Protestant gains, not protect them.
  - hint: Which agreement ended the religious fighting in Germany in the 1550s?

### Source P6 (questions E2-P16–E2-P18)
