### Source P1 (questions E5-P01–E5-P03)
> … Mistress now of North America, lording it in India, through the company whose territorial conquests had been ratified by native princes, over twenty millions of inhabitants,—a population larger than that of Great Britain and having a revenue respectable alongside of that of the home government,—England, with yet other rich possessions scattered far and wide over the globe, had ever before her eyes, as a salutary lesson, the severe chastisement which the weakness of Spain had allowed her to inflict upon that huge disjointed empire. …
> "For the first time since the Middle Ages," says M. Martin,* speaking of the Seven Years' War, "England had conquered France single-handed almost without allies, France having powerful auxiliaries. She had conquered solely by the superiority of her government."
> Yes! but by the superiority of her government using the tremendous weapon of her sea power. This made her rich, and in turn protected the trade by which she had her wealth. With her money she upheld her few auxiliaries, mainly Prussia and Hanover, in their desperate strife. Her power was everywhere that her ships could reach, and there was none to dispute the sea to her. …
> The one nation that gained in this war** was that which used the sea in peace to earn its wealth, and ruled it in war by the extent of its navy, by the number of its subjects who lived on the sea or by the sea, and by its numerous bases of operations scattered over the globe. …
> * Henri Martin, a French historian of the 1800s.
> ** The Seven Years' War, 1756–1763.
— Alfred Thayer Mahan, United States naval officer and historian, The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783, 1890 (Text from the first edition (Boston: Little, Brown, 1890), pp. 326–329; adapted, with one misprint corrected as in later printings)

**E5-P01** [Topic 5.2 · LO B · Skill 3.A]  Mahan's main argument in the passage is that Britain
- (A) won the Seven Years' War mainly because its navy and sea trade gave it wealth and reach
- (B) won the Seven Years' War mainly because its army was larger than the armies of its enemies
- (C) won the Seven Years' War by building a large alliance of European powers against France
- (D) lost much of its overseas trade during the Seven Years' War to stronger French fleets
KEY: A. Explanation: Mahan says the one real winner used the sea to grow rich in peace and ruled the sea in war. Trade paid for the war, and the navy protected the trade.
  - why not B: Mahan credits sea power, not the size of Britain's army. Britain's army was small compared with those of France and Austria.
  - why not C: Mahan quotes a historian who says Britain won "almost without allies." Its main helpers were Prussia and Hanover.
  - why not D: Mahan says the opposite: no one could "dispute the sea" with Britain.
  - hint: Mahan answers the French historian with "Yes! but …" What does he add after "but"?

**E5-P02** [Topic 5.3 · LO C · Skill 2.C]  Which limitation should a historian consider when using this passage as a complete explanation of Britain's victory?
- (A) Mahan quotes a French historian, so his account mainly reflects how the French saw the war.
- (B) Mahan wrote over a century after the war, when few written records from the 1750s and 1760s survived.
- (C) Mahan's naval background may have led him to stress sea power more than other military or diplomatic factors.
- (D) Mahan relies on claims made by Prussia and Hanover, which wanted credit for the victory.
KEY: C. Explanation: Mahan's career as a naval officer may help explain why he puts sea power first. To judge why Britain won, a historian should also look at evidence about land war, money, and Britain's allies, such as Prussia.

  - why not B: Many records of the war survived. Historians in 1890 could use printed letters, reports, and earlier histories, and Mahan even quotes Henri Martin's history.
  - why not D: Mahan does not rely on Prussia or Hanover. He mentions them only as Britain's helpers.
  - hint: Which choice describes something that is actually true of Mahan or of the passage, and that would also leave the explanation incomplete?

**E5-P03** [Topic 5.1 · LO A · Skill 4.B]  The developments Mahan describes are best understood in the context of

### Source P2 (questions E5-P04–E5-P06)
> Cahier of the grievances, complaints, and protests of the electoral district of Carcassonne, drawn up by the commissioners named by the general assembly of the third estate …
> The third estate of the electoral district of Carcassonne, desiring to give to a beloved monarch, and one so worthy of our affection, the most unmistakable proof of its love and respect, of its gratitude and fidelity … declares that the happiness of the nation must, in their opinion, depend upon that of its king, upon the stability of the monarchy, and upon the preservation of the orders which compose it and of the fundamental laws which govern it …
> 1. Public worship should be confined to the Roman Catholic apostolic religion, to the exclusion of all other forms of worship …
> 8. … the nation should hereafter be subject only to such laws and taxes as it shall itself freely ratify.
> 9. The meetings of the Estates General of the kingdom should be fixed for definite periods …
> 10. In order to assure to the third estate the influence to which it is entitled in view of the number of its members, the amount of its contributions to the public treasury, and the manifold interests which it has to defend or promote in the national assemblies, its votes in the assembly should be taken and counted by head.
> 11. No order, corporation, or individual citizen may lay claim to any pecuniary exemptions. … All taxes should be assessed on the same system throughout the nation.
> 12. The due exacted from commoners holding fiefs should be abolished, and also the general or particular regulations which exclude members of the third estate from certain positions, offices, and ranks which have hitherto been bestowed on nobles either for life or hereditarily. A law should be passed declaring members of the third estate qualified to fill all such offices for which they are judged to be personally fitted.
— Cahier (list of grievances) of the Third Estate of the district of Carcassonne, in southern France, written in the spring of 1789 for the meeting of the Estates-General (English translation in James Harvey Robinson and Charles A. Beard, Readings in Modern European History, vol. 1 (Boston, 1908); adapted)
Words to know: grievances = complaints about unfair treatment; third estate = everyone in France who was not a member of the clergy or the nobility; fidelity = loyalty; orders = the three estates: clergy, nobility, and commoners; ratify = approve; counted by head = counted one vote per deputy; corporation = an organized group, such as a town or guild; pecuniary exemptions = freedom from paying money, such as taxes; due exacted from commoners holding fiefs = a fee charged to commoners who owned land that came with feudal rights; hereditarily = passed down from parent to child

**E5-P04** [Topic 5.4 · LO D · Skill 3.A]  Which of the following best describes the demands in the cahier?
- (A) The Third Estate wants no tax exemptions and more say in government, under the king.
- (B) The Third Estate wants the Estates-General to replace the king as the maker of France's laws.
- (C) The Third Estate wants the Church's lands sold to pay off the king's debts.
- (D) The Third Estate wants the nobles to keep their offices in return for paying higher taxes.
KEY: A. Explanation: The cahier asks that no group be free from taxes, that votes be counted by head, and that offices be open to talent. It also shows loyalty to the king and the Catholic Church.
  - why not B: Article 8 asks that laws and taxes be ratified by the nation, but the cahier still praises "a beloved monarch" and wants "the stability of the monarchy."
  - why not C: The cahier does not mention Church lands, and Article 1 asks that Catholic worship be protected. The National Assembly, not this cahier, took over Church lands in November 1789.
  - why not D: Article 12 asks that offices held by nobles be opened to members of the Third Estate.
  - hint: Look for what the cahier asks to change and what it wants to keep.

**E5-P05** [Topic 5.4 · LO D · Skill 4.B]  The demand in article 10 is best understood in the context of
- (A) the king's plan to raise new taxes without calling the Estates-General
- (B) a dispute over whether the Estates-General would vote by order or by head
- (C) the nobles' demand that the Third Estate's number of deputies be doubled
- (D) a royal order that the three estates meet in one hall and vote as one body
KEY: B. Explanation: If each estate voted as one body, the clergy and nobles could outvote the Third Estate two to one. Counting votes by head would give the Third Estate, with half the deputies, a real chance to win.
  - why not A: Louis XVI had already agreed to call the Estates-General. The cahiers were written for that meeting.
  - why not C: In December 1788 Louis XVI's government, on Necker's advice, doubled the Third Estate's deputies. Doubling did not settle whether votes would be counted by order or by head.
  - why not D: On June 27, 1789, the king told the remaining clergy and nobles to join the National Assembly. This came after the cahiers had been written.
  - hint: Article 10 talks about how votes "should be taken and counted." Why would the Third Estate care how votes were counted?

**E5-P06** [Topic 5.4 · LO D · Skill 5.A · Continuity and Change]  Which of the following developments shows the Revolution going furthest beyond the demands in this cahier?
- (A) The National Assembly declared that citizens would pay taxes on an equal basis.
- (B) The Estates-General met in 1789 for the first time since 1614.
- (C) The National Convention abolished the monarchy and declared France a republic in 1792.
- (D) The National Assembly opened government offices to men of talent, whatever their birth.
KEY: C. Explanation: In spring 1789 the Third Estate of Carcassonne praised the king and wanted to keep the monarchy. In September 1792 the National Convention abolished the monarchy and set up a republic, going far beyond these demands.

  - why not B: The meeting of the Estates-General was what the cahier was written for, and article 9 asks for more meetings.
  - why not D: Opening offices to talent was the goal of article 12.
  - hint: Three choices match demands in the cahier. Which one does the cahier not ask for?


### Source P3 (questions E5-P07–E5-P09)
> The National Assembly, after having heard the report of the ecclesiastical committee, has decreed and do decree the following as constitutional articles:
> Title I, Article I. Each department* shall form a single diocese, and each diocese shall have the same extent and the same limits as the department. …
> IV. No church or parish of France nor any French citizen may acknowledge upon any occasion, or upon any pretext whatsoever, the authority of an ordinary bishop or of an archbishop whose see shall be under the supremacy of a foreign power, nor that of his representatives residing in France or elsewhere; without prejudice, however, to the unity of the faith and the intercourse which shall be maintained with the visible head of the universal Church, as hereafter provided. …
> Title II, Article I. Beginning with the day of publication of the present decree, there shall be but one mode of choosing bishops and parish priests, namely that of election. …
> XIX. The new bishop may not apply to the pope for any form of confirmation, but shall write to him, as to the visible head of the universal Church, as a testimony to the unity of faith and communion maintained with him.
> XXI. Before the ceremony of consecration begins, the bishop elect shall take a solemn oath … to be loyal to the nation, the law, and the king, and to support with all his power the constitution decreed by the National Assembly and accepted by the king. …
> Title III, Article I. The ministers of religion, performing as they do the first and most important functions of society … shall be supported by the nation.
> * In 1790 the National Assembly divided France into 83 new units called departments, each with its own elected officials.
— Civil Constitution of the Clergy, law passed by the National Assembly of France, July 1790 (English translation in James Harvey Robinson, Readings in European History, vol. 2 (Boston, 1906); adapted)
Words to know: ecclesiastical = having to do with the church; diocese = the area under one bishop; see = the district governed by a bishop or archbishop; supremacy = highest authority; without prejudice to = without harming; intercourse = communication; confirmation = official approval; consecration = the ceremony that makes a priest a bishop

**E5-P07** [Topic 5.4 · LO D · Skill 1.B]  The decree is best described as an effort to
- (A) make France's clergy independent of both the pope and the government
- (B) strengthen the pope's power to choose and approve French bishops
- (C) put the Catholic Church in France under the control of the state
- (D) return to the Church the lands the National Assembly had taken in 1789
KEY: C. Explanation: The decree made bishops and priests elected officials supported by the nation, fit dioceses to the new departments, and required newly elected bishops to swear loyalty. These rules put the Church in France under much greater state control.
  - why not A: The decree makes the clergy swear loyalty to the nation and takes their pay from the nation. It ties them to the government.
  - why not B: Article XIX says a new bishop "may not apply to the pope" for approval. The decree cuts the pope's power.
  - why not D: The decree pays priests salaries from the nation instead. The Church's lands were being sold.
  - hint: Who chooses the bishops under this decree, and who pays them?

**E5-P09** [Topic 5.4 · LO D · Skill 5.B · Causation]  The carrying out of this decree, including a later rule that serving clergy take an oath, contributed most directly to which of the following?
- (A) strong support for the Revolution among Catholic peasants in western France

- (C) a drop in the number of nobles leaving France to oppose the Revolution
- (D) a split among the clergy, as many priests refused to swear the required oath

> The French people to the people of ——; brothers and friends:

We offer to bring this inestimable blessing to you, for it has always been rightly ours, and only by a crime have our oppressors robbed us of it. We have driven out your tyrants.
