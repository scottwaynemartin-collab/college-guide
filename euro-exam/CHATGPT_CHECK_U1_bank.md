### Source P1 (questions E1-P01–E1-P03)
> Your Cicero has been in my possession four years and more. There is a good reason, though, for so long a delay; namely, the great scarcity of copyists who understand such work. It is a state of affairs that has resulted in an incredible loss to scholarship. Books that by their nature are a little hard to understand are no longer multiplied, and have ceased to be generally intelligible, and so have sunk into utter neglect, and in the end have perished. This age of ours consequently has let fall, bit by bit, some of the richest and sweetest fruits that the tree of knowledge has yielded; has thrown away the results of the vigils and labours of the most illustrious men of genius, things of more value, I am almost tempted to say, than anything else in the whole world. …
> But I must return to your Cicero. I could not do without it, and the incompetence of the copyists would not let me possess it. What was left for me but to rely upon my own resources, and press these weary fingers and this worn and ragged pen into the service? … Not a single word did I read except as I wrote. … So the pen held back the eye, and the eye drove on the pen, and I covered page after page, delighting in my task, and committing many and many a passage to memory as I wrote.
> … I said to myself, "So Cicero copied orations that another wrote, and you are not ready to copy his? What ardour! what scholarly devotion! what reverence for a man of godlike genius!"
— Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch), Italian poet and scholar, letter to Lapo da Castiglionchio, a friend in Florence who had lent him a book by Cicero, written in the 1350s (Translated by James Harvey Robinson and Henry Winchester Rolfe, Petrarch: The First Modern Scholar and Man of Letters (New York: G. P. Putnam's Sons, 1898), pp. 275–277; adapted)
> Words to know: Cicero = Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BCE), a Roman writer and speaker; copyists = people who copied books by hand; multiplied = here, copied; intelligible = understandable; vigils = nights spent awake working; orations = speeches; ardour = strong enthusiasm

**E1-P01** [Topic 1.2 · LO B · Skill 3.A · easy]  Petrarch's main concern in the first paragraph is that
- (A) ancient works were being lost because few people could copy them well
- (B) the Church had forbidden scholars to read pagan Roman writers
- (C) printers were filling the market with careless copies of old books
- (D) Latin had been replaced by Italian as the main language taught in the schools of Florence
KEY: A. Explanation: Petrarch says hard books "are no longer multiplied." In the end, he says, they "have perished." Without good copyists, the works of ancient writers were being lost.
  - why not B: There was no such ban. Monks had copied Roman writers like Cicero for centuries.
  - why not C: Printing with movable type did not begin in Europe until the 1450s, about a hundred years after this letter. Petrarch's problem was a shortage of skilled people who copied by hand.
  - why not D: Petrarch does not mention schools. Latin stayed the language of learning in European schools for centuries.
  - hint: Reread the sentences about copyists and about books that "have perished." What is Petrarch afraid of?

**E1-P02** [Topic 1.2 · LO B · Skill 1.B · medium]  Petrarch's attitude toward Cicero best illustrates which of the following?
- (A) the scholastic use of Aristotle to explain Christian beliefs
- (B) the Christian humanist call to translate the Bible into everyday languages
- (C) the humanist revival of interest in ancient Roman writers
- (D) the Renaissance artist's study of the human body
KEY: C. Explanation: Petrarch is often called the father of humanism. He searched for old Roman books and copied them by hand. He even calls Cicero a man of "godlike genius." This love of classical texts was at the heart of the Italian Renaissance.
  - why not A: Scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas used Aristotle's logic to explain Christian teaching. Petrarch admires Cicero's own writing and his "godlike genius."
  - why not B: Christian humanists such as Erasmus later wanted the Bible studied in its original languages and read in everyday languages. Petrarch is copying a work by a pagan Roman, not the Bible.
  - why not D: Artists such as Leonardo studied anatomy. Petrarch's letter is about copying a book, not about art.
  - hint: What kind of writer is Cicero, and how does Petrarch feel about him?

**E1-P03** [Topic 1.4 · LO E · Skill 5.B · Causation · medium]  The problem Petrarch describes in the first paragraph was most directly reduced by which later development?
- (A) The fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Turks
- (B) The printing press
- (C) The founding of monastery writing rooms
- (D) The Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books
KEY: B. Explanation: Printing with movable type spread from Mainz, in Germany, after the 1450s. A printer could make hundreds of copies of a book like Cicero's, so rare works were far less likely to be lost.
  - why not A: When Constantinople fell in 1453, Greek scholars and books came to Italy. That added texts, but it did not solve the shortage of people who could copy them.
  - why not C: Monasteries had used writing rooms to copy books by hand since the early Middle Ages, long before Petrarch. Hand copying was the slow method he complained about.
  - why not D: The Index, first issued in 1559, banned certain books. It limited reading instead of making more copies.
  - hint: Think about what happened about a hundred years after this letter that changed how books were made.


### Source P2 (questions E1-P04–E1-P06)
> Having acquired all the books of every department which were to be found, written both by ancient and modern doctors, and translations as well in every branch, he desired to have all the Greek books that were to be found; all the works of Aristotle in Greek; all the works of Plato, each volume bound in the finest kid-skin; all the works of Homer in one volume … the Cosmography of Ptolemy, with illustrations, in Greek, a most excellent book … There were to be seen Hebrew books, all that could be found in that language, beginning with the Bible, and all those who have commented upon it …
> His Lordship* having completed this worthy task at the great expense of more than 30,000 ducats, … In this library all the books are beautiful in the highest degree, all written with the pen, not one printed, that it might not be disgraced thereby; all elegantly illuminated, and there is not one that is not written on kid-skin.
> * Federico da Montefeltro (1422–1482) became rich as a condottiere, a commander of hired soldiers who fought for other Italian states.
— Vespasiano da Bisticci, Florentine bookseller, describing the library of Federico da Montefeltro, Duke of Urbino (died 1482), in his Lives of Illustrious Men, written in the late 1400s (Translated in Merrick Whitcomb, A Literary Source-Book of the Italian Renaissance (Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania, 1898; printing of 1900), pp. 75–76; adapted)
> Words to know: department = subject; doctors = here, learned writers; kid-skin = fine leather or parchment made from the skin of young goats; Cosmography of Ptolemy = a geography of the known world by a Greek scholar of the 100s CE; ducats = gold coins; illuminated = decorated by hand with color and gold

  - why not B: The library held the Bible and Christian writers. Federico was a Catholic ruler, and the pope made him Duke of Urbino in 1474.
  - why not C: The passage lists Greek, Hebrew, and religious works, not business books.

KEY: B. Explanation: Humanists wanted to read Plato, Homer, and the Bible in Greek and Hebrew. They did not want to rely only on Latin translations. Reading texts in the first language was part of a new, careful way of study.

**E1-P06** [Topic 1.4 · LO E · Skill 3.A · easy]  Vespasiano's remark that none of the books in the library was printed best supports which conclusion?
- (A) Some rich collectors at first valued handwritten books more.
- (B) Printing had not yet reached Italy when Federico built his library.

- (D) Printed books cost more than books written on kid-skin.

  - why not D: Printed books were usually much cheaper, since many copies could be made at once. That is one reason a proud collector looked down on them.

### Source P3 (questions E1-P07–E1-P09)

**E1-P09** [Topic 1.6 · LO G · Skill 5.B · Causation · medium]  The kind of military technology Leonardo describes later helped European states expand overseas mainly by

### Source P4 (questions E1-P10–E1-P12)
> Nothing makes a prince so well thought of as to undertake great enterprises and give striking proofs of his capacity.
> Among the princes of our time Ferdinand of Aragon, the present king of Spain, may almost be accounted a new prince, since from one of the weakest he has become, for fame and glory, the foremost king in Christendom. And if you consider his achievements, you will find them all great, and some extraordinary.
> In the beginning of his reign he made war on Granada, which enterprise was the foundation of his power. — Niccolò Machiavelli, Florentine official and political writer, The Prince, written in 1513 (Translated by N. H. Thomson (2nd ed., Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1897), as printed in James Harvey Robinson, Readings in European History, vol. 2 (Boston: Ginn, 1906), pp. 9–10; adapted)

**E1-P11** [Topic 1.2 · LO C · Skill 1.B · medium]  Machiavelli's way of judging Ferdinand's "pious cruelty" best reflects which of the following?
- (A) The belief that kings receive their power from God
- (B) The Christian humanist ideal of a prince who follows Christ
- (C) The idea that natural law limits what rulers may do

  - why not C: Natural-law thinkers such as Grotius later argued that some rules bind all rulers and nations. Machiavelli sets no such limits. He judges actions by whether they make a ruler stronger.

> In order that the subjects of His Illustrious Highness, the most honorable and magnanimous Duke of Urbino … may have all the rules that a good merchant needs, I decided to compile, in addition to the subjects already treated in this work, a special treatise which is much needed. … For, as we know, there are three things needed by any one who wishes to carry on business carefully. ### Source X1 (questions E1-E01–E1-E03)
> In the character of these states, whether republics or despotisms, lies, not the only, but the chief reason for the early development of the Italian. To this it is due that he was the first-born among the sons of modern Europe.
> In the Middle Ages both sides of human consciousness—that which was turned within as that which was turned without—lay dreaming or half-awake beneath a common veil. ### Source X7 (questions E1-E19–E1-E21)
> … Of all these nations God our Lord gave charge to one man, called St. Peter, that he should be Lord and Superior of all the men in the world …
> One of these Pontiffs, who succeeded that St. Peter as Lord of the world, … made donation of these isles and Terra-firma to the aforesaid King and Queen and to their successors, our lords, with all that there are in these territories … So their Highnesses are kings and lords of these islands and land of Terra-firma by virtue of this donation …
> Wherefore, as best we can, we ask and require you that you consider what we have said to you, and that you take the time that shall be necessary to understand and deliberate upon it, and that you acknowledge the Church as the Ruler and Superior of the whole world … and the high priest called Pope, and in his name the King and Queen Doña Juana our lords, in his place, as superiors and lords and kings of these islands and this Terra-firma by virtue of the said donation, and that you consent and give place that these religious fathers should declare and preach to you the aforesaid.
> … But, if you do not do this, and maliciously make delay in it, I certify to you that, with the help of God, we shall powerfully enter into your country, and shall make war against you in all ways and manners that we can, and shall subject you to the yoke and obedience of the Church and of their Highnesses; we shall take you and your wives and your children, and shall make slaves of them, and as such shall sell and dispose of them as their Highnesses may command; and we shall take away your goods, and shall do you all the mischief and damage that we can … and we protest that the deaths and losses which shall accrue from this are your fault, and not that of their Highnesses, or ours, nor of these cavaliers who come with us.
— The Requirement (Requerimiento), a statement written in 1513 by Juan López de Palacios Rubios, a legal adviser to the Spanish crown, to be read aloud to Native American peoples by Spanish commanders before they made war (English translation in Arthur Helps, The Spanish Conquest in America, vol. 1 (London: John W. Parker, 1855), pp. 379–382; adapted)
> Words to know: Pontiffs = popes; the King and Queen Doña Juana = King Ferdinand of Aragon and his daughter, Queen Juana of Castile, for whom he governed Castile; donation = gift; Terra-firma = the mainland of Central and South America; deliberate upon = think carefully about; religious fathers = priests; maliciously = with bad intentions; accrue = result; cavaliers = soldiers on horseback

  - C: Incorrect. The Reformation began in 1517, after the Requirement was written, and the document supports the pope's authority.
  - D: Incorrect. The Dutch began trading in the East Indies around 1600, nearly a century later.

### Source X8 (questions E1-E22–E1-E25)
**Source 1**
> The King. Our officials who reside in the city of Seville in our House of Trade of the Indies; Know ye that I have given permission, and by the present [instrument] do give it, to Lorenzo de Gorrevod, governor of Bresa, member of my Council, whereby he, or the person or persons who may have his authority therefor, may proceed to take to the Indies, the islands and the mainland of the ocean sea already discovered or to be discovered, four thousand negro slaves both male and female, provided they be Christians, in whatever proportions he may choose. …
> … if the said governor of Bresa or the persons aforesaid who may have his authority, should make any arrangements with traders or other persons to ship the said slaves, male or female, direct from the isles of Guinea and other regions from which they are wont to bring the said negroes to these realms and to Portugal, or from any other region they please, even though they do not bring them to register in that house, they may do so provided that you take sufficient security that they bring you proof of how many they have taken to each island and that the said negroes male and female, have become Christians on reaching each island, and how they have paid the customs duties there … .
> Done in Saragossa, the eighteenth day of August of the year 1518.
— King Charles I of Spain (who became Holy Roman Emperor Charles V in 1519), royal license to Lorenzo de Gorrevod, a member of his council, 1518 (English translation in Elizabeth Donnan, ed., Documents Illustrative of the History of the Slave Trade to America, vol. 1 (Washington: Carnegie Institution, 1930), pp. 41–42; adapted. The bracketed word is Donnan's)
> Words to know: House of Trade of the Indies = the royal office in Seville that controlled trade with Spain's American colonies; Bresa = Bresse, a region then part of the Duchy of Savoy, now in eastern France; the Indies = Spain's lands in the Americas; the isles of Guinea = the West African coast and its islands; are wont to = usually do; these realms = the Spanish kingdoms; security = a promise backed by money
**Source 2**
> Your Reverence writes me that you would like to know whether the negroes who are sent to your parts have been legally captured. To this I reply that I think your Reverence should have no scruples on this point, because this is a matter which has been questioned by the Board of Conscience in Lisbon, and all its members are learned and conscientious men. Nor did the bishops who were in São Thomé, Cape Verde, and here in Loando—all learned and virtuous men—find fault with it. We have been here ourselves for forty years and there have been [among us] very learned Fathers; in the Province of Brazil as well, where there have always been Fathers of our order eminent in letters, never did they consider this trade as illicit. Therefore we and the fathers of Brazil buy these slaves for our service without any scruple. …
> … I declare, moreover, that in the fairs where these negroes are bought there are always a few who have been captured illegally because they were stolen or because the rulers of the land order them to be sold for offenses so slight that they do not deserve captivity, but these are few in number and to seek among ten or twelve thousand who leave this port every year for a few who have been illegally captured is an impossibility, however careful investigation may be made. And to lose so many souls as sail from here—out of whom many are saved—because some, impossible to recognize, have been captured illegally does not seem to be doing much service to God, for these are few and those who find salvation are many and legally captured.
— Luis Brandão, head of the Jesuit college in Luanda, in the Portuguese colony of Angola, letter to Alonso de Sandoval, a Jesuit priest in the Spanish colonies who had asked whether the slave trade was lawful, March 12, 1610 (English translation in Elizabeth Donnan, ed., Documents Illustrative of the History of the Slave Trade to America, vol. 1 (Washington: Carnegie Institution, 1930), pp. 123–124; adapted. The bracketed words are Donnan's)
> Words to know: Your Reverence = a polite way to address a priest; scruples = moral doubts; Board of Conscience in Lisbon = a royal council of churchmen and lawyers that advised the king of Portugal on moral questions; São Thomé, Cape Verde = Portuguese island colonies off the coast of Africa; Loando = Luanda, the main Portuguese port in Angola; our order = the Jesuits, a Catholic religious order; illicit = against the law or morally wrong; fairs = markets; salvation = being saved by God after death

**E1-E22** [Topic 1.9 · LO L · Skill 4.A · medium]  Source 1 is best understood in the context of which of the following?

- (D) The spread of serfdom in eastern Europe

  - C: Incorrect. The Dutch did not enter the Atlantic slave trade on a large scale until the 1600s.

**E1-E25** [Topic 1.9 · LO L · Skill 5.A · Continuity and Change · medium]  Taken together, the two sources best illustrate which change between 1518 and 1610?
- (A) Church leaders turned against the slave trade and persuaded rulers to limit it.

CONTEXT NOTE ADDED (version 2026-11-09c):
Ferdinand gained control of the three military orders in stages, not by one grant in 1489. Calatrava came under the Catholic Monarchs on the death of its last master, García López de Padilla, in 1487; Santiago after the death of Alonso de Cárdenas (1493), with papal grants in the 1490s (Wikipedia, 'Order of Santiago', dates the papal assignment of its administration to 1499); Alcántara in the early 1490s (the Catholic Encyclopedia, 'Order of Alcántara', says Alexander VI gave Ferdinand its grand mastership for life in 1492). Pope Adrian VI joined the three masterships permanently to the crown in 1523 (some accounts say 1522). Sources differ on the exact years. E1-P46's why-not D now cites only words the students can see ('the virtual incorporation', 'establishing the royal supremacy') and adds the safe general fact that popes allowed Ferdinand to run the orders.
