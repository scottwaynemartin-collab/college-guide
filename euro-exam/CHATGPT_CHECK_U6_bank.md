### Source P1 (questions E6-P01–E6-P03)
> The natural and physical advantages of England for manufacturing industry are probably superior to those of every other country on the globe. …
> Three things may be regarded as of primary importance for the successful prosecution of manufactures, namely, water-power, fuel, and iron. Wherever these exist in combination, and where they are abundant and cheap, machinery may be manufactured and put in motion at small cost …
> Not less important for manufactures than the copious supply of good water, is the great abundance of coal found in the very same district. … It is superfluous to remark that this mineral fuel animates the thousand arms of the steam-engine, and furnishes the most powerful agent in all chemical and mechanical operations. …
> To the above natural advantages, we must add, the acquired advantage of a canal communication, which ramifies itself through all the populous parts of this county* … The canals, having been accomplished by individual enterprise, not by national funds, were constructed to supply a want already existing … The recent introduction of railways will have a great effect in making the operations of trade more intensely active, and perfecting the division of labour, already carried to so high a point. …
> The political and moral advantages of this country, as a seat of manufactures, are not less remarkable than its physical advantages. The arts are the daughters of peace and liberty. In no country have these blessings been enjoyed in so high a degree, or for so long a continuance, as in England. > Words to know: prosecution = carrying on; copious = large, plentiful; superfluous = not needed; animates = brings to life, powers; ramifies itself = branches out; individual enterprise = private businesspeople; national funds = government money; division of labour = splitting work into many small, separate tasks; the arts = here, skilled trades and industries; mercantile enterprise = business and trade; capital = money used to start or grow a business

**E6-P01** [Topic 6.1 · LO A · Skill 3.A · easy]  Which of the following best describes Baines's main argument?
- (A) England led in manufacturing because of its resources, its transport, and its secure laws.
- (B) England led in manufacturing because its government owned the mines, canals, and mills.
- (C) England led in manufacturing because its guilds trained more skilled workers than other countries.
- (D) England led in manufacturing because its growing population created a larger market for cotton cloth.
KEY: A. Explanation: Baines names three kinds of advantages. Some came from nature: water power, coal, and iron close together. Some were built: canals and new railways. Some came from government: "just laws," secure property, and safe money for business. Together, he says, they explain England's lead.
  - why not B: Baines says the canals were built "by individual enterprise, not by national funds." British mines and mills were private businesses too.
  - why not C: Guilds had lost most of their power in England by the 1700s. Baines credits water, coal, iron, canals, railways, and secure property instead.
  - why not D: A growing population did help British industry. But Baines does not give it as a reason here. He credits England's resources, transport, and laws for its lead.
  - hint: Baines sorts England's advantages into a few kinds. What are they?

**E6-P02** [Topic 6.1 · LO A · Skill 5.B · Comparison · medium]  Baines notes that England's canals were built "by individual enterprise, not by national funds." In contrast, industrialization in many continental European states after 1815 was more often
- (A) backed by governments that built or helped pay for railways
- (B) slowed by laws that banned private companies from building railways
- (C) led by craft guilds that owned and ran the new factories
- (D) led by peasant cooperatives that pooled village savings to build factories
KEY: A. Explanation: In Britain, private businessmen did most of the work of industrializing. In France, Belgium, and the German states, governments did more. They built or backed railways, set tariffs, and supported banks and technical schools.
  - why not B: Governments on the continent built their own lines, but they also helped private railway companies. France's railway law of 1842, for example, joined state and private building.
  - why not C: Guilds had little to do with the new factories, and many states weakened or abolished them in the 1800s. Factories were owned by private businessmen, sometimes with state help.
  - why not D: Farm cooperatives spread later in the 1800s. They mostly ran dairies and village credit banks, not railways or factories. On the continent, states, banks, and private investors paid for industry.
  - hint: Who paid for transport in Britain, according to Baines? Was it the same on the continent?

**E6-P03** [Topic 6.2 · LO B · Skill 3.D · medium]  Baines's claim about England's "political and moral advantages" is best supported by which of the following?
- (A) Britain's kings ruled without Parliament for most of the 1700s, which kept taxes low for manufacturers.
- (B) Britain's Parliament, which included merchants and manufacturers, passed laws protecting property.
- (C) Britain's Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to most adult men, including factory workers.
- (D) Britain's Parliament passed the Corn Laws to keep bread cheap for factory workers.
KEY: B. Explanation: Baines says that in England "personal liberty and property have been secure" and "capital has accumulated in safety." Britain's Parliament included men who made money from trade and industry. It passed laws that protected property and helped trade grow.
  - why not A: Parliament met every year after 1689. Britain's kings did not rule without it.
  - why not C: The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote mainly to middle-class men. Most working men still could not vote, as the Chartists complained a few years later.
  - why not D: The Corn Laws taxed grain brought in from abroad. They kept bread prices high and helped landowners, and manufacturers such as Richard Cobden fought to repeal them.
  - hint: Baines praises "just laws." Which choice describes British government accurately?


### Source P2 (questions E6-P04–E6-P06)
> … under the existing conditions of the world, the result of general free trade would not be a universal republic, but, on the contrary, a universal subjection of the less advanced nations to the supremacy of the predominant manufacturing, commercial, and naval power … The system of protection, inasmuch as it forms the only means of placing those nations which are far behind in civilisation on equal terms with the one predominating nation (which, however, never received at the hands of Nature a perpetual right to a monopoly of manufacture, but which merely gained an advance over others in point of time), the system of protection regarded from this point of view appears to be the most efficient means of furthering the final union of nations, and hence also of promoting true freedom of trade. …
> The advance which England has made in manufactures, navigation, and commerce, need therefore not discourage any other nation which is fitted for manufacturing production … But above all things we must have enough national spirit at once to plant and protect the tree, which will yield its first richest fruits only to future generations. We must first gain possession of the home market of our own nation, at least as respects articles of general necessity … This is especially the task which the German commercial union has to solve, if the German nation is not to remain far behind the French and North Americans, nay, far behind even the Russians. …
> It is a very common clever device that when anyone has attained the summit of greatness, he kicks away the ladder by which he has climbed up, in order to deprive others of the means of climbing up after him. In this lies the secret of the cosmopolitical doctrine of Adam Smith …
— Friedrich List, German economist who had lived in the United States, The National System of Political Economy, 1841 (English translation by Sampson S. Lloyd (1885), in Friedrich List, The National System of Political Economy, new edition (London: Longmans, Green, 1904; impression of 1909), pp. 102–103, 162, 295; adapted)
> Words to know: subjection = being put under someone else's control; supremacy = highest power; protection = a policy of taxing imported goods to help a country's own industries; inasmuch as = because; predominating nation = the most powerful nation (here, Britain); perpetual = lasting forever; monopoly = complete control of a market by one seller; German commercial union = the Zollverein, a customs union of German states; cosmopolitical doctrine = an economic theory that looks only at the good of the whole world, not of single nations

**E6-P04** [Topic 6.3 · LO D · Skill 3.A · easy]  Which of the following best describes List's main argument?
- (A) Nations should adopt free trade right away so that a peaceful union of nations can form.
- (B) Germany should win colonies overseas before it tries to build its own industries.
- (C) Less developed nations should protect their industries with tariffs until they can compete.
- (D) Britain's lead in manufacturing is a permanent gift of nature that other nations cannot hope to match.
KEY: C. Explanation: List says free trade helps the nation that is already ahead. Nations that are behind, such as Germany, should tax imports to "plant and protect the tree" of their own industry.
  - why not A: List says free trade can come only after nations reach the same level. Until then, he calls protection "the only means" for nations that are "far behind."
  - why not B: List says Germany must "first gain possession of the home market of our own nation." Colonies are not his first step.
  - why not D: List says Britain "never received at the hands of Nature a perpetual right to a monopoly of manufacture." It only got there first.
  - hint: What does List want Germany to do with its "home market"?

**E6-P05** [Topic 6.2 · LO B · Skill 2.B · medium]  Members of which group would have most strongly disagreed with List's argument?
- (A) German manufacturers facing British competition
- (B) British free traders such as Richard Cobden
- (C) Prussian officials who led the Zollverein
- (D) American supporters of protective tariffs
KEY: B. Explanation: Cobden and the Anti-Corn Law League believed free trade made every nation richer and more peaceful. List made fun of that view. He said Britain preached free trade only after protection had made it strong. It was like someone who "kicks away the ladder" after climbing up.
  - why not A: German manufacturers wanted protection from cheaper British goods. They would have welcomed List's ideas.
  - why not C: List hoped the Zollverein, the "German commercial union," would protect Germany's home market. Some Prussian officials wanted lower tariffs than List did. But they did not reject all protection, as Cobden did.
  - why not D: List had lived in the United States and admired American tariffs that protected new industries. Their supporters agreed with him.
  - hint: List attacks the "cosmopolitical doctrine of Adam Smith." Who in the 1840s followed Smith's ideas on trade?

**E6-P06** [Topic 6.3 · LO D · Skill 5.B · Continuity and Change · medium]  List's ideas were most directly reflected in which of the following later developments?
- (A) Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846
- (B) Britain's repeal of the Navigation Acts in 1849
- (C) The Anglo-French free-trade treaty of 1860
- (D) The German Empire's protective tariff law of 1879
KEY: D. Explanation: List's book helped convince Germans that new industries needed protection. In 1879, during a long economic slump, Bismarck's German Empire put tariffs on imported factory goods and grain.
  - why not A: Repealing the Corn Laws moved Britain toward free trade in grain. List wanted something different: tariffs that protect a nation's new industries until they can compete.
  - why not B: Ending the Navigation Acts let foreign ships carry British trade. It was another step toward free trade.
  - why not C: The Cobden-Chevalier Treaty of 1860 cut tariffs between Britain and France. It moved away from protection, not toward it.
  - hint: Three of these choices lowered trade barriers. Which one raised them?
