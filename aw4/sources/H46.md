EXCERPT (verbatim):

The fiscal history of the Company can be roughly summarized by saying that in its early period, when it was more trader than ruler, it made money on the whole; that in its later period, when it was more ruler than trader, it lost.

There is a temptation to argue from this that the Company was ruined by a change of policy, or, if not that, that it grew weaker internally and owed its fall to a corruption of its administration. I believe that neither of these hypotheses is true, but that the Company was inefficient both in respect to trade and government from its very beginning. It enjoyed, however, exceptionally good luck when it began its career; it found its rivals in the East so weak that it was able by crude measures to secure a monopoly of the trade, and it sold its products for very high prices in the European market. Prices fell as time went on and rivals grew stronger; then first the weaknesses grew apparent, and it was forced by competition from the field of trade to the field of government where it could protect itself for a time. There can be no doubt that the extension by the Dutch of their political influence in Java was due to the fact that they were unable longer to make money by buying goods in the open market, and were forced to get them in a way which for a time proved cheaper.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Day argues that the Dutch East India Company was both a business and a government, that it made money while it was mainly a trader but lost money once it became mainly a ruler, and that it turned to ruling Java not by choice but because rising competition forced it to get goods by political power (tribute and forced deliveries) instead of by trade.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: Clive Day (1871-1951), professor of economic history at Yale. This was a scholarly monograph based on the published Dutch colonial archives (De Jonge's document collections); Day also wrote the textbook A History of Commerce (1907).
- Background a student needs: "the Company" = the Dutch East India Company (VOC), chartered by the Dutch States General in 1602 as a joint-stock company with a trade monopoly east of the Cape of Good Hope and the power to make war, build forts, and sign treaties (see H46b). Its Asian headquarters was Batavia (Jakarta) on Java from 1619. It was dissolved at the end of 1799.
- Claim and counter-claim built in: Day names two rival explanations ("a change of policy"; "corruption of its administration") and rejects both for his own (built-in inefficiency hidden by early luck, then competition pushed it into governing). That structure is ideal for AP "identify the argument" or "explain how a historian challenges another view" questions.
- Point of view: an American economist of the Progressive Era, sympathetic to free trade and critical of monopoly; elsewhere he condemns the Company's forced-delivery system for harming Javanese farmers. Later historians generally agree the VOC profited greatly in the 1600s and declined in the 1700s, but debate the causes (competition from the English East India Company, smuggling and corruption, falling spice prices, high military costs).
- Vocabulary: "fiscal history" (history of its income and spending); "monopoly"; "crude measures" (force, e.g., the conquest of the Banda Islands in 1621 to control nutmeg, a good example to cite).
