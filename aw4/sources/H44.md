EXCERPT (verbatim):

The first advent of the slave traders increased and encouraged native industry, as is evidenced by the bronze work of Benin; but soon this was pushed into the background, for it was not bronze metal but bronze flesh that Europe wanted. A new tyranny, blood-thirsty, cruel, and built on war, forced itself forward in the Niger delta. The powerful state of Dahomey arose early in the eighteenth century and became a devastating tyranny, reaching its highest power early in the nineteenth century. Ashanti, a similar kingdom, began its conquests in 1719 and grew with the slave trade. Thus state building in West Africa began to replace the city economy, but it was a state built on war and on war supported and encouraged largely for the sake of trade in human flesh. The native industries were changed and disorganized. Family ties and government were weakened. Far into the heart of Africa this devilish disintegration, coupled with Christian rum and Mohammedan raiding, penetrated. The face of Africa was turned south on these slave traders instead of northward toward the Mediterranean, where for two thousand years and more Europe and Africa had met in legitimate trade and mutual respect. . . . Hereafter Africa for centuries was to appear before the world, not as the land of gold and ivory, of Mansa Musa and Meroe, but as a bound and captive slave, dumb and degraded.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Du Bois argues that the Atlantic slave trade at first stimulated some West African industry but soon reshaped the region around war and slave raiding: states like Dahomey and Asante grew powerful by selling captives, while local industries, families, and governments were disrupted and Africa's older image as a land of wealth was replaced by the image of a slave.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: W. E. B. Du Bois (1868-1963), the first African American to earn a Harvard PhD (1895), sociologist and historian, a founder of the NAACP (1909) and editor of its magazine The Crisis. His first book was The Suppression of the African Slave-Trade (1896). The Negro (1915), a short survey for a general-reader series, was one of the first English-language histories to present Africa as having a long history of states and cultures.
- Purpose / point of view: written to refute the racist claim, common among white scholars of his day, that Africa had no history and that slavery reflected African inferiority; he argues Africa's later "stagnation" was caused by the slave trade. The tone is openly moral ("devilish disintegration").
- Connects directly to the CED: KC-4.3.II.A.ii says the Asante and Kongo grew in influence through participation in Atlantic trade networks; Du Bois agrees they grew but stresses the human cost ("a state built on war").
- Accuracy notes for the teacher: Asante's rise is now usually dated to about 1700 under Osei Tutu (defeat of Denkyira, 1701) rather than "1719." Many modern historians (e.g., John Thornton, 1992) stress that African rulers and merchants controlled the supply side and that Asante's wealth also came from gold and kola, not slaves alone; Walter Rodney (1972) developed Du Bois's argument that the trade "underdeveloped" Africa. This makes a good "how might another historian respond?" prompt.
- Dated or figurative language (left verbatim): "native" (local/African); "Mohammedan" (older term for Muslim; "Mohammedan raiding" refers to slave raiding from the Muslim north and the trans-Saharan trade); "Christian rum" (European rum traded for captives); "bronze flesh" (Du Bois's wordplay contrasting Benin's bronze art with enslaved people); "dumb" (here meaning voiceless). Meroe = ancient Nubian kingdom; Mansa Musa = 14th-century ruler of Mali famed for his gold.
