From 1503-10 to 1591-1600 there was a steady increase in the receipts of treasure. The period 1600-30 showed a marked decline, and the period 1630-60 a precipitous drop, in registered gold and silver. Altho the scope of the present paper does not permit a complete explanation of the dwindling receipts of specie, the following causes may be named: an increase in interloping; a rise in the expenses of mining together with fixed prices of the precious metals; a decrease in the fertility of the mines; an increase in the averia, which in certain years became confiscatory; decimation of the labor supply by rude work in the mines; an increase in trade with the Orient; unnecessary delay in the delivery of private treasure; delivery of private bullion in vellon at more than its market value; a revival of the practice of sequestration in the closing years of the reign of Philip IV; and an increase in the wealth and population of the Indies, which caused more treasure to be retained in the colonies.

[Hamilton's footnote to "an increase in trade with the Orient":] Immediately after the permanent settlement of the Philippines, the Spanish colonists began to trade with the Orient through the port of Acapulco in New Spain. [...] In 1659 the House of Trade and Sevillan Merchant Gild estimated that 500,000 pesos of Peruvian treasure annually made their way to China through the port of Acapulco in New Spain.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Units: Hamilton's note says "In this case peso means 450 maravedis' worth of gold or silver"; these are registered (legal, taxed) imports only, so smuggling is not counted and real flows were larger. "Public" = crown receipts (mainly the royal fifth, the quinto); "private" = merchants' and colonists' treasure. Figures are ANNUAL AVERAGES for each decade.
- Hamilton adds (p. 464) that the average annual receipts for 1591-95 "would have paid for 21 1/3 days' work of all the persons in the country employed for salaries and wages" in Spain. Peak decade: 1591-1600 (about 13.9 million pesos a year).
- Terms: "interloping" = illegal trade by non-licensed (often foreign) merchants; "averia" = a convoy tax on treasure fleets; "vellon" = copper (billon) coinage; "sequestration" = royal seizure of private treasure; "the Indies" = Spanish America; "New Spain" = Mexico. "Decimation of the labor supply by rude work in the mines" refers to deaths of Indigenous forced laborers (e.g., under the mita at Potosí).
- Main silver sources: Potosí (Bolivia, from 1545) and Zacatecas/Guanajuato (Mexico). The Acapulco-Manila galleon trade (from 1565-1571) carried American silver to Manila to buy Chinese silk and porcelain. Hamilton later expanded this work in American Treasure and the Price Revolution in Spain, 1501-1650 (1934; not public domain, not used).
