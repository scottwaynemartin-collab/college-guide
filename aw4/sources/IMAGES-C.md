## IMG-C1 (Topic 4.4, also 4.1/4.5): Nanban byōbu, "Arrival of the Southern Barbarians," by Kanō Naizen, c. 1600 (PRIMARY)

- File page: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nanban-Screens-by-Kano-Naizen-c1600.png
- Direct thumbnail (1280 px): https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/Nanban-Screens-by-Kano-Naizen-c1600.png/1280px-Nanban-Screens-by-Kano-Naizen-c1600.png
  - Verification: a GET at 16:19 UTC returned HTTP 200, image/png. The downloaded file is a valid PNG of 1280 x 554 px, and I viewed it. Later HEAD checks returned HTTP 429 because Wikimedia was rate-limiting this machine. The URL itself is correct.
- Original file size: 9500 x 4112 px (PNG)
- License line on the file page: {{PD-Art|PD-old-auto-expired|deathyear=1616}}. Commons summary: "Public domain."
- Creator / date / holder: Kanō Naizen (1570-1616), c. 1600. Ink, color and gold leaf on paper; six-panel folding screen (the right-hand screen of a pair). Kobe City Museum, Japan (https://www.kobecitymuseum.jp/collection/detail?heritage=365028). Commons got the image from Google Arts & Culture.
- What is shown: The screen is a single wide scene framed by gold-leaf clouds. On the left a large dark European carrack with three masts lies at anchor. Flags and pennants fly from it, sailors climb and perch in the rigging, and a small boat ferries people and goods to shore. In the center a crowd of newly landed Portuguese merchants walks along the beach. They wear tall or brimmed hats and baggy, puffed trousers. One richly dressed leader walks under a large dark parasol carried by a servant. Attendants, several of them dark-skinned (servants or enslaved people traveling with the Portuguese), carry chests, bundles and a palanquin-like box. A white horse is led along, and small dogs run among the crowd. On the right, black-robed Jesuit priests and grey-robed friars come out to greet the arrivals beside a row of shops, with Japanese people watching. At upper right is a tiled Japanese-style building with an interior scene. The Commons description says it is a Christian church (Nanbanji) with a ceremony before an altar, and that the shop sells imported goods such as tiger and leopard skins, silk and ceramics. Use the image to show that trade and Catholic missionary work arrived together. It also shows Japanese artists' curiosity about the "Southern Barbarians" (Nanban, the Japanese name for the Portuguese) before the Tokugawa restrictions of the 1630s (compare C44).

## IMG-C2 (Topic 4.2, LO B/C; also 4.4): Cantino planisphere, 1502, showing the Tordesillas line

- File page: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cantino_planisphere_(1502).jpg
- Direct thumbnail (1280 px): https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9c/Cantino_planisphere_%281502%29.jpg/1280px-Cantino_planisphere_%281502%29.jpg
  - Verification: curl -I returned HTTP/2 200, content-type image/jpeg, 296,780 bytes. The downloaded file is a 1280 x 601 px JPEG, and I viewed it.
- Original file size: 10643 x 4998 px
- License line on the file page: {{PD-Scan|PD-old-100}}. It is also a Commons featured picture.
- Creator / date / holder: an anonymous Portuguese cartographer, 1502. Manuscript chart on parchment. Biblioteca Estense Universitaria, Modena, Italy. It is named for Alberto Cantino, the agent of the Duke of Ferrara, who obtained it in Lisbon in 1502.
- What is shown: A hand-drawn, colored world chart. It is covered with compass roses and straight rhumb lines, and red horizontal lines mark the Arctic Circle, the Tropic of Cancer, the Equator (labeled "Linha equinocialis") and the Tropic of Capricorn. Africa is in the center and drawn in detail. Its coasts carry small flags and pictures of towns and castles, including a fortress on the Gulf of Guinea coast labeled "Castello damina" (the Portuguese fort of São Jorge da Mina/Elmina). Europe and the Mediterranean are at top center. Arabia, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf are painted in color, with India and a rough, partly guessed outline of Southeast Asia to the right. At the far left a single straight vertical line runs from top to bottom through the Atlantic Ocean. This is the line of demarcation of the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), and a column of red lettering runs beside it; the lettering is too small to read in the 1280 px thumbnail, so use the full file to quote it. West of the line are Caribbean islands, labeled in red as the Antilles of the King of Castile ("Has antilhas del Rey de castella"). East of the line are a stretch of the Brazilian coast, drawn green with trees and parrots, and in the north a land labeled as belonging to the King of Portugal ("Terra del Rey de portuguall"). Together these show the Spanish-Portuguese division of newly reached lands. The labels were read from the 1280 px thumbnail; check spellings on the full-resolution file before quoting them.
- Use notes: Pair with C41/C42 (Portuguese motives and da Gama). The map records Portuguese knowledge only about four years after da Gama reached India (1498) and two years after Cabral reached Brazil (1500).
