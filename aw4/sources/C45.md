STATUS: Optional target. No ≤1930 English text of Kangxi's 1721 vermilion comment on the papal legate's decree was found in the time allowed. This is the best public-domain substitute found: an official Qing imperial statement (1724) classing Christianity as a "corrupt" sect while keeping missionaries at court for their mathematics.

EXCERPT (verbatim):
With respect to books which were not written by the sages, and unsanctioned records, which alarm the age, and astonish the vulgar; which promote irregularities, and eat the people as a canker,--these all contain strange dogmas which should be rooted out. [...]

From of old three sects have been delivered down. Beside the sect of the learned, there are those of Taou and Fuh. [...]

The sect of the Western Ocean which honours Teen-choo, ranks also among those that are corrupt; but because these men [i. e. the Romish missionaries] understand the mathematics, therefore government employs them: of this you ought to be aware. To walk in these by-roads and deceive the people is what the law will not excuse. The impositions of conjurors have also a determined punishment. The intention of government in enacting these laws was none other than to prohibit the people from doing evil, and encourage them to do good; to induce them to degrade the corrupt, and honour the pure; to retire from danger, and advance to repose. [...]

Soldiers and people! act conformably to his sacred injunctions; and stop the progress of these strange sects as you would that of torrents, flames, robbers, and thieves. Indeed the injury of torrents, flames, robbers, and thieves, terminates on the body; but that of false religions extends to the human heart.

Milne italicizes Teen-choo, Taou, Fuh (italics not marked here). Spacing before semicolons normalized. "[i. e. the Romish missionaries]" is Milne's own bracketed insertion in the 1817 text. "[...]" marks my omissions: after the first paragraph, a sentence pitying soldiers and people misled into "devious paths"; after "Taou and Fuh," a quotation from Zhu Xi (Choo-tsse) and a paragraph on wandering impostors and secret societies (the Pah-leen/White Lotus and Wan-heang sects); after "advance to repose," two paragraphs on obedience and praise of Kangxi. "his sacred injunctions" = the Kangxi Emperor's. The 1870 reprint differs in small details (e.g. "by-roads"; a doubled "the the" in its bracket), so cite the 1817 edition.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Kangxi (r. 1661-1722) issued the 16 maxims of the Sacred Edict in 1670; Yongzheng (r. 1722-1735) published the "Amplified Instructions" in 1724, and both were read aloud to the public twice a month across the empire. The seventh maxim told subjects to reject heterodox teachings and uphold Confucian orthodoxy ("the sect of the learned").
- "The sect of the Western Ocean which honours Teen-choo" = Roman Catholic Christianity (Tianzhu, "Lord of Heaven"); "Taou and Fuh" = Daoism and Buddhism. Jesuits (e.g., at the imperial Astronomical Bureau) were kept at Beijing for astronomy, calendar-making and mathematics.
- Background: Kangxi's 1692 edict had tolerated Christianity, but the Chinese Rites Controversy (papal decrees of 1704/1715 forbidding Chinese converts to honor Confucius and ancestors; legate Mezzabarba's mission, 1720-21) led Kangxi to restrict missionaries; Yongzheng banned Christian preaching in the provinces in 1724 and expelled most missionaries to Macao, while foreign trade was later confined to Canton (1757). The CED pairs this kind of policy with Tokugawa Japan's restrictions (compare C44).
- Point of view: an imperial, Confucian-orthodox text aimed at ordinary soldiers and commoners; translated by a Protestant missionary, who labels the Catholics "Romish."
