EXCERPT (verbatim):

For nearly two centuries, she [Spain] had persisted in an economic policy fatally inconsistent with her powers and resources. Although struggling under tremendous disabilities in Europe, she was still attempting, upon the slender pleas of prior discovery and papal investiture, to reserve half the world to herself. That Seville should try to supply Spanish-American markets with all the foreign merchandise they required, that Castile should undertake to absorb all the metallic riches of the western hemisphere, were stupendous blunders. One city was sufficient to conduct the early trade with Hispaniola and neighboring islands; but its success was no justification for permitting what might have been a temporary provision to become a perpetual right. As it was, restrictions and prohibitions proved to be little avail, and the system of great annual fleets and single ports of call in the end a failure. The galleon trade grew every year weaker, and seemed destined to disappear altogether.

Spain did not invent the colonial system. It had been applied earlier by the Portuguese in the East Indies. It was imitated later by the Dutch, English, and French. . . . Nor did Spain's exclusivism greatly exceed that maintained by the other colonial powers. Her distinction rests upon the fact that she had the opportunity to employ it in a vaster theatre than was given to any other nation before the nineteenth century. . . . Spain was no less judicious or disinterested than her neighbors. But, industrially bankrupt at home by reason of mistaken economic ideas, the colonial system in her case was peculiarly disastrous.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Spain's attempt to funnel all American trade through Seville in annual fleets and to keep all American silver for Castile was a "stupendous blunder" that failed, not because Spain's mercantilism was worse than other empires' but because Spain applied it on a vast scale while its own industry was too weak to supply its colonies.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: Clarence H. Haring (1885-1960), Harvard historian of Latin America (Rhodes Scholar; also wrote The Buccaneers in the West Indies, 1910, and later The Spanish Empire in America, 1947). This book was the first detailed English-language study of the Casa de Contratacion (House of Trade, founded 1503) and the flota (convoy) system, built from documents in the Archive of the Indies in Seville.
- Background a student needs: by law only Seville (later Cadiz) could trade with Spanish America; from the 1540s merchant ships had to sail in armed annual convoys (the flotas to New Spain and the galeones to Tierra Firme/Panama) that carried silver home and European goods out. "Papal investiture" = the Pope's 1493 grants (and the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas) dividing newly found lands between Spain and Portugal. "Exclusivism" = keeping foreigners out of colonial trade.
- Point of view: Haring judges Spain from an early-20th-century free-trade view ("mistaken economic ideas" = mercantilism and the drive to hoard bullion). Note that he defends Spain against the charge of being uniquely bad ("no less judicious or disinterested than her neighbors"), which makes a nice nuance question: he blames circumstances (scale, weak home industry), not Spanish character.
- Evidence elsewhere in the same book (see H42b): by the later 1600s foreigners supplied five-sixths of the goods on the Seville fleets, and smugglers traded directly with colonists.
- What later historians revised: historians such as Henry Kamen and J. H. Elliott have debated how far Spain truly "declined" in the 1600s versus how far the American colonies became more self-sufficient and kept more of their silver; much silver also left through smuggling and the Manila galleons, so official Seville registers understate the flow.
