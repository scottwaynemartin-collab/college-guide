EXCERPT (verbatim):

The rapid melting away of the population of the West Indies during the first quarter of a century of the Spanish rule was the first appearance in modern times of a phenomenon of familiar occurrence in the later history of the contact of nature peoples with a ruling race. Through the impassioned descriptions of Las Casas, which were translated into the principal languages of Europe, it is the most familiar instance of the kind; and, as a consequence, it is generally believed that the Spaniards were cruel and destructive above all other colonists. . . . That the wars of subjugation were very destructive of life is only too clear; that famine followed war to prolong its ravages is equally certain; that the average Spaniard recklessly and cruelly overworked the Indians there is no doubt.

Nevertheless, there were other and more subtle causes in operation. Diseases were imported by the whites, which were mitigated for them by some degree of acquired immunity, but which raged irresistibly through a population without that defence. Of these new diseases small-pox was one of the most destructive. In the epidemic of small-pox in 1518 the natives, Peter Martyr reports, died like sheep with the distemper. . . . When Pamfilo de Narvaez was despatched to recall Cortes, a negro on one of his ships was stricken with the disease, which was soon communicated to the Indians and raged irresistibly, sweeping off in some provinces half the population. . . . At more or less long intervals the Indian populations were swept by a pestilence from which the whites were exempt.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
The collapse of the Indigenous population after 1492 is usually blamed on Spanish cruelty, but although war, famine, and overwork killed many, Bourne argues that European diseases such as smallpox, to which Indigenous Americans had no immunity, were a deeper ("more subtle") cause.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: Edward Gaylord Bourne (1860-1908), Yale historian of Spanish America (see H43, same book). He relies on Spanish chroniclers (Peter Martyr, Motolinia, Herrera) and on 19th-century scholars (Waitz, Peschel).
- Point of view: as in H43, Bourne wants to correct the Las Casas-based image of uniquely cruel Spaniards, so he stresses disease. He does not deny cruelty ("there is no doubt"), which makes this a good passage for "identify the historian's claim and qualification."
- Dated language (left verbatim): "nature peoples" (an old translation of the German Naturvolker, a now-rejected label for societies without states or writing), "a ruling race," "the whites," "the natives," and "a negro on one of his ships." The last refers to an enslaved or free African man on Narvaez's 1520 expedition, whom Spanish chroniclers blamed for bringing smallpox to Mexico; the outbreak devastated Tenochtitlan in 1520. A one-line gloss is enough.
- Accuracy notes: Bourne's statement that the pestilence (matlazahuatl, 1545 and 1576) spared the Spanish reflects the chroniclers; modern epidemiologists still debate what those epidemics were (one theory: a native hemorrhagic fever, "cocoliztli"). Modern work (Alfred Crosby, The Columbian Exchange, 1972; later demographic studies) agrees disease was the largest single cause of decline, while stressing that war, forced labor, and famine made epidemics deadlier.
- Vocabulary: "subjugation" (conquest); "acquired immunity" (resistance built up by earlier exposure); "pestilence" (deadly epidemic).
- Pairs with H43 (same author on Spanish law), with Las Casas (in raw/), and with any image or account of smallpox in Mexico (e.g., the Florentine Codex).
