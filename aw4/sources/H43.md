EXCERPT (verbatim):

The preservation and civilization of a large proportion of the native stock on the continent is a feature of the Spanish colonial system remarkable in itself and inadequately appreciated by the average American, whose familiarity with the Spanish Indian policy rarely goes beyond the days of the conquest and of the extinction of the natives of the islands. To the prolonged efforts of the crown in behalf of its Indian vassals many a popular history gives less space than to the terrible stories of cruelties which Las Casas heaped up. . . .

The encomienda system, the genesis of which has been described in an earlier chapter, tended to degenerate into a serfdom approaching slavery and capable of great abuses; but the crown tried to prevent these evils so far as possible. In the code for the Indies prepared in 1542, commonly called the "New Laws," the future enslavement of the Indians was absolutely prohibited, and all slaves whose masters could not prove a just title were to be liberated; . . . encomenderos who had abused their Indians were to forfeit their holdings; no new encomiendas were to be granted, and existing ones were to lapse on the death of the holder.

In securing this legislation Las Casas, "the apostle of the Indians," had been pre-eminently influential, but the practical difficulties of its execution proved insuperable. . . . The Indian legislation of the Spanish kings is an impressive monument of benevolent intentions which need not fear comparison with the contemporary legislation of any European country affecting the status of the working-classes.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Bourne argues that Spanish colonial policy toward Indigenous Americans was better than its reputation: the Crown tried from the start to forbid Indian slavery and to curb encomienda abuses (as in the New Laws of 1542), and popular histories that rely on Las Casas's atrocity stories ignore these "benevolent intentions."

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: Edward Gaylord Bourne (1860-1908), professor of history at Yale and a specialist on Spanish colonization. Spain in America was volume 3 of The American Nation series, a major scholarly US history of its day.
- Point of view / purpose: Bourne is pushing back against the English-language tradition (built on Las Casas's Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies) that painted Spain as uniquely cruel, a view later called the "Black Legend" (a term popularized by the Spanish writer Julian Juderias in 1914, ten years after this book). He was writing in 1904, when the United States had just taken the Philippines and Puerto Rico from Spain; two pages later he compares Las Casas to the abolitionist William Lloyd Garrison and compares Spain's unenforced Indian laws to the poorly enforced Fifteenth Amendment (that passage, pp. 256-257, is clear but needs heavy explanation and uses the dated phrase "the negro question," so it was not chosen).
- Weakness students can spot: Bourne judges policy mostly by laws and intentions, and admits enforcement failed ("the practical difficulties of its execution proved insuperable"). His own footnote notes that Charles V repealed the ban on encomiendas in 1545, after colonists protested (and in Peru, rebelled). Labor drafts (repartimiento, and the Andean mit'a) continued forced Indigenous labor for centuries.
- Dated language (left verbatim): "civilization of ... the native stock" and "Indian vassals." Worth a one-line gloss on the exam page. (The omitted sentence's "wild Caribs" refers to Carib peoples labeled hostile, whom Spanish law allowed to be enslaved.)
- Vocabulary: "encomienda" (a grant of the labor and tribute of a group of Indigenous people to a Spanish colonist, who was supposed to Christianize and protect them); "encomendero" (holder of an encomienda); "lapse" (end and return to the Crown).
- Later historians: Lewis Hanke (The Spanish Struggle for Justice, 1949) built on Bourne's point that Spaniards themselves debated Indigenous rights; most modern historians agree the Crown's laws were real but that Spanish rule still rested on coerced labor and that epidemic disease, not only cruelty, caused most of the population collapse (see H47).
