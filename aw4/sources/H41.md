EXCERPT (verbatim):

The conclusion is inescapable that the discoveries of America and of the Cape route to the East Indies were highly important factors in the rise of modern capitalism. . . . The price revolution set in motion by American gold and silver contributed directly to the progress of capitalism. . . . The price paid for goods in the East Indies was largely determined by their value in Europe when the traders sailed, but by the time the ships returned prices had usually risen a great deal. Hence enormous windfalls were thrown in the laps of traders and manufacturers.

In England and France the vast discrepancy between prices and wages, born of the price revolution, deprived labourers of a large part of the incomes they had hitherto enjoyed, and diverted this wealth to the recipients of other distributive shares. As has been shown, rents, as well as wages, lagged behind prices; so landlords gained nothing from labour's loss. . . .

The windfalls thus received, along with gains from the East India trade, furnished the means to build up capital equipment, and the stupendous profits obtainable supplied an incentive for the feverish pursuit of capitalistic enterprise. We find, as might be expected, that during the seventeenth and latter part of the sixteenth centuries England, France, and the Low Countries were seething with such genuinely capitalistic phenomena as systematic mechanical invention, company formation, and speculation in the shares of financial and trading concerns.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
American gold and silver set off a "price revolution" in which prices rose faster than wages and rents, so merchants and manufacturers in England, France, and the Low Countries reaped windfall profits that they reinvested, making American treasure (with the East India trade) a main cause of the rise of capitalism.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: Earl J. Hamilton (1899-1989), American economic historian, then an assistant professor of economics at Duke University. He built his case from years of work in Spanish archives (the Archive of the Indies in Seville) counting treasure imports and collecting prices. His famous book, American Treasure and the Price Revolution in Spain, 1501-1650 (Harvard, 1934), is too late for this exam's rule; this 1929 article is his first statement of the "profit inflation" thesis.
- Claim + evidence a student can follow: claim = the price revolution "contributed directly to the progress of capitalism"; evidence = prices outran wages and rents in England and France (Hamilton's Table II, omitted here, puts French prices at 248 but wages at only 113 for 1576-1600, with 1451-1500 = 100), so profits swelled and were invested in "company formation" and invention.
- Vocabulary to gloss on the exam page: "price revolution" (the long inflation of c. 1500-1650); "windfalls" (unexpected gains); "distributive shares" (the parts of income that go to workers as wages, landlords as rent, and owners as profit). The omitted "profiteers ... 1916 to 1919" sentence compares the gains to those of businesses that got rich from inflation during World War I; it can be restored if the exam has room (raw lines 19747-19752).
- Point of view / purpose: an economist in the 1920s answering Max Weber and Werner Sombart (who stressed religion or a "capitalist spirit"); Hamilton argues for a material, monetary cause instead. He is Europe-centered: he treats Asia mainly as a place where European silver was spent.
- What later historians revised: John U. Nef (1937) attacked the profit-inflation thesis, arguing the wage data were thin and that industrial growth did not track prices; others argue population growth, not only silver, drove the price rise; Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giraldez (1995) stress that a huge share of American silver went on to China, so the story is global, not just European. Hamilton's own treasure figures were later revised upward for the late 1600s (Michel Morineau, 1985). These are useful for a "how might a historian challenge this?" question.
