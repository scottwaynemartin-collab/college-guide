EXCERPT (verbatim):
As we have frequently spoken of the Kingdom of Angola, it is now time to treat of it more fully, for, as has been said, though formerly ruled by a governor under the King of Congo, this governor has, since the king became a Christian, made himself absolute ruler. Having usurped all those countries over which he bare rule, and in process of time conquered some of the surrounding provinces, he is now a great and rich prince, and hardly less powerful than the King of Congo, to whom he pays [OCR: "p:iys"] tribute or not, according as he chooses.

It came to pass that after Don Juan II., of Portugal, planted the faith of Christ in the Kingdom of Congo, and the king became a Christian, the Lord of Angola was always friendly towards him, being to some extent a vassal, and their people trading together. He sent tribute every year to the King of Congo, and trade was carried on at the Port of [OCR: "o{"] Luanda between the Portuguese and the people of Angola by permission of the King of Congo. [OCR: ","] Slaves were bought and exchanged for other merchandise, all being sent to the Island of St. Thomas, the trade here being united with that of St. Thomas, ships first calling at that island, and then going on to Loanda. Trade becoming successful, they began to send vessels themselves from Lisbon to Angola, and also a governor, Paulo Diaz di Novais by name, to whom belonged this privilege on account of his ancestors having first discovered [OCR: "difcovercd"] this trade. To this Paulo Diaz, Don Sebastian, King of Portugal, conceded the power of conquering 33 leagues of country, beginning at the River Coanza, and going southward inland, where he was to take all he could gain for himself and his heirs.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Kongo's ruler (the manikongo) Nzinga a Nkuwu was baptized as João I in 1491 after Portuguese contact (1483); his son Afonso I (r. c. 1509-1542) made Catholicism a royal religion, sent sons and nobles to study in Portugal (one son, Henrique, became a bishop in 1518), and traded slaves, copper and ivory with the Portuguese, largely through the island of São Tomé ("St. Thomas").
- In his 1526 letters Afonso did not reject trade or slavery as such; he protested that Portuguese merchants were dealing directly with his subjects, that free Kongolese including nobles were being kidnapped and sold, and that this was undermining royal authority. He set up inspectors to check that captives offered for sale had been legally enslaved.
- The substitute passage reflects the 1570s-1580s: Ndongo (called "Angola" after its ruler's title, ngola) had broken away from Kongo; Paulo Dias de Novais received a royal charter in 1571 and founded Luanda in 1576, beginning the Portuguese colony of Angola. Later in the 1600s Queen Njinga (Ana Nzinga) of Ndongo and Matamba resisted the Portuguese (Topic 4.6).
- Sourcing: Pigafetta wrote in Rome from Duarte Lopes's interviews; the work promotes Catholic missions in Kongo, and its chronology for earlier reigns is not always reliable.
