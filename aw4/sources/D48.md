EXCERPT (verbatim) - Hackett 1911, pp. 98-99:
In the first place, as noted above, the efforts of the Spaniards to suppress not only the religious beliefs but also the ancient habits and customs of the Indians in other respects, and to make them conform to European methods of living, created friction between the two races. The Indians, however, continued well grounded in their native religious beliefs and practices, and the Spaniards found it practically impossible to suppress them either by the number of missionaries brought in, or by the severity of the punishments inflicted. In 1675 these "superstitious practices" reached such alarming extremes that Governor Trevino determined to stamp them out for good. Having captured forty-seven medicine men, who were alleged to be guilty of sorcery and withcraft [sic], he hanged three of them, as a warning to future soothsayers, and inflicted severe punishment upon the others, among whom was a certain native named Pope. [...] On being released from captivity in 1675, he returned to his pueblo, smarting under the punishment he had received, and full of resentment for the Spaniards. He at once began making preparations for a general revolt [...]. Other miscellaneous influences, such as the offering of prizes to the warriors by the chiefs; the belief that the Spaniards could all be driven out of the country; and the almost universal desire to return to their ancient customs, all operated to make the revolt of the Indians general.

(Editorial marks: [...] = omitted text (a correction of an earlier historian, and Popé's move to Taos and his claims of spirit guidance). Hackett's footnote numbers removed; "withcraft" is a misprint in the 1911 text.)

SUPPLEMENTARY EXCERPT (verbatim) - Hackett 1916, Pueblo people's stated reasons as recorded by the Spaniards in December 1681:
[At Isleta:] They denied all responsibility for these things, and stated that the Indian chiefs of the revolt and all the Indians of Taos and Picuris [OCR: "Picurfs"], and of the Tewa nation, had come down after the Spaniards [OCR: "S])aniards"] had been [OCR: "Ixh'U"] driven out, and, after burning the churches, images, and instruments of divine cult, had ordered them to return to their manner of living as in the days of their paganism before the Spaniards [OCR: "Si)aniards"] and religious had interfered. These chiefs, the Isleta Indians stated, they had been forced to obey.

[Near Cochiti:] They stated that they had revolted on account of the persecution of Francisco Xavier, Luis de Quintana, and Diego Lopez Sambrano. Vexed at this Mendoza [OCR: "^lendoza"] ordered all of the Spaniards as well as the apostates to assemble, and with Joseph de Arbizu and Diego Lucero acting as interpreters, asked the Indians to state with exactness the causes for their actions. They then stated that they had revolted from fear and because Francisco Xavier had burned their estufas [OCR: "est u fas"] for having practiced witchcraft in them. Out of revenge for this they had burned the churches of the Spaniards.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- The Pueblo Revolt began 10 August 1680, coordinated among many pueblos (Tewa, Tiwa, Keres, Tano, Jemez, Hopi and others) by Popé of San Juan (Ohkay Owingeh) and other leaders using knotted cords to count the days. About 400 Spanish colonists and 21 Franciscan friars (of roughly 32-33 in the province) were killed; survivors under Governor Antonio de Otermín retreated to El Paso. Spain did not reconquer New Mexico until Diego de Vargas's campaigns of 1692-1696.
- "Religious" (as a noun) = members of religious orders, i.e., Franciscan friars. "Estufas" = the Spaniards' word for kivas, underground ceremonial chambers. "Apostates" = baptized Pueblos who had abandoned Christianity. Francisco Xavier was a Spanish official (maestro de campo/secretary) blamed for persecutions; Trevino = Governor Juan Francisco Treviño (1675 crackdown).
- Other grievances discussed by historians include forced labor and tribute under the encomienda and repartimiento, drought and famine in the 1660s-1670s, and Apache raids.
- Sourcing caution: the supplementary statements come from Pueblo people being questioned by the Spanish army that had just reoccupied their towns (Otermín's 1681 expedition), so they were under pressure; Hackett is paraphrasing Spanish transcripts.
