EXCERPT (verbatim):

The Viceroy was more than a mere conqueror: in a letter to King Manoel he formulated a system of government based on definite strategic and commercial principles. Its fundamental idea is the importance of sea-power; for the "blue water-school" of naval strategy never had a more convinced champion than Almeida. "Avoid the annexation of territory," he writes, "build no more fortresses than may be absolutely necessary to protect your factories from a sudden raid: we can spare no men from the navy." . . .

Almeida regarded maritime power solely as a means to commercial supremacy in the East. Missionary and crusading ideals were for the moment in abeyance: the whole purpose of his policy was to secure for King Manoel a monopoly in the exportation of Indian and East African products to Europe. This merchandise, of which the letter gives a somewhat miscellaneous catalogue—including pepper, slave-girls, silk—had hitherto reached Europe through the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea, whence it passed by caravan to the Italian and Levantine ships awaiting it in various Mediterranean ports. In Indian waters, the carrying trade was exercised almost exclusively by Muhammadans—Arab and Persian, Turk and Egyptian. Almeida's plan was to drive these infidels from the seas, to put Portuguese traders in their place, and to divert the Indian export-trade to the Cape route.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Jayne argues that the first Portuguese viceroy, Almeida, built Portugal's Indian Ocean power on control of the sea rather than land: he wanted few forts and no territory, using naval force to drive Muslim merchants off the main routes and win a royal monopoly on shipping Asian goods to Europe by the Cape route.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: K. G. Jayne, a British author; the book (Methuen, London, 1910) is a narrative history told through biographies of Portuguese leaders, drawing on Portuguese chronicles (Correa, Barros) and Albuquerque's letters. It is not an academic monograph, but its quotations and claims follow the chronicles closely.
- Historical background: Francisco de Almeida was viceroy of Portuguese India 1505-1509; his "blue water" (navy-first) policy was replaced by Afonso de Albuquerque (governor 1509-1515), who seized key ports (Goa 1510, Malacca 1511, Hormuz 1515) as fortified bases. Either way, the Portuguese held coastal forts and "factories" (trading posts), not large inland territories; they enforced their control through naval patrols and the cartaz, a paid pass that Asian ships had to carry or risk seizure (see H45b and H45c on safe-conducts and licences).
- Point of view: Jayne is sympathetic to Portuguese "heroes" yet critical of their violence elsewhere in the book (his preface speaks of da Gama's "ignorant ferocity"). Phrases like "drive these infidels from the seas" voice the crusading outlook of Almeida and King Manoel, not Jayne's own judgment; worth a gloss.
- Vocabulary: "annexation of territory" (taking land to rule directly); "factories" (trading posts, not manufacturing plants); "carrying trade" (shipping other people's goods for profit); "Levantine" (from the eastern Mediterranean); "Cape route" (around the Cape of Good Hope).
- Later historians: K. N. Chaudhuri, Sanjay Subrahmanyam, and others stress that the Portuguese never controlled most Asian trade; Gujarati, Arab, Malay, and Chinese merchants kept trading, often by buying passes or avoiding Portuguese ports, and the Red Sea spice route revived by mid-century. That matches the CED's KC-4.3.II.A.iii (Indian Ocean networks continued to flourish).
