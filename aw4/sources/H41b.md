EXCERPT (verbatim):

It is significant that Portugal, Holland, England, and France—the countries that received a large percentage of American gold and silver—monopolised the East India trade. Was there a causal connection between treasure and trade? . . . For more than two thousand years the East has proved a necropolis for European gold and silver. The seventeenth-century pamphleteers who in tract after tract denounced the English East India Company for draining away the country's treasure were not mistaken as to the facts. European products were carried to the East, but silver was the commodity that could be exchanged for Oriental goods on the most advantageous terms. So treasure flowed from Portugal, Holland, England, and France to the Orient in exchange for the eagerly sought spices and luxury goods of that region. Notwithstanding the enormous profits obtained in the East India trade, the passage around the Cape of Good Hope might have been rendered nugatory by a dearth of specie but for the vast streams of Mexican and Peruvian silver flowing into Europe. The voyage of Columbus was an imperative supplement to that of da Gama.

Editorial notes: The ellipsis omits three sentences. The first says the Cape route would have boosted trade anyway but that the Ottoman barrier was not the only obstacle; the next two attribute Asia's absorption of silver to an "inexplicable" Asian "penchant for hoarding treasure" (a stereotype; see notes). Footnote numbers removed. The space before the question mark was closed up. No words changed. "Monopolised" is Hamilton's British-style spelling (as printed in Economica).

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Europeans could buy Asian spices and luxury goods mainly with silver, so the Cape route to Asia would have been useless without the American silver that flowed into Europe; Columbus's voyage made da Gama's pay off.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: see H41 (Earl J. Hamilton, American economic historian at Duke, writing from Spanish archival data).
- Vocabulary to gloss: "necropolis" (city of the dead: silver that went East never came back); "nugatory" (worthless); "dearth of specie" (shortage of coined money); "the Orient"/"Oriental" (older Western terms for Asia, now avoided).
- Dated framing (left in the source, not in the excerpt): the omitted sentence blames Asian "hoarding." Modern historians reject that explanation. They point instead to China's huge demand for silver after the Ming government began collecting taxes in silver (the "Single Whip" reforms of the later 1500s), and to silver being worth more in China than in Europe, which made shipping it there profitable.
- What Hamilton leaves out: he describes silver reaching Asia only through Europe. From 1571 the Spanish Manila galleons also carried Mexican and Peruvian silver straight across the Pacific from Acapulco to Manila, where Chinese merchants exchanged silk and porcelain for it. That is a good "what is missing from this argument?" prompt (see Flynn and Giraldez, 1995).
- Hamilton's footnote supports the "pamphleteers" point: he notes complaints against the English East India Company for draining silver began about 1640, when imports of American treasure into Europe fell sharply.
- Pairs with Thomas Mun's England's Treasure by Forraign Trade (already in raw/ as a primary source), which defends the East India Company's export of silver.
