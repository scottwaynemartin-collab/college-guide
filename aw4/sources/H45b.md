EXCERPT (verbatim):

It should be noted that the chartered companies and associations of merchant adventurers which opened the trade-routes of the world to English commerce in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries had few forerunners in Portugal. The sole right of sending merchant-ships to India was vested in the King, who could use the entire resources of the state to further his commercial interests. Licence to trade was only granted by way of pension and reward, or in return for a substantial fee, or else on a profit-sharing basis. The same system was applied to the natives of India wherever Albuquerque could enforce it.

He was urged by the King to boycott Musalman traders, but pointed out that, although he favoured Hindus and native Christians, the Muhammadans alone possessed sufficient capital to do business on a large scale, and almost monopolized the carrying-trade. He went on to explain that the commercial interests of every community were bound inextricably together. Hindu Rajas would not lightly forego the dues paid by Musalman merchants, and even the Hindu capitalists of Gujarat invariably entrusted their cargo-fleets to Musalman crews, though Gujarat was the one part of north-western India (destes partes) in which the leading ship-owners did not profess Islam.

ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE:
Unlike the later English and Dutch companies, Portugal's Asian trade was a royal monopoly run by licences, but even Albuquerque admitted Portugal could not do without Muslim (and Gujarati) merchants, because they had the capital and ships that made Indian Ocean trade work.

CONTEXT NOTES:
- Author: see H45 (K. G. Jayne, British narrative historian, 1910, drawing on Albuquerque's letters, the "Cartas").
- Why it is useful: one passage covers two CED ideas: (1) state-run monopoly versus private joint-stock companies (contrast with H46/H46b on the Dutch East India Company); (2) continuity: Asian merchant networks stayed central even under Portuguese pressure (KC-4.3.II.A.iii; CED examples include Gujaratis).
- "Licence to trade ... applied to the natives of India" refers to the Portuguese pass system (cartaz): Asian ships had to buy a Portuguese licence or risk capture.
- Vocabulary: "Musalman"/"Muhammadans" (older English terms for Muslims); "Hindu Rajas" (Hindu rulers of coastal states); "dues" (port fees and customs); "destes partes" (Portuguese, "of these parts," quoted from Albuquerque's letter); "chartered companies" (like the English East India Company, 1600).
- Point of view: Jayne generally admires Albuquerque's statesmanship ("financier, diplomatist, administrator").
