EXCERPT (verbatim):
[...] after the taking of Ceuta he always kept ships well armed against the Infidel, both for war, and because he had also a wish to know the land that lay beyond the isles of Canary and that Cape called Bojador [...]. And this was the first reason of his action.

The second reason was that if there chanced to be in those lands some population of Christians, or some havens, into which it would be possible to sail without peril, many kinds of merchandise might be brought to this realm, which would find a ready market, and reasonably so, because no other people of these parts traded with them, nor yet people of any other that were known; and also the products of this realm might be taken there, which traffic would bring great profit to our countrymen.

The third reason was that, as it was said that the power of the Moors in that land of Africa was very much greater than was commonly supposed, and that there were no Christians among them, nor any other race of men; and because every wise man is obliged by natural prudence to wish for a knowledge of the power of his enemy; therefore the said Lord Infant exerted himself to cause this to be fully discovered, and to make it known determinately how far the power of those infidels extended.

The fourth reason was because during the one and thirty years that he had warred against the Moors, he had never found a Christian king, nor a lord outside this land, who for the love of our Lord Jesus Christ would aid him in the said war. Therefore he sought to know if there were in those parts any Christian princes, in whom the charity and the love of Christ was so ingrained that they would aid him against those enemies of the faith.

The fifth reason was his great desire to make increase in the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ and to bring to him all the souls that should be saved [...].

The omitted middle of the first reason (same paragraph, restorable verbatim if needed) says no one knew "the nature of the land beyond that Cape," and that Henry acted because otherwise "no mariners or merchants would ever dare to attempt it--(for it is clear that none of them ever trouble themselves to sail to a place where there is not a sure and certain hope of profit)--and seeing also that no other prince took any pains in this matter, he sent out his own ships against those parts, to have manifest certainty of them all. And to this he was stirred up by his zeal for the service of God and of the King Edward his Lord and brother, who then reigned." 

CONTEXT NOTES:
- "The Lord Infant" = Prince Henry of Portugal ("Henry the Navigator," 1394-1460), third son of King John I; "King Edward" = King Duarte (r. 1433-1438), Henry's brother. Ceuta, in Morocco, was captured by Portugal in 1415. Cape Bojador (on the Western Sahara coast) was first passed by Henry's captain Gil Eanes in 1434.
- Zurara was the official royal chronicler, writing at the command of King Afonso V; the chronicle is openly a celebration of Henry, so its account of his motives is a court-sponsored, idealized one. Right after the five reasons Zurara adds a "sixth" reason: Henry's horoscope.
- "Moors" = Muslims of North Africa; "infidels" = Muslims. The hope of finding a distant Christian ally reflects the Prester John legend (often linked to Christian Ethiopia).
- What happened next: Portuguese voyages reached the Senegal and Cape Verde by the 1440s; from 1441 they brought captive Africans to Portugal, which the chronicle later presents as conversion (the fifth reason continues with Zurara saying he saw "the first captives" and their descendants become Christians). This leads toward the Atlantic slave trade, trading forts such as Elmina (1482), and Vasco da Gama's voyage to India (1497-99).
- Misreading risk: "Guinea" here means West Africa generally, not the modern country; "havens" = harbors.
