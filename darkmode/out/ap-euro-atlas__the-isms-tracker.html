Absolutism

Absolutism is monarchy in which one ruler makes law, sets taxes, and commands the army without needing anyone else's consent.
Absolutists argued that authority has to sit in one place or it does not work at all. A single sovereign — answerable to God rather than to nobles, courts, or assemblies — was supposed to end the religious wars and noble revolts of the previous century. Subjects received order and protection; they did not receive a say.
First appears
Unit 3 · 1660s–1680s — Louis XIV governs France with no chief minister and never calls the Estates-General, and Bossuet supplies the divine-right theology that makes resisting him a sin as well as a crime.
Who held it
Ruling dynasties (Bourbon, Hohenzollern, Habsburg, Romanov), court clergy like Bossuet, jurists like Jean Bodin, and the service nobles and bureaucrats whose careers ran through the crown rather than through their own estates.
Defined against
Constitutionalism — the English and Dutch model in which a representative body must consent to taxes and laws.
How it changes across the course
Unit 3
c. 1648–1715
Absolutism at full strength. Louis XIV moves the court to Versailles and turns nobles into courtiers, keeps a standing army, and revokes the Edict of Nantes; Peter the Great's Table of Ranks makes noble standing depend on state service. In the east it runs on a bargain: the monarch gets political obedience and the nobles keep their serfs.

Enlightened absolutism. Frederick II and Joseph II keep every scrap of absolute power but aim it at Enlightenment reforms — legal codes, religious toleration, state schooling, an end to judicial torture. Reform is issued downward from the throne, which is exactly why the throne gives up nothing.

The mistake students make

Students hear 'absolute' and picture a totalitarian state. Louis XIV had no radio, no political police, and no interest in what a peasant believed — he wanted taxes, soldiers, and obedience, and he bargained constantly with provincial courts, venal officeholders, and the Church. Absolutism claimed unlimited legal authority; it never had unlimited reach.

What the exam tests

Comparison. Unit 3 asks you to compare absolutism with constitutionalism, and Unit 4 asks what was actually 'enlightened' about enlightened absolutism. The strongest answers say plainly that enlightened monarchs reformed law and religion while surrendering none of their power.

Conservatism

Conservatism is the belief that a society's inherited institutions — monarchy, church, aristocracy, custom — carry the accumulated wisdom of centuries and should be changed slowly, if at all.
Conservatives start from a claim about people: human nature is flawed and not perfectible, so no committee of reasonable men can redesign a society from scratch without wrecking it. Institutions that have lasted have lasted for a reason, and change should grow out of what already exists rather than replace it. Monarchy, the established church, and landed aristocracy are worth keeping because they hold everything else steady.
First appears
Unit 5 · 1790 — Edmund Burke publishes Reflections on the Revolution in France, arguing that the French had torn up centuries of accumulated wisdom for an abstract theory and would end under army rule.
Who held it
Landed aristocrats, established churches both Catholic and Protestant, monarchs and their diplomats, army officers, and — later in the century — peasants and small property holders who feared the cities more than they feared the king.
Defined against
Liberalism, and behind it the revolutionary claim that a society can be reasoned out and rebuilt from first principles.
How it changes across the course
Unit 5
c. 1790–1815
Born as an argument and then written into a peace treaty. Burke supplies the theory in 1790; at Vienna the victors turn it into policy through legitimacy (put the old dynasties back), compensation, buffer states, and the Holy Alliance's pledge to help one another put down revolutions.

Defensive and openly repressive. De Maistre pushes the position past preserving what exists to restoring what revolution destroyed — which is what 'reactionary' means.

The mistake students make

Students read 'conservative' as 'right-wing dictator' and file Metternich next to Mussolini. Nineteenth-century conservatism defended kings, bishops, and aristocratic land — the exact institutions fascists were willing to shove aside for a mass party and a charismatic leader. Burke's whole argument was against remaking society; fascism's whole argument was for remaking it.

What the exam tests

Causation and continuity/change. Unit 6 asks why conservative governments suppressed movements for change after 1815; Unit 7 (KC-3.4.II.B) asks how a new generation of conservative leaders used popular nationalism to create or strengthen the state. That switch — from fearing nationalism to riding it — is the change the essay is built on.

Liberalism

Liberalism is the belief that government exists to protect individual rights, must be limited by a constitution and the rule of law, and should answer to elected representatives.
Liberals held that people hold rights before any government exists, and that the state's only job is to protect those rights and then stay out of the way. They wanted written constitutions, equality before the law, free markets, careers open to talent, and legislatures that could refuse a king's taxes. What they mostly did not want, at least at first, was democracy — the vote was for men with enough property to have judgment and a stake.
First appears
Unit 5 · 1789–1791 — The moderate phase of the French Revolution turns Enlightenment theory into law: the August Decrees abolish privilege, the Declaration of the Rights of Man states the principles, and the Constitution of 1791 builds a constitutional monarchy with a taxpaying franchise.
Who held it
The bourgeoisie — merchants, manufacturers, bankers, lawyers, doctors, officeholders — plus reform-minded nobles and professionals. Strongest in Britain, France, Belgium, and the German commercial cities; weakest wherever there was no large middle class.
Defined against
Conservatism above it (kings, bishops, hereditary privilege) and, increasingly after 1848, socialism below it.
How it changes across the course
Unit 5
c. 1789–1815
Liberalism arrives as a program rather than a party: constitution, legal equality, careers open to talent, and a vote tied to tax payment. The 1791 constitution's split between 'active' citizens who could vote and 'passive' citizens who could not — including every woman — shows how quickly a property line got drawn through the word equality.
Marquis de LafayetteEmmanuel-Joseph SieyèsThomas PaineDeclaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789)Constitution of 1791Napoleon's Civil Code guarantees legal equality and property rights (1804)
Unit 6
c. 1815–1914
First, classical liberalism at its purest: laissez-faire economics, free trade, Bentham's greatest-happiness test in place of tradition, and a franchise still fenced by property — the 1832 Reform Act gave seats to industrial cities and the vote to the middle class while deliberately leaving workers out. Then liberalism changes its mind: KC-3.3.II.A says it 'shifted from laissez-faire to interventionist economic and social policies,' and the same movement that fought factory regulation ends up passing public health acts, compulsory schooling, and social insurance.

Liberals get much of what they wanted — legal codes, parliaments, free trade — delivered by governments they did not control.

The mistake students make

This is the biggest vocabulary trap in the course. Nineteenth-century liberalism is not modern American liberalism — it meant free markets, low tariffs, a government that stayed out of the economy, and suffrage restricted by property. A liberal in 1830 would have opposed most of what Americans now call liberal policy. Second trap: liberal does not mean democratic. The Constitution of 1791 and the 1832 Reform Act were liberal victories that deliberately kept most men from voting.

What the exam tests

Continuity and change. Unit 6 Learning Objective J is literally 'explain how and why Liberalism shifted from laissez-faire to interventionist economic and social policies.' If a prompt gives you documents from 1830 and 1900, the expected argument is that the label stayed the same while the policy reversed — and that industrial slums are why.

Nationalism

Nationalism is the belief that people who share a language, history, and culture form a nation, that the nation deserves its own state, and that it has the first claim on a person's loyalty.
Nationalists argue that the natural political unit is not a dynasty's inherited lands but a people. Because a nation is defined by culture, proving one exists means collecting its songs, standardizing its language, and writing its history — which is why dictionaries and fairy-tale collections counted as political work. Accept that argument and two conclusions follow: a state ruling several nations has no right to exist, and a nation split among several states has a claim to be joined.
First appears
Unit 5 · 1792–1815 — The levée en masse turns war into a national project, and French occupation teaches Germans, Spaniards, and Italians to think of themselves as Germans, Spaniards, and Italians — nationalism exported by accident, on the points of French bayonets.
Who held it
Early on, students, professors, journalists, lawyers, and poets — educated men without power. Later, mass parties, army officers, schoolteachers, and eventually whole electorates, once compulsory schooling and cheap newspapers had done their work.
Defined against
Dynastic legitimacy — the Vienna settlement's rule that borders belong to ruling families, not to peoples.
How it changes across the course
Unit 5
c. 1792–1815
Nationalism appears first as a weapon against Napoleon. Fichte lectures occupied Berlin that Germans are one people bound by one language, Herder argues every people has its own spirit expressed in its folk culture, Spanish guerrillas bleed the French army, and Russia burns its own countryside rather than feed the invader.

The mistake students make

Students treat nationalism as one thing with one moral value. Before 1848 it is a liberating force allied with liberalism — Greeks, Poles, and Italians trying to get out from under empires, and illegal nearly everywhere. After 1871 the same word covers chauvinism, colonial competition, Pan-Slavism, and racial exclusion. An essay that says 'nationalism caused World War I' without saying which nationalism has skipped the entire argument.

What the exam tests

Causation and continuity/change. Unit 7 Learning Objective B asks how the development and spread of nationalism affected Europe from 1815 to 1914, and KC-3.3.I.F hands you the five categories to organize the answer: romantic idealism, liberal reform, political unification, racialism with anti-Semitism, and chauvinism.

Socialism

Socialism is the belief that a society's wealth and productive property should be owned or directed collectively, so that the people who create the wealth share in it.
Socialists began from a fact liberals did not deny: industrial workers produced enormous value and received subsistence wages. Their answer was that factories, land, and capital should not be private property at all, or at least should be run for everyone's benefit rather than for an owner's profit. What separated socialists from one another was method — build model communities, win elections, organize unions, or overthrow the state.
First appears
Unit 6 · 1820s–1840s — Owen, Saint-Simon, and Fourier design cooperative communities, technocratic plans, and model factory villages meant to prove by demonstration that a fairer system would actually work.
Who held it
At first middle-class theorists and a few reforming factory owners; after about 1860, industrial workers themselves, organized through mutual aid societies, trade unions, and mass parties — strongest in Germany, France, and Britain.
Defined against
Laissez-faire liberalism, and specifically its claim that a wage contract freely signed is therefore a fair one.
How it changes across the course
Unit 6
c. 1820–1900
Two phases inside one unit. Utopian socialists try persuasion and demonstration projects; then Marx and Engels supply a systematic critique of capitalism, and by 1900 socialism exists as mass parties with dues, branches, and newspapers — the SPD, the British Labour Party, the Russian Social Democrats — fighting elections instead of building phalansteries.

Unit 7
c. 1890–1914
The movement splits over how socialism arrives. Bernstein argues that Marx's predicted collapse is not happening and socialists should simply win elections; Luxemburg answers that reform can never produce socialism; Sorel abandons parliaments altogether for the general strike, arguing that a myth that moves people beats an analysis that is correct.

Unit 8
c. 1917–1939
The split becomes permanent and hostile. After 1917 the word covers both the Soviet state and the parliamentary parties that refused to follow it, and communists and social democrats spend the interwar years fighting each other as often as they fight the right. The Popular Fronts of 1936 in France and Spain briefly reunite them and then fall apart.

In the West, socialism becomes the welfare state and is accepted by conservative parties too: nationalized industry, public health, pensions, public housing. In the East it means one-party rule and central planning — and in 1980 Polish shipyard workers organize a free union against a workers' state. After 1989 the eastern version collapses and the western version is put on a budget.

The mistake students make

Students use socialism, communism, and Marxism as synonyms. In this course socialism is the umbrella term: it covers Owen's cooperative villages, Bernstein's election-winning SPD, and Britain's National Health Service as well as Lenin's party. Most European socialists in 1900 wanted the vote, shorter hours, and factory inspection — not a revolution — and after 1917 they were the communists' most hated rivals.

What the exam tests

Causation. KC-3.3.I.D says socialism 'evolved from a utopian to a Marxist scientific critique of capitalism,' so the expected essay traces that evolution and explains why it happened: industrialization manufactured a proletariat, and the demonstration communities changed nothing.

Marxism

Marxism is Karl Marx's claim that history is driven by struggle between the class that owns the means of production and the class that does the work, and that capitalism must eventually produce the workers who overthrow it.
Marx argued that a society's politics, law, and religion grow out of how it produces things and who owns the machinery. Capitalism concentrates wealth in fewer hands and misery in more, so it manufactures the very class — the proletariat — with both the numbers and the reason to end it. He presented this not as a preference but as a law of historical development, which is why he called it scientific socialism and why the CED calls his analysis deterministic.
First appears
Unit 6 · 1848 — Marx and Engels publish The Communist Manifesto weeks before the revolutions of 1848; Marx then spends twenty years on the economic analysis that appears as Das Kapital in 1867.
Who held it
Party intellectuals, union organizers, and skilled industrial workers in Germany, Russia, and France — almost nowhere a majority, but disciplined, literate, and well organized.
Defined against
Utopian socialism, which Marx dismissed as wishful, and anarchism, which refused even a temporary workers' state.
How it changes across the course
Unit 6
c. 1848–1900
Theory plus evidence. Engels documents Manchester's slums street by street and Marx supplies the system that claims to explain them; by the end of the century Marxism is the official doctrine of the largest workers' party in Europe, and Zetkin is organizing working women inside it.
Karl MarxFriedrich EngelsClara ZetkinThe Communist Manifesto (1848)The Condition of the Working Class in EnglandDas Kapital (1867)
Unit 7
c. 1890–1916
Marxism argues with itself and then explains away its own failed prediction. Bernstein points out that workers are getting better off, Luxemburg answers that reform is a trap, and Lenin proposes in 1916 that imperialism is capitalism's final stage — empire let the system export its crisis, which is why the collapse had not arrived on schedule.

Unit 8

Marxism–Leninism: a small vanguard party seizes power on the working class's behalf in a mostly peasant country — precisely where Marx said revolution should not come first. Under Stalin it becomes the official doctrine of a state and is used to justify collectivization, the Five-Year Plans, and the purges.

State doctrine across half of Europe — taught in schools, enforced by the Warsaw Pact, and increasingly disbelieved. Every attempt to reform it from inside is stopped by troops, and Havel argues that the system survives only because ordinary people keep going through the motions. In 1989 enough of them stop.

The mistake students make

Students write that Marx 'invented communism' or 'created the Soviet Union.' Marx died in 1883, thirty-four years before the Bolshevik Revolution, and he expected revolution in the most industrialized countries — Britain or Germany — not in agrarian Russia. Lenin's addition, that a small disciplined party can act for a working class that barely exists yet, is a change to Marx, not a footnote to him.

What the exam tests

Sourcing and causation. KC-3.6.II.C calls Marxism 'a systematic critique of capitalism and a deterministic analysis of society and historical evolution.' Know what deterministic means here — Marx claimed to have found laws, not opinions — because that claim is what a document-based question will ask you to weigh.

Romanticism

Romanticism is the movement of roughly 1780–1850 that treated feeling, imagination, nature, and the individual's inner life as better guides to truth than reason and rules.
Romantics answered the Enlightenment on its own ground: if reason is the only way of knowing, why does it fail to explain love, terror, faith, or the shock of standing under a mountain? They preferred the particular to the universal — this valley, this people's songs, this person's grief — and treated the artist's inner experience as the real subject of the work. Their keyword is the sublime: awe mixed with fear, valued precisely because it cannot be measured.
First appears
Unit 5 · c. 1790–1820 — Rousseau's insistence that feeling matters as much as reason opens the door; Wordsworth calls poetry the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings, and Friedrich paints tiny human figures in front of enormous fog and mountains.
Who held it
Poets, painters, composers, and folklorists, mostly young and mostly middle class, strongest in Germany and Britain — writing for the same expanding reading public that was buying novels and periodicals.
Defined against
Enlightenment rationality, and its artistic partner Neoclassicism with its clean lines, balance, and classical subjects.
How it changes across the course
Unit 4
c. 1740–1789
Not yet a movement, but the taste is already turning inside the Enlightenment. Rousseau argues that civilization corrupts natural goodness and that feeling makes a person good; Goethe's Werther sets off a continent-wide craze for private emotion; and Pietist and Methodist revivals put intense personal feeling back into religion while the philosophes are still arguing about reason.

Unit 5
c. 1790–1830
The full movement. Wordsworth writes about shepherds and beggars in ordinary speech, Friedrich paints the sublime, Beethoven breaks classical proportion — and then scratches Napoleon's name off the Eroica when he crowns himself emperor. Herder turns collected folk songs into an argument that each people has its own spirit, which is where Romanticism and nationalism fuse.

Romanticism becomes political — and then gets replaced. Delacroix paints an uprising as something close to a sacred event, the Grimms' fairy tales serve as evidence that a German nation already exists in the countryside, and Wagner builds vast operas out of Germanic myth. The mistake students make

Students read 'Romantic' as 'about love.' It is about intensity of feeling of any kind — awe, terror, grief, patriotic fury — and its signature idea is the sublime, not romance. The more useful point is that Romanticism is what gave nationalism its emotional content: folk songs, legends, and landscape painting were arguments that a people exists and deserves a state.

What the exam tests

Causation. Unit 5's key concept says Romanticism 'emerged as a challenge' to Enlightenment rationality, so any prompt expects you to name what it was reacting against. Expect painting stimuli — Friedrich, Delacroix, Goya — and be ready to explain the artist's purpose rather than describe the picture.

Realism

Realism is the mid-19th-century turn toward showing ordinary people and ordinary work exactly as they were, without heroism, flattery, or moral uplift.
Realists held that art should report the world rather than improve it. A road mender or a woman gathering leftover grain deserved a canvas the size normally reserved for kings and saints, and a novel should follow a mining town rather than a legend. Behind it sat materialism — the assumption that wages, machines, bodies, and environment explain human life better than spirit or destiny do.
First appears
Unit 7 · 1848–1850 — The revolutions fail and artists stop painting dreams. Courbet exhibits The Stone Breakers in 1849, and the CED dates Europe's turn to 'a realist and materialist worldview' from exactly that failure.
Who held it
Painters and novelists working for a mass reading public rather than for courts or academies, plus the dealers, publishers, and critics who backed them — strongest in France, Britain, and Russia.
Defined against
Romanticism, and specifically its taste for legend, exotic settings, and heroic feeling.
How it changes across the course
Unit 7
c. 1848–1890
Realism dominates and then hardens into naturalism. Courbet and Millet paint labor at monumental scale, Dickens serializes industrial poverty to an audience of millions, and Zola researches his novels like a reporter and treats heredity and environment as forces that decide a life. Critics read Millet's bent peasant women as a political threat, which shows how directly the style was tied to the fear left over from 1848.

Realism loses the avant-garde and gains the dictators. Serious art moves toward abstraction and the interior life, while plain reporting survives as protest — Remarque's trench novel sold in the millions and was burned by the Nazis in 1933. Fascist and Soviet states then demanded heroic, easily read representational art, while modernists like Picasso answered atrocity with Guernica rather than with reportage.

The mistake students make

Students use 'realistic' to mean 'looks like a photograph' and then label any highly finished painting realist. Realism is defined by subject and attitude, not by polish: a glossy academic canvas of Napoleon is not realist, and a rough, unfinished-looking picture of road menders is. The scandal over Courbet was never that his work looked real — it was that he gave laborers the scale and seriousness reserved for history painting.

What the exam tests

Continuity and change. KC-3.6 frames the whole 1815–1914 period as a tension 'between objectivity and scientific realism on one hand, and subjectivity and individual expression on the other.' A stimulus question will hand you an image and ask which side of that tension it sits on, and why.

Imperialism

Imperialism is one state taking political and economic control of territory and people outside its own borders.
Imperialists claimed their conquests were necessary, profitable, and good for the conquered: markets and raw materials for the home economy, coaling stations and canals for the navy, and a duty to spread religion, law, and medicine. Critics answered at the time that empire cost taxpayers more than it earned, that it enriched a narrow class of investors, and that the civilizing language was a cover for force.
First appears
Unit 5 · 17th–18th centuries — Empire begins in this course as trade: the Atlantic system, chartered joint-stock companies with their own armies, and mercantilist rules meant to keep colonial commerce inside one nation's ships.
Who held it
Trading companies and their shareholders, then — after 1870 — governments, naval and army officers, missionaries, settlers, and organized pressure groups like the Pan-German League that pushed cabinets to expand rather than merely following them.
Defined against
Anti-imperialism, argued both morally (empire is theft and cruelty) and economically (it enriches investors while taxpayers fund the fleets).
How it changes across the course
Unit 5
c. 1650–1815
Empire as commerce. Chartered companies run forts, fleets, and eventually governments — Clive's victory at Plassey turns the British East India Company into the ruler of Bengal — while the Atlantic system moves manufactured goods, enslaved people, and plantation crops in a three-cornered circuit. Colonial wars are simply European rivalry fought somewhere warmer.

The mistake students make

Students blur the old empires with the New Imperialism and tell 1492 and 1884 as one story. The differences are method and speed: before 1800 Europeans mostly traded from coastal enclaves and left local rulers in place; after 1870 European states annexed and administered the interior directly, and did it in about thirty years. The second common error is assuming empire went unchallenged at home — Hobson, the Congo Reform Association, and anti-imperialist MPs were arguing loudly the entire time.

What the exam tests

Causation. KC-3.5.I gives you the motives to name: national rivalry and strategic concerns, the search for raw materials and markets, and claims of cultural and racial superiority. Strong essays add KC-3.5.II's enabling technologies, because motive alone cannot explain why the partition happened after 1870 instead of in 1750.

Social Darwinism

Social Darwinism is the claim that Darwin's biology applies to human societies — that the nations, classes, and races that dominate do so because they are naturally fitter.
Its supporters lifted 'survival of the fittest' out of biology and applied it to competition among people: helping the weak interferes with a natural sorting process, and a nation that conquers another has proved its own vitality. Darwin made no such argument. The CED's own wording is that his scientific account of biological change supplied a justification for racialist theories 'inadvertently,' and the slogan itself was coined by Herbert Spencer, not by Darwin.
First appears
Unit 7 · 1859–1883 — Darwin publishes On the Origin of Species in 1859; Spencer coins 'survival of the fittest' in 1864; Galton names eugenics in 1883 and proposes improving a population by managing who has children.
Who held it
Imperial administrators and their publicists, opponents of factory laws and poor relief, racial theorists, and eugenics campaigners drawn from across the political spectrum — including reformers who thought of themselves as progressive.
Defined against
The Enlightenment claim of universal human equality, and the reform politics built on top of it.
How it changes across the course
Unit 7
c. 1859–1914
It supplies a scientific-sounding excuse for things Europeans were already doing: colonial conquest, laissez-faire economics, and racial hierarchy. Because it borrowed the vocabulary of biology and measurement, opposing it meant appearing to argue against science rather than against prejudice — which is a large part of why it spread so easily.

Unit 8
c. 1918–1945
It becomes law. Eugenic thinking produces immigration limits, marriage restrictions, and compulsory sterilization statutes in a number of countries, and the Nazi state builds its stated 'new racial order' on the same logic — a ranked hierarchy with Germans ruling, Slavic peoples reduced to laborers, and Jews and Roma removed entirely. That was not a wartime excess but the goal occupation policy was designed to achieve.

Unit 9
c. 1945–present
Discredited as science and abandoned as public argument once the camps were opened and Nuremberg documented what it had licensed. Lemkin's word genocide enters international law with the UN convention of 1948, and explicit biological ranking of peoples disappears from mainstream European politics — later arguments about immigration are made on economic and cultural grounds instead.

The mistake students make

Students write that 'Darwin caused imperialism,' or that Darwin was a Social Darwinist. Darwin described how species change over time; he did not argue that rich nations deserve their wealth or that the poor should be left to fail. 'Survival of the fittest' is Herbert Spencer's phrase. Keep the science and the ideology in separate sentences, and use the CED's own word — inadvertently.

What the exam tests

Sourcing. The CED pairs this topic with Skill 2.C, the significance of a source's point of view and purpose. Expect a document that uses biological language about nations or races, and a question asking what the author is actually trying to justify with it.

Anti-Semitism

Anti-Semitism is hostility toward Jews; over the 19th century it changed from a religious objection into a racial claim that no conversion could erase.
Older European hostility treated Jews as people who had refused Christianity, so baptism was in principle a way out of it. The version that spread after about 1870 called Jews a separate biological race — permanently foreign no matter how assimilated — and blamed them collectively for finance, revolution, and national decline. That change is what made first legal exclusion and then extermination thinkable as government policy.
First appears
Unit 4 · 1780s — It appears in this course first as the thing being dismantled: Joseph II grants Jews civil rights, and the CED flags civil equality for Jews as a much harder step than tolerating a rival Christian church — one only some states were willing to take.
Who held it
In its 19th-century racial form: nationalist pressure groups, parts of the clergy, army officers, and small shopkeepers and artisans squeezed by industrial competition — and mass parties that discovered it won elections, as Karl Lueger's Christian Social Party did in Vienna.
Defined against
Jewish emancipation — the legal equality western European states had been extending since the late 18th century.
How it changes across the course
Unit 4
c. 1780–1800
Legal restrictions start coming off. Joseph II grants toleration to Protestants and civil rights to Jews in the 1780s, and by 1800 most governments in western and central Europe have extended toleration to Christian minorities. Full civil equality for Jews goes further than that, and most states stop short.

Unit 7
c. 1870–1914
Emancipation and hostility rise together. Jewish families enter the professions, universities, and the army at exactly the moment a new racial anti-Semitism denies that assimilation is even possible. Dreyfus is convicted on forged evidence in the most legally emancipated country in Europe, Lueger governs Vienna on the issue, and Herzl concludes that Jews are a nation and need a state of their own.

It becomes German state policy and then genocide. Roughly six million Jews were killed, about 1.1 million of them at Auschwitz — and collaborationist governments such as Vichy France passed their own anti-Jewish laws and made arrests themselves.

The mistake students make

Students treat anti-Semitism as a constant background hum and jump straight from medieval persecution to 1942. The CED asks for the change: religious hostility offered an exit through conversion, and the racial version invented after about 1870 offered none. That is also why 'people were prejudiced' is not an answer — the Dreyfus affair happened in a republic founded on the rights of man, a century after France emancipated its Jews.

What the exam tests

Causation and continuity/change. KC-3.3.I.F lists 'racialism with a concomitant anti-Semitism' as one of the ways nationalists built loyalty, and KC-3.3.I.G makes Zionism a direct response to it. Unit 8 then asks how the Nazi 'new racial order' became occupation policy. The expected chain is emancipation, racial backlash, legal exclusion, genocide.

Feminism

Feminism is the claim that women are entitled to the same legal, economic, and political rights as men, together with the organized effort to win them.
Feminists argued that the differences used to justify excluding women were produced rather than natural. Wollstonecraft said women appear irrational only because they are denied education; de Beauvoir wrote that one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman. The demands changed over two centuries but the shape of the argument did not: identify a legal or customary disability, show it is a choice somebody made, and campaign to reverse it.
First appears
Unit 4 · 1790s — Wollstonecraft answers Rousseau's argument for keeping women out of public life by turning Enlightenment logic against an Enlightenment thinker; Condorcet argues for full political rights for women and for free people of color.
Who held it
Educated middle-class women and a minority of male allies at first; after about 1860 also working women organizing through unions and socialist parties — though the middle-class suffrage societies and the socialist women's movement often distrusted each other.
Defined against
The cult of domesticity, and the marriage laws under which a wife's property, earnings, and legal identity belonged to her husband.
How it changes across the course
Unit 4
c. 1770–1800
It begins as an argument inside the Enlightenment rather than as a movement. Rousseau argues women belong out of public life; Wollstonecraft and Condorcet answer him with the Enlightenment's own tools. Meanwhile salons organized and hosted by women give the objection practical force at exactly the moment Rousseau is arguing to shut women out.

Anarchism

Anarchism is the belief that all governmental authority is unnecessary and harmful and should be abolished, leaving a society held together by voluntary cooperation.
Anarchists agreed with socialists that private ownership of factories was the problem, and disagreed sharply about the cure. Any state, they argued — including a workers' state run by a revolutionary party — turns into a new machine for coercion, so the target is authority itself rather than just capitalist authority. Cooperation would then be organized from below through unions, communes, and voluntary associations.
First appears
Unit 6 · 1860s–1870s — Bakunin insists that any state, including a socialist one, becomes a tyranny, and is expelled from Marx's International in 1872 — the split that separates anarchism from Marxism permanently.
Who held it
Artisans, landless laborers, and industrial workers in places where the state was hostile and unions were illegal — strongest in Spain, Italy, Russia, and parts of France, weakest where socialist parties could actually win elections.
Defined against
Marxism, over the single question of whether a temporary workers' state is acceptable.
How it changes across the course
Unit 6
c. 1860–1890
Anarchism is defined by what it refuses. The CED gives anarchists their own key concept — all forms of governmental authority are unnecessary and should be replaced by a society based on voluntary cooperation — a position that put them outside the mass socialist parties then being built and got Bakunin thrown out of the International.
Mikhail BakuninKarl MarxBakunin expelled from Marx's International (1872)Statism and Anarchy
Unit 7
c. 1890–1914
It turns to direct action and to myth. Sorel argues that the general strike is worth believing in for its power to move workers rather than for whether it would actually work, and a wave of assassinations across Europe makes anarchism the era's byword for political violence — which also gave governments an easy reason to dismiss the whole movement.

c. 1936–1939
Anarchism gets one real test and loses it. In Republican Spain anarchist unions ran collectivized farms and factories and fought Franco, while also fighting the communists over who would control the war effort. The CED uses a 1936 Spanish anarchist poster as a stimulus and expects you to distinguish anarchism from fascism rather than just name both.

Students hear 'anarchy' and write 'chaos.' Anarchists were not against organization — they built unions, schools, and cooperatives — they were against organization backed by force. The distinction the CED actually tests is anarchism versus Marxism: both wanted to end capitalism, but Marxists accepted a workers' state as a stage along the way and anarchists refused it outright, which is precisely what got Bakunin expelled in 1872.

What the exam tests

Sourcing and comparison. The CED includes a 1936 Spanish anarchist poster as a stimulus and warns that a good answer must do more than simply mention the words Anarchism or Fascism — you are expected to state what each side believed and what the poster's maker wanted from its audience.

Fascism

Fascism is a movement that places the nation above the individual, rejects both liberal democracy and communism, glorifies struggle and war, and concentrates power in one charismatic leader.
Fascists argued that parliaments were talking shops that had already failed, that classes should be fused into a single national body instead of set against one another, and that a nation is made great by discipline, sacrifice, and force rather than by debate. To people humiliated by defeat and frightened by economic collapse, they offered a story in which the nation had been betrayed from within and could be restored by a leader who did not have to ask permission.
First appears
Unit 8 · 1919–1922 — Mussolini builds a movement out of postwar bitterness, squad violence against socialists, and fear of communism, and is handed the premiership after the March on Rome in 1922.
Who held it
Veterans, unemployed young men, small shopkeepers and farmers squeezed by the Depression, and — critically — conservative elites, industrialists, and army officers who believed they could use the movement and then control it.
Defined against
Liberal democracy and communism simultaneously; KC-4.2.II names 'the rise of communism' and 'uncertain transitions to democracy' among the conditions it grew in.
How it changes across the course
Unit 7
c. 1880–1914
The ideas exist before the movement does. The CED says fascism had 'roots in the pre-World War I era': the turn to irrationality and impulse and the belief that conflict and struggle produce progress, Sorel's argument that a mobilizing myth beats a correct analysis, chauvinist nationalism, and racial theory presented as science. Nietzsche belongs in this story only as a thinker whose ideas were later distorted by nationalists he despised.

The mistake students make

Students write that fascists 'seized power in a coup.' Both came in through legal doors — Mussolini was appointed prime minister by the king, Hitler was appointed chancellor by the president — after years of street violence had made their movements look like the only available source of order. The other frequent error is treating fascism and communism as interchangeable because both produced dictatorships: they recruited different people, promised opposite futures, and each treated the other as its principal enemy.

Causation. Totalitarianism

Totalitarianism is a system in which the state claims authority over every part of life — work, schooling, leisure, family, belief — and permits no independent institution to exist.
What separates it from ordinary dictatorship is ambition. An authoritarian government wants obedience and will settle for silence; a totalitarian one wants belief, so it takes over youth groups, unions, churches, newspapers, art, and in some cases the calendar itself. In practice it runs on a single party, a cult built around the leader, mass media piped directly into homes, and a political police that treats private opinion as a crime.
First appears
Unit 8 · 1920s–1930s — Applied first to Mussolini's Italy and then, with far more force, to Stalin's Soviet Union and Hitler's Germany — states that used modern media and modern policing to reach into places no earlier government could.
Who held it
This is a label historians and political scientists apply rather than a banner people marched under, though Mussolini's regime used the word approvingly about itself.
Defined against
Liberal democracy — and also the older absolutism, which claimed unlimited legal authority but never tried to occupy private life.
How it changes across the course

Not the word, but arguably the first attempt. The Jacobin republic conscripted the whole population through the levée en masse, fixed prices and wages, closed churches and replaced the calendar under de-Christianization, and used fast-moving tribunals against suspected enemies. Historians disagree about how far that comparison holds, and an essay is stronger for saying so.

Communism

Communism is the goal of a classless society with no private ownership of the means of production — and, after 1917, the name for the states and parties that claimed to be building it.
In Marx's account communism is the end point: once the working class controls production, classes disappear and the state itself becomes unnecessary. In practice after 1917 it meant a one-party state, an economy run by central planning rather than markets, and a claim that the party spoke for the working class whether or not the workers agreed. Supporters pointed to rapid industrialization, literacy, and guaranteed work and housing; critics pointed to famine, camps, and the absence of any lawful way to say no.
First appears
Unit 6 · 1848 — The word arrives with The Communist Manifesto, where it names a future Marx expected rather than any government that existed.
Who held it
Before 1917, a minority faction inside socialist parties. After 1917, the Soviet party-state and its bloc, plus communist parties with real support in France and Italy — strongest among factory workers, union militants, and intellectuals.
Defined against
Capitalism, and — bitterly — the reformist social democrats who shared the same ancestor.
How it changes across the course
Unit 6
c. 1848–1900
A word for a destination, not a system. In this unit communism is a prediction about where class struggle ends; the organizations that actually existed were mutual aid societies, unions, cooperatives, and socialist parties contesting elections.

Existentialism

Existentialism is the postwar argument that human beings are not handed a purpose in advance — we exist first, and then have to create meaning through the choices we make.
If there is no built-in human nature and no guaranteed plan, then no God, tradition, party, or expert can tell you what your life is for — and nothing excuses what you do with it. Sartre's phrase was that we are condemned to be free: freedom is a burden rather than a prize, because every choice belongs entirely to you. It was written by people who had just watched reason, progress, and obedience produce two world wars.
First appears
Unit 9 · 1943–1949 — Sartre publishes Being and Nothingness in occupied Paris and then Existentialism Is a Humanism; de Beauvoir applies the same argument to women in The Second Sex.
Who held it
Writers, philosophers, students, and readers in Paris and then across Western Europe — it spread through novels, plays, and cafés far more than through university lecture halls.
Defined against
Positivism, and the whole 19th-century confidence that science and reason would eventually settle how to live.
How it changes across the course
Unit 7
c. 1880–1914
The ground is cleared first. Nietzsche declares that God is dead and attacks conventional morality, Bergson argues that intuition reaches truths analysis cannot, and Freud relocates the real self below conscious thought. The CED's summary for the period is a new relativism in values and a loss of confidence in the objectivity of knowledge.

The mistake students make

Students summarize existentialism as 'life is meaningless, so nothing matters,' which is nearly the opposite of the argument. Sartre's point is that because nothing supplies meaning in advance, every choice matters completely, and you cannot hide behind your nature, your class, or your orders. That is exactly why it gripped a generation arguing about resistance and collaboration in occupied France.

What the exam tests

Continuity and change. Unit 9 Learning Objective O asks how and why European culture changed from the period following World War II to the present, and KC-4.3.I.B gives the causal chain in order — war and depression, then existentialism, then postmodernism. Learn that order; the sequence is the answer.

Modernism

Modernism is the turn in art, music, and literature after about 1880 toward experiment and the artist's own perception, on the assumption that there is no single correct way to see the world.
Modernists stopped treating a painting as a window onto one moment seen from one place, or a novel as an ordered story with a beginning and an end. If what you observe depends on where you stand — as relativity, quantum theory, and Freud each suggested in their own fields — then the honest thing for art to do is show its own construction. The result deliberately provoked audiences who expected beauty, patriotism, and a recognizable subject.
First appears
Unit 7 · 1874–1907 — Rejected by the official Salon, the Impressionists mount their own exhibition in 1874 and move the subject of a painting from the world to the act of seeing; thirty years later Picasso and Braque break the object into facets viewed from several directions at once.
Who held it
Artists, composers, and writers working outside academies and state exhibitions, backed by private dealers, small magazines, and an urban audience — centered on Paris, Vienna, and Berlin before 1914.
Defined against
Academic art and the Salon jury system, and behind them the positivist confidence that there is one objective world to be recorded.
How it changes across the course
Unit 7
c. 1874–1914
Modernism starts where confidence in objective knowledge ends. Monet records changing light rather than objects, Van Gogh reports his own state rather than the view, and Cubism abandons the single viewpoint altogether — at the same moment Planck, Einstein, and Freud are unsettling the idea of a fixed, knowable reality in physics and psychology.

Unit 8
c. 1914–1945
The war radicalizes it and the dictatorships attack it. Futurism, Dada, and Surrealism push further from representation, Schoenberg abandons keys and Joyce abandons plot, and the Bauhaus strips design down to function. Then the Nazi and Soviet states denounce this work and drive its makers abroad — Schoenberg, Einstein, and the Bauhaus staff all left, taking the European avant-garde with them.

Unit 9
c. 1945–present
The center of gravity moves to New York. Abstract Expressionism makes the act of painting itself the content, Pop Art takes its images from advertising and celebrity, and Bauhaus design becomes the standard look of rebuilt European city centers. After about 1970 postmodernism replaces modernism's hunt for the new with quotation, irony, and a deliberate blurring of high and popular culture.

The mistake students make

Students use 'modern' to mean 'recent' and end up calling anything after 1900 modernist. Modernism is a specific set of choices — breaking the frame, showing the process, refusing a single viewpoint — and it has an end date: by about 1970 the CED has moved on to postmodernism, which doubts the very idea of artistic progress modernism assumed. The other slip is treating modernism as apolitical; it was banned, burned, and exiled by both the Nazi and Soviet states.

What the exam tests

Causation. KC-3.6.III says that 'a new relativism in values and the loss of confidence in the objectivity of knowledge led to modernism in intellectual and cultural life.' A strong essay ties one specific work to one specific loss of confidence — Cubism to the collapse of the single viewpoint, Freud to the unreliable narrator — rather than listing artists.
