A path through the skills.

The official course framework develops rhetorical situation, claims and evidence, reasoning and organization, and style through reading and writing. The sequence below is this studio’s original learning pathway.

01 / Rhetorical situationRead the room before the sentence.

Identify the writer, audience, purpose, context, and exigence—the pressure or occasion that prompts a response. Audience is the group being addressed; purpose is what the writer wants to accomplish.

See it in action

A student asks the school board to keep a late bus. Board members control funding; the request arrives before a budget vote.

Try it

Name the decision the audience can actually make. How would the message change for classmates?

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

To board members, cost and access may matter. To classmates, the writer might emphasize shared experiences and participation. These are reasoned possibilities, not guarantees about any audience.

02 / Claims & evidenceSeparate a position from its support.

A claim can be questioned. Evidence gives readers a reason to accept it. Evaluate relevance, accuracy, and sufficiency rather than treating a vivid example as proof of every possible case.

See it in action

“Extend library hours” is a claim. A count of students turned away at closing could support it, but only if the count is credible and the sample is appropriate.

Try it

Write one claim about a school or community practice. Identify evidence that would actually test it.

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

A survey of current users might show demand, but it can miss people unable to visit during current hours. A useful argument considers that gap.

03 / CommentaryBuild the bridge from evidence to claim.

Commentary explains how and why evidence matters. It does more than restate the quotation or announce that it proves the point.

See it in action

Fictional practice: Claim — the school should provide a covered bike rack. Detail — riders in a one-week student log reported wet seats after three afternoon storms. Commentary should explain why protection from rain could make cycling more usable; this small log cannot establish every student’s needs.

Try it

Using the bike-rack claim and detail above, complete: This detail supports my claim because ____. The reasoning depends on ____. Name one limitation of the log.

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

This detail supports my claim because riders reported wet seats after three storms, which suggests rain makes the current rack less usable. The reasoning depends on the assumption that a cover would keep seats dry enough to make cycling more practical. One limitation: a one-week log from a few riders cannot show how many students would ride more if the rack were covered.

The second sentence exposes an assumption; naming the limitation keeps the claim honest.

04 / Line of reasoningGive each paragraph a job.

A line of reasoning is the progression of claims that supports a larger position. Organize by ideas and relationships, not by the order sources appear in a packet.

See it in action

Claim 1 establishes a problem of access. Claim 2 evaluates a feasible response. Claim 3 answers a cost objection.

Try it

Make a three-step outline. Write the relationship between each pair of steps.

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

“Because access is uneven, a targeted extension is justified; because resources are limited, the extension should be piloted and evaluated.” The steps build on one another.

05 / Rhetorical choicesExplain a move in its situation.

Writers choose examples, contrasts, concessions, organization, diction, imagery, and sentence patterns. Analyze how a choice works here; a device label alone is not analysis.

See it in action

A speaker first acknowledges a budget concern, then proposes a limited pilot.

Try it

What does the concession make possible for a skeptical audience?

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

It recognizes the audience’s constraint and presents the proposal as responsive to it. Do not claim that every listener is persuaded.

06 / SynthesisMake your sources interact.

Synthesis uses multiple sources to develop your own argument. Ask whether one source supports, challenges, qualifies, or reframes another. Cite sources when borrowing information or ideas.

See it in action

A survey shows demand for later hours; a budget memo limits staffing; a schedule suggests a one-night pilot.

Try it

Write a sentence connecting two sources through a relationship, then explain its significance.

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

The survey supports the need for access, while the budget memo shapes how that need can be met. Together they support a targeted pilot rather than an unlimited extension.

07 / Qualification & counterargumentMake room for a real complication.

A qualification states the conditions or limits of a claim. A counterargument is a reasonable alternative view; respond to its strongest version instead of a caricature.

See it in action

“Revision should matter more than speed when the goal is durable learning, though time limits matter when a task requires an immediate decision.”

Try it

Use the revision-versus-speed claim above, or one of your own. Identify a case in which it would be less convincing. Does it narrow or overturn your position?

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

A narrower claim can be stronger. Explain why the exception matters instead of attaching “however” to an unrelated point.

08 / Style & revisionChoose clarity with intention.

Style includes diction, syntax, tone, and the arrangement of ideas. Revise for a particular audience and purpose. Precise verbs often do more than inflated vocabulary.

See it in action

“The policy affects participation” becomes “The early closing time excludes students who arrive on the late bus.”

Try it

Revise “The policy affects participation” using the detail above, or revise a sentence from your own draft. Name what your revision clarifies.

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

The revision identifies a mechanism and a group affected. It is more useful because readers can test the claim.

09 / Transfer & reflectionUse the skill beyond one prompt.

Try a fresh text or issue. Work independently, review against the relevant criteria, and choose one next move. A practice result describes this attempt; it does not predict an AP score.

See it in action

After improving commentary in a rhetorical analysis paragraph, apply the same evidence-to-claim bridge to an argument paragraph.

Try it

Compare an attempt with its model in any earlier step. What changed in your reasoning? Where will you use that move next?

Reveal a possible explanation

Try your own explanation first. A short note is enough; you can also work on paper or in another app.

A useful reflection names an action: “I explained why the example matters to the audience,” rather than “I wrote more.”

Use the terms as tools.

Exigence: what prompts the response. Claim: a position that needs support. Commentary: the reasoning that connects evidence and claim. Concession: recognition of a reasonable opposing point. Qualification: a limit or condition. Tone: an attitude conveyed by choices in language.

Naming a term is a beginning. Explaining what it accomplishes is the work.

The hours on the door

Original editorial for a fictional city council. All people and circumstances are invented for practice.

1At six o’clock, the library’s lights remain bright, but its doors are locked. On the pavement, a student balances a laptop on one knee while the building’s wireless signal reaches where its welcome cannot. The schedule says the library is open to everyone. The pavement tells a more complicated story.

2Extending hours every night would be expensive, and exhausted staff cannot be the hidden subsidy for a public good. Yet that objection should shape a proposal, not end the conversation. A single evening each week, staffed and funded in advance, would let the city test whether later access meets a real need.

3Count the visits, but also ask who comes. A room full of the same people staying longer is different from a room that welcomes people who could never arrive before closing. The question is not whether the library can remain open indefinitely. It is whether a small change can make the word “everyone” a little more honest.

The repair table

Original fictional essay for a neighborhood newsletter. People, events and observations are invented for practice.

1On Saturday, the community center’s repair table collected three lamps, a jammed zipper, and a toaster whose owner had carried it there in a shopping bag. By noon, two lamps shone again. The toaster went home unrepaired. In the volunteers’ photograph, however, only the working lamps appeared. A neat picture of success can make a useful project harder to understand.

2The organizers wanted a hopeful image, and their hope was reasonable. A repaired object saves its owner the price and effort of replacing it. A shared repair may also teach someone to ask what failed before buying something new. Yet a table covered in tools is not a promise that every object can be rescued. Some products conceal replaceable parts behind glued cases; some failures require equipment or expertise that neighbors do not have. Publishing only successes risks making those limits look like individual shortcomings.

3A better record would pair each repair with a brief account of what made it possible and record unsuccessful attempts with equal care. The list might reveal that ten volunteers could not find the same inexpensive replacement switch. That information would tell residents something about design and supply, not simply volunteer skill. It could also guide the next workshop: a lesson on diagnosis might help more people than another photograph of a glowing lamp.

4The repair table should remain a place of encouragement. Encouragement becomes more durable when it survives an honest account of limits. If every visit must produce a rescued object to count as valuable, the center will eventually have an incentive to turn difficult objects away. A record of what people learned would keep the door open to the toaster as well as the lamp.

Who gets counted at the creek?

Original fictional public-science column. The town, project and observations are invented; no real research findings are claimed.

1The creek survey began with a cheerful invitation: anyone could help count birds before breakfast. At seven on a Tuesday, twelve residents met the project leader at the footbridge. Their notebooks would help the town decide where to restore habitat. The invitation said anyone; the meeting time quietly supplied a narrower definition.

2A common time and route have a scientific purpose. Observations made under comparable conditions are easier to interpret than scattered reports gathered whenever someone happens to look outside. The project leader was right to resist turning a survey into an unstructured collection of sightings. But consistency in the method does not require pretending that a convenient schedule is convenient for everyone.

3The town could train a second group to follow the same route on a weekend morning and record each session’s date, weather and observers. Those observations should not simply be pooled as though Tuesday and Saturday were interchangeable. They could first be compared, with differences treated as questions to investigate. The additional session would widen participation while making the consequences of a changed schedule visible.

4Including more residents is valuable for another reason. A restoration decision will affect walkers, nearby workers and families who seldom visit at dawn. Their concerns do not become bird counts, and a public meeting cannot substitute for a survey. It can reveal which questions the survey has failed to ask. A project can invite people to help collect its evidence and also invite them to examine what that evidence is being used to decide.

The honest forecast

Original fictional science-communication essay. A forecaster explains public uncertainty; the scenario is invented, not weather or safety advice. Longer practice; still unofficial and uncalibrated.

1When our town began publishing a daily air-quality outlook, the first draft offered what several readers said they wanted: one large label, placed where nobody could miss it. The label made the page easy to scan. It also concealed a problem. Conditions could differ across neighborhoods and change before the afternoon commute. A single word looked decisive partly because it left out the decisions behind it.

2The forecasting team proposed adding a range and a short explanation. At the public meeting, one resident asked whether a range was simply a way to avoid being wrong. The question deserved more than a lecture about probability. People use an outlook to organize ordinary obligations. A parent arranging pickup or a coach planning practice may need a next step sooner than a technical account of the model. Information can be carefully qualified and still fail the person reading it.

3Yet removing uncertainty from the display would not remove it from the air. It would move uncertainty from a visible statement into an unstated assumption that the label applies everywhere until tomorrow. A useful forecast should distinguish what is measured, what is estimated, and what could change. The explanation should be short enough to use but specific enough to prevent a range from becoming a decorative disclaimer.

4We revised the page around two questions: what does the outlook suggest now, and what change would make a reader check again? The technical methods remained available behind a clearly labeled link. This design did not make every reader comfortable. Some wanted fewer details; others wanted the full model on the first screen. Their disagreement exposed the real task: not choosing between accuracy and usefulness, but deciding which information belongs at the point of a decision.

5No page can guarantee an ideal choice under uncertain conditions. Nor does every uncertainty deserve equal space. A minor modeling limitation may matter less to a reader than a known gap in local observations. The test of the revised outlook is therefore not whether its wording prevents all criticism. It is whether readers can state what the forecast supports, what it does not support, and when they should seek an update.

The empty label

Original fictional museum essay about civic memory. People, records and exhibit plans are invented; claims concern this scenario, not an actual institution.

1The smallest object proposed for the station exhibit was a brass key. Its donor remembered it hanging beside her grandfather’s coat, but she could not say which door it opened. Our first label solved the uncertainty with confidence: “Stationmaster’s key.” It fit the case beautifully. It fit the surviving evidence less well.

2A colleague suggested leaving the key out. An exhibit, she argued, should not ask visitors to pay for our unfinished research. Her concern was fair. Curators have a duty to distinguish what a record establishes from what they merely hope it means. Yet omission would also make a choice invisible. The visitor would see a polished account without encountering the uncertain object that helped us recognize how incomplete that account remained.

3The station’s official files offered dates, payroll lists and plans. Families offered recollections of waiting, leaving and returning. Neither kind of material could simply stand in for the other. A schedule could establish when a train was supposed to arrive; it could not tell us what a child felt while waiting for a parent. A memory could illuminate that experience without establishing the exact minute of arrival. Respecting a source required asking a question it could reasonably answer.

4We decided to display the key with a label that separated the donor’s recollection from our inference. The label stated that its use was unknown and invited visitors with relevant information to contact the museum. That invitation was not permission to turn every attractive story into fact. New accounts would still need to be documented, compared and, where possible, checked against other records. A blank in our knowledge was a reason to investigate, not a license to invent.

5The revised exhibit also faced a quieter question: whose families had been asked to contribute? An invitation printed inside the museum might reach the people already comfortable entering it and miss others with connections to the station. We could not treat a wall full of stories as proof that we had heard the whole town. Before adding another case, we needed to consider the paths by which a story could reach us.

6The key remained small. Its label became slightly longer. What changed most was the relationship we offered the visitor. The museum no longer asked for trust on the basis that every gap had been closed. It asked for trust in the care with which an open gap was described, investigated and kept distinct from what we knew.

A path that leaves room

Original fictional conservation proposal. Observations and decisions are invented for rhetorical practice; this is not ecological research or trail-safety guidance.

1From the road, the unofficial footpath looked like a thin scratch beside the marked trail. From the ground, it looked more like a decision repeated: the shortest route to the overlook, chosen one walker at a time. Our first response was to place a sign across it. By the following weekend, people had worn a narrow detour around the sign.

2It would have been easy to call this a failure of care. Some visitors surely noticed the request and ignored it. But the detour alone could not tell us why everyone took it. The marked route’s first sign was partly hidden behind a branch. Its map described a viewpoint without showing whether the longer route included stairs. A visitor reading the map could not easily distinguish a needless loop from a route with a purpose.

3These shortcomings did not make the damaged ground unimportant. They changed where responsibility had to be examined. A public request to protect a place should also give visitors a fair opportunity to understand and follow that request. Better information cannot guarantee compliance, but poor information can make a refusal difficult to distinguish from a misunderstanding.

4A volunteer proposed closing the overlook for a season. Another proposed paving the shortcut so walkers would remain on a durable surface. Each option might reduce a particular problem while creating another. Closure would protect the immediate area but remove access, including for people who could not return later. Paving might concentrate use, but it could also make a newly convenient route permanent before we understood its effects. We should evaluate these proposals by more than the speed with which they make the scratch disappear.

5I propose a limited first step: uncover the route sign, make the map’s access information explicit, and place a temporary barrier that explains the reason for the detour. Record where people turn and invite comments at the trail entrance. Those observations would not alone measure recovery of the ground; that would require a different kind of assessment. They could, however, help us see whether visitors understand the intended route before we decide that understanding has failed.

6A trial must also have an ending. If repeated damage continues, clearer information should not become a permanent excuse for delay. The group should agree in advance on a review date and on what additional evidence is needed to choose among closure, redesign and continued access. The aim is not to preserve every current convenience or to celebrate a sign as a solution. It is to make the next restriction—or the decision not to impose one—answer to the problem we have actually investigated.

When a pause belongs in the story

Original, unofficial, uncalibrated writing/revision practice. The writer, interview, publication and editing example are invented instructional material, not reported research. This focused set is not an official question set, full-length exam, proficiency diagnostic or AP score predictor.

A student is drafting an editorial for the students who produce a school’s audio magazine. The editorial argues that editors should retain meaningful pauses in interviews rather than automatically cutting them. Treat every question independently, using the original draft. Do not carry a revision from one question into another. Paragraph and sentence numbers are stable reference points. Attempt independently, explain two difficult choices, then check the reasoning and revise a paragraph of your own. Any classroom timing is a teaching choice, not an official exam limit.

Paragraph 1(1) Technology has changed communication in many important ways, and audio editing is one of those ways. (2) In a draft episode of our audio magazine, a graduating senior describes the last rehearsal before her theater teacher retired. (3) “I thought we would just finish the scene,” she says, followed by a pause, “but nobody wanted to leave.” (4) An editor can remove the pause with two clicks; whether those clicks improve the story is a different question. (5) Our magazine should think carefully about pauses because editing is important and interviews contain many kinds of sound.

Paragraph 2(6) A transcript records the senior’s words, but the recording also records the interval between them. (7) With the pause removed, her second statement follows the first as smoothly as an item on a list. (8) A pause reveals exactly what a speaker is feeling. (9) In the uncut version, the listener has time to register a difference between finishing the assigned scene and wanting the shared experience to continue. (10) The silence does not supply another fact about the rehearsal; it changes the pace at which the existing facts reach us. (11) Removing it is therefore an interpretation, not merely a cleaning operation. (12) The editor recorded the interview on a silver device whose carrying case had a broken zipper.

Paragraph 3(13) Keeping every hesitation would not solve the problem. (14) A speaker searching for an address may pause for reasons unrelated to the episode’s subject, and a long interruption can obscure rather than clarify a thought. (15) Nor should editors turn discomfort into a dramatic effect simply because it makes a recording compelling. (16) In the rehearsal interview, the senior approved the account for publication but asked that a separate discussion of a family difficulty remain private. (17) The request does not authorize editors to imply that the private difficulty caused her hesitation in the published passage. (18) We cannot verify that connection. (19) We should not suggest it through a caption, a musical cue, or the placement of another clip.

Paragraph 4(20) Editors need a repeatable way to make these decisions together. (21) First, compare the original clip with the proposed cut and describe what the cut changes: factual information, pacing, emphasis, or the apparent relationship between statements. (22) Then write down the things about the changes so other people can understand the things that happened. (23) If a disagreement concerns the speaker’s intended meaning or permission to use material, ask the speaker rather than settling the issue by guessing. (24) A shorter clip is not automatically a more faithful one. (25) This process will not produce identical choices from every editor, but it will make those choices available for discussion instead of hiding them behind a claim of technical necessity.

Paragraph 5(26) Our listeners deserve more than a recording from which every trace of hesitation has disappeared. (27) They deserve editors who can explain why a pause was removed or allowed to remain. (28) Audio magazines can be an interesting activity for students with different interests.

Model revision

Access should be measured by successful use, not by the number of items the library purchases. The director’s proposal promises that residents can borrow equipment for occasional needs (Source A), but the advisory group identifies work schedules and transportation as barriers (Source D). If the intended borrowers cannot collect the equipment, even a well-stocked collection misses its purpose. The pilot should therefore test evening pickup and lighter items before expanding. That choice would also make the $20,000 projected budget easier to evaluate: the town could compare costs with completed loans rather than assuming that availability guarantees use (Source B).

The opening belongs to the writer; sources play different roles; the explanation turns their relationship into a condition for action. This is one model paragraph, not a scored six-point essay.

1. Read the room

Suggested 12–15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 1.A, 1.B

Read the room

A rhetorical situation includes the writer, audience, purpose, context, exigence, and message. Context is the surrounding situation; exigence is the problem or development calling for a response now. An audience inference must rest on textual evidence. “Everyone values safety” is weaker than explaining why this speaker makes safety especially relevant to these listeners.

Worked example

A student addresses a school transportation committee authorized to approve routes after an after-school bus is canceled: “You should not have to choose between keeping your budget and keeping students involved. Let us test one late route on Thursdays before we ask you for a daily service.”

The committee can authorize transportation, so the student’s purpose is to win a limited trial. The cancellation creates the immediate need to speak. “Keeping your budget” acknowledges a financial constraint; a one-day trial responds to that concern. This ties an audience assumption to a specific choice rather than simply labeling the audience “adults.”

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

A neighborhood volunteer writes to local shop owners after a shopping street changes its waste-collection schedule: “Yesterday, overflowing bins greeted your first customers before you could. I am not asking you to hire another worker. If each business posts the new pickup times beside its back door, we can test a shared reminder system for two weeks. Tell us where the schedule still fails; the goal is a cleaner opening, not another rule to police.”

Try independently

Identify writer, audience, purpose, context, exigence, and message. Distinguish the last three rather than repeating “trash” for each. Identify two audience concerns; for each, cite a phrase and explain how the writer addresses it. What can you reasonably infer about this audience, and what would be an unsupported assumption?

Hint 1

Separate the event that prompted this message from the response the writer wants. Look for phrases that anticipate an objection.

Hint 2

“Hire another worker” addresses cost or workload; “not another rule” addresses resistance to policing. Explain why these concerns matter to shop owners here. The text does not establish their political views or personal wealth.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

The volunteer writes to shop owners to secure participation in a two-week reminder trial. The surrounding context is a changed collection schedule; the immediate problem is overflowing bins at opening. The message is that a small coordinated change might improve conditions without adding staffing or enforcement. “Your first customers” appeals to their interest in a welcoming business. “Not another rule to police” anticipates the burden of enforcement. It is reasonable to infer concern about operating smoothly, but not that every owner opposes all regulation.

Transfer to a different task

Rewrite the first two sentences for the sanitation department rather than shop owners. Then explain which audience concern you changed and why. Do not simply replace the greeting.

Compare / A possible transfer

Try the new task first, or intentionally compare now.

“Several storefront bins were full at opening yesterday. Could we compare pickup times with the street’s opening hours before proposing a change?” This emphasizes information and coordination rather than customer impression.

Revise without losing the first attempt

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

Which audience claim did your evidence actually support? What did you remove because it was a guess?

Review evidence: Accurate situation distinctions; two supported audience inferences; a purposeful transfer, not a synonym swap.

2. Open and close with purpose

Suggested 15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 2.A, 2.B

Open and close with purpose

An opening establishes the issue and gives these readers a reason to consider it. An ending can clarify a consequence, limit, or next step. Neither requires a dramatic hook, a dictionary definition, or a word-for-word thesis repeat. Audience awareness changes what you explain, acknowledge, and emphasize—not whether you tell the truth.

Worked example

Task: ask a principal for a weekly quiet lunch room. Weak opening: “Since the beginning of time, students have eaten lunch.” Purposeful opening: “For students who need a brief break from cafeteria noise, lunch does not always feel like a break. A supervised quiet room once a week would let us test another option without replacing the cafeteria.” Purposeful ending: “If the trial attracts few students or creates a supervision problem, revise it rather than expand it. The first decision is whether a small test is worth learning from.”

The opening names a local need and a limited proposal; the ending identifies a decision and conditions instead of promising universal success.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

A community center has one unused Saturday morning room. A proposal would let teenagers organize a monthly repair-and-reuse workshop there. No attendance or savings data exist yet. Staff worry about supervision and tools; teenagers worry that the event will feel like another class.

Try independently

Write a 2–3 sentence introduction for the center director and a different introduction for potential teenage participants. Keep the facts and proposal consistent. Write a 2–3 sentence conclusion for the director that offers a reasonable next step and addresses a limitation. Annotate one choice in each version: “I chose ___ because this audience ___.”

Hint 1

What does each audience need to decide? You do not have evidence to promise a certain turnout or savings.

Hint 2

For the director, acknowledge supervision and scope. For participants, explain what they can choose or make. A conclusion could propose a single supervised trial with a review afterward.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

Director: “The unused Saturday room could host a small repair workshop without committing the center to a permanent program. I propose one supervised trial with a restricted tool list, followed by a staff review.” Participants: “Have an object you want to fix but do not know where to start? Help shape a Saturday repair workshop where participants choose a project and trade ideas rather than follow a lecture.” Director conclusion: “Please approve one trial only after a supervisor and tool rules are in place. Afterward, use participation and safety observations to decide whether another workshop is worth offering.”

Transfer to a different task

A participant argues that “repair” sounds intimidating. Revise the participant opening without disguising the actual activity. Explain why your new invitation remains accurate.

Revise without losing the first attempt

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

Did your conclusion advance the reader’s thinking, or merely repeat your introduction?

Review evidence: Introductions fit distinct audiences; no invented results; conclusion makes a relevant move beyond repetition.

3. Test the argument

Suggested 15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 3.A, 3.B, 3.C

Test the argument

A thesis organizes an argument; supporting claims help establish it. Evidence is material offered as support, not a claim made louder. A qualification limits when or how a claim holds. An alternative perspective may require revising a claim without abandoning it. Ask whether evidence is relevant, credible, and sufficient—three different questions.

Worked example

“Because the school courtyard has little shade, we should test movable shade structures before installing permanent ones. Three afternoon observations found most occupied seats in shade, but those observations do not show how students would use the courtyard in winter.”

The recommendation is the thesis. The observations support the narrower claim that shade matters on the observed afternoons. “Before installing permanent ones” limits the proposal; the winter caveat limits what the evidence can establish. Neither proves a year-round preference.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

“Our town should keep the public fountain open later on summer weekends, provided the maintenance team can cover the added hours. During three July evenings, volunteers counted more people gathering nearby after six than before six. Several nearby café owners also said a later closing would make the square more welcoming. These observations support a summer trial, not a permanent year-round change. A resident who lives beside the square warns that later hours could prolong noise; the trial should therefore include a fixed closing time and a way to record complaints.”

Try independently

State the overarching thesis and explain how its limits shape the argument. Separate two pieces of evidence from the claims they support. Identify a condition, a limitation, and a counterargument; explain how each affects the proposal. Which claim remains insufficiently supported: greater evening use in the three observations, café-owner support, or effects on residents’ noise exposure? Explain without treating the invented observations as universal facts.

Hint 1

Look for “provided,” “not,” and the resident’s warning. Evidence about how many people are present does not directly measure noise.

Hint 2

A café owner’s preference is evidence of that person’s view, not proof of effects on all residents. The thesis calls for a limited change, not unrestricted late operation.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

The thesis favors later summer-weekend hours only if maintenance can cover them. The counts show more people nearby after six on three July evenings, not demand for later fountain operation; café comments show support among those consulted. Staffing is a condition, summer-only is a limit, and the noise concern is a counterargument. The fixed closing time responds to that concern but does not prove it solved. Noise effects remain unmeasured, so the writer should not claim the trial will be quiet.

Transfer to a different task

Add this new information: “The three observations all occurred during a festival.” Revise the evidentiary conclusion in two sentences. Do not discard the observations or pretend they represent an ordinary weekend.

Compare / A possible transfer

Try the new task first, or intentionally compare now.

“The counts show more people gathered nearby after six on those festival evenings, not demand for later fountain operation on ordinary weekends. Observe ordinary weekends and ask whether people want or would use later fountain hours before extending the inference.”

Revise without losing the first attempt

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

Which distinction mattered most: fact versus claim, relevance versus sufficiency, or limitation versus contradiction?

Review evidence: Thesis recognized with limits; support not overstated; counterargument treated substantively.

4. Build and qualify a claim

Suggested 18 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 4.A, 4.B, 4.C

Build and qualify a claim

A defensible thesis takes a position requiring support. A paragraph needs both evidence and an explanation of how that evidence advances the claim. A useful qualification specifies a condition or addresses a substantial objection; inserting “sometimes” everywhere is not enough.

Worked example

Thin claim: “Community gardens are good.” Defensible claim: “A neighborhood should offer a small shared garden only when residents can establish a maintenance plan, because access without upkeep can create a neglected space rather than a shared resource.”

The revised claim identifies a decision, a condition, and a reason that could be tested or challenged.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

Question: Should a fictional school run a semester-long device-free lunch table as an optional pilot? Invented evidence cards: A: In a voluntary survey of 40 students, 26 said they would try such a table at least once. B: A counselor reports that some students use phones at lunch to coordinate rides or check on family. C: The cafeteria has one table available; no additional staff have been assigned. D: A student says, “Everyone who uses a phone is antisocial.”

Try independently

Write a defensible thesis answering the question with an appropriate limit or condition. Develop a paragraph using two cards. Explain what each supports and what it cannot prove. Respond fairly to the strongest concern without insulting phone users. Identify one card you would reject or sharply limit, and explain why.

Hint 1

The survey is voluntary and small. Interest in trying is not proof of lasting benefit. Optional participation changes the force of the phone-access objection.

Hint 2

A reasonable position could support a small optional trial with exceptions, or reject it until logistics are clearer. Connect your chosen evidence to your actual position. D is an unsupported generalization, not credible evidence about all users.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

“The school should test an optional device-free table for one semester, provided students can step away for necessary messages and the arrangement does not require unassigned supervision. The 26 interested survey respondents suggest enough initial interest to try one table, though a voluntary survey cannot predict participation across the school. The counselor’s warning also matters within an optional pilot: participants may need to handle necessary messages, so they should be able to step away without penalty. The table can then test demand without treating practical phone use as a failure to participate.” This paragraph uses a limited inference, explains the evidence, and incorporates the objection into its policy. It does not establish that phone-free lunches improve mental health.

Transfer to a different task

New constraint: the only available table is needed for a student requiring a particular accessible location. Revise your thesis and one sentence of commentary. Preserve access rather than assuming it can be displaced.

Revise without losing the first attempt

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

Where did your evidence make you narrow or change your original position?

Review evidence: Arguable thesis; specific support; commentary explains limits and relevance; genuine qualification.

5. Follow the reasoning

Suggested 15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 5.A, 5.B, 5.C

Follow the reasoning

A line of reasoning connects claims so they advance an argument. Organization concerns those relationships, not merely paragraph order. Methods of development include narration, description, definition, comparison, and cause-and-effect. Naming a method is only the beginning: explain why it helps this writer make this argument. Sequence alone does not prove causation.

Worked example

A writer opens with a resident struggling to cross a wide street, compares two crossing designs, and ends by recommending a reversible trial.

The story makes the access problem concrete; comparison supplies criteria for choosing a response; the trial recommendation follows from uncertainty. “First, next, last” describes order but not this reasoning.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

A. “At the repair event, Mina brought a lamp with a loose switch. She left with a working lamp and a written explanation of what the volunteer changed.”

B. “That explanation separates a learning workshop from a free repair counter. A workshop should leave the visitor better able to understand the object, even when a repair is impossible.”

C. “A repair counter may finish more objects quickly; a teaching workshop may spend longer on each. If our goal is shared practical knowledge rather than maximum throughput, we should evaluate the workshop by what participants can explain, not simply by the number of repaired items.”

Try independently

Give each paragraph a job in the argument, not a one-word topic. Explain how B connects A to C and what would be lost if B disappeared. Identify two methods of development and explain their contribution. Does Mina’s successful repair prove that workshops cause lasting self-reliance? Why or why not?

Hint 1

A is an individual episode. B defines what the author means by “workshop.” C compares goals and measures.

Hint 2

Without the definition, the move from one lamp repair to a different evaluation standard would be less justified. An anecdote can illustrate the proposed distinction without establishing a general causal effect.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

A supplies a concrete instance; B turns that instance into a definition of a teaching workshop; C uses comparison to argue for an evaluation standard consistent with that definition. B connects the example’s written explanation to the broader purpose of understanding objects. Narration makes the idea visible; definition sets the criterion; comparison clarifies the trade-off. One successful repair cannot prove lasting self-reliance because no later behavior or alternative explanation is examined.

Transfer to a different task

Write a one-sentence bridge if a new paragraph about safety training follows C. Explain the relationship your bridge makes explicit.

Compare / A possible transfer

Try the new task first, or intentionally compare now.

“If understanding is the goal, participants must also learn when a repair is unsafe to attempt.” This extends the definition of useful learning rather than merely announcing another topic.

Revise without losing the first attempt

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

Did you explain what the organization accomplishes, or only label its parts?

Review evidence: Connected paragraph functions; method linked to purpose; causal inference kept proportional.

6. Design the reasoning

Suggested 18 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 6.A, 6.B, 6.C

Design the reasoning

Plan the reader’s thinking, not a required number of paragraphs. A transition should clarify a relationship—contrast, consequence, condition, extension—not decorate a gap in reasoning. Choose a method because it helps establish your claim. A short story may illustrate a need; a comparison may clarify a choice; neither replaces evidence explaining why your recommendation follows.

Worked example

Disconnected: “The center needs a quiet room. Furthermore, rooms need supervision.” Connected: “A quiet room would offer another way to use the center, but that option is workable only if supervision can be arranged.”

The revised sentence identifies a condition. “Furthermore” merely adds a fact without explaining its role.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

A town can pilot either a tool-lending shelf or a monthly tool-use workshop, but cannot staff both. The shelf offers flexible pickup times; the workshop provides supervised instruction at one fixed time. There are no reliable cost or turnout estimates yet.

Try independently

Choose a provisional recommendation and give three paragraph jobs in a logical order. Write about 120–170 words using comparison, definition, narration, or cause-and-effect purposefully; this is a learning guide, not a scoring threshold. Explain the relation between scenario evidence and your claim; do not invent statistics. Identify two transitions and label their relationships. Explain why your method fits your purpose and identify a limitation.

Hint 1

Choose a criterion before declaring a winner. “Access” could mean convenient pickup or the ability to use a tool safely; define which problem your argument prioritizes.

Hint 2

A plan might define the immediate goal, compare the options against it, then specify what to measure in a trial. Do not assume workshops make everyone safe or shelves require no oversight.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

“For a first trial, the town should prioritize supervised instruction rather than flexible borrowing. The goal should be to help residents understand basic tool use, not simply to place more tools in circulation. A lending shelf offers convenient pickup, whereas a workshop lets a participant ask questions while practicing. That difference makes the workshop a closer fit for the proposed learning goal. However, its fixed schedule may exclude residents who work at that time. The town should therefore treat attendance and requests for alternate times as evidence for its next decision, rather than assume low turnout means no interest. If access to instruction is the priority, a workshop is a reasonable starting point; if later evidence shows scheduling is the main barrier, the plan should change.” Comparison serves the decision; “whereas” marks contrast and “therefore” connects a limitation to an evaluation step. This is a reasoned proposal, not proof of the better program.

Transfer to a different task

Argue for the shelf instead using a different legitimate priority. Change the reasoning, not just the final recommendation.

Keep your original above. Put a revised passage or explanation here and name the change you made.

Reflect

7. Notice what sentences do

Suggested 15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 7.A, 7.B, 7.C

Notice what sentences do

Analyze the effect of a choice in context. A longer sentence is not inherently more sophisticated. Coordination can place ideas on a similar level; subordination can frame one as a condition, concession, or background. Punctuation helps readers see relationships, but a mark does not have one automatic emotional effect.

Worked example

“The plan is inexpensive, and it reaches few residents.” Compare: “Although the plan is inexpensive, it reaches few residents.”

Both convey the same two basic claims. The second treats low cost as a concession and emphasizes limited reach as the main judgment. Calling it a “complex sentence” is less useful than explaining that relationship. Students should be able to analyze complex syntax; artificially complex writing is not a timed-essay requirement.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

A resident opposes replacing a public garden with parking. Version A: “The garden is small, but its value is not. It gives this crowded block something rare: a place to remain without buying anything.” Version B: “Although the garden occupies little land, it provides a place where residents may stay without making a purchase.”

Try independently

Explain how A’s word choices and short opening create a different tone from B. Use a specific phrase. In B, identify the main assertion and concession; explain the change in “Although the garden offers a place to stay without buying anything, it occupies little land.” Explain the colon’s function in A. Evaluate “The garden is small, its value is not”: what clarity/convention problem appears, and how could punctuation address it without changing the meaning?

Hint 1

Ask which idea receives main-clause emphasis. “Something rare” creates an expectation that the words after the colon will specify it.

Hint 2

A uses compact contrast and accessible wording; B sounds more measured. Reversing subordination makes small size the main assertion. The revision joins independent clauses with only a comma; a period, semicolon, or suitable conjunction can clarify the boundary.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

A’s “small” versus “value is not” creates a punchier defense, while “remain without buying anything” emphasizes public access. B conveys a more measured judgment. Its opening concedes small size so the main clause can stress the benefit. Reversing the clauses shifts emphasis toward land occupied, weakening the benefit’s prominence. The colon introduces an explanation of “something rare.” A semicolon in “The garden is small; its value is not” correctly separates two independent clauses while retaining close contrast; a period also works. The mark alone does not guarantee persuasiveness.

8. Revise for meaning

Suggested 15 minutes · untimed learning guide, not an official exam limit.

Practice available · 8.A, 8.B, 8.C

Revise for meaning

Revision should preserve your intended claim while making it clearer and better suited to readers. Precision is not the same as complicated vocabulary. Correctness supports communication; no isolated punctuation trick earns a rubric point by itself. More than one revision may be effective.

Worked example

Before: “Due to the fact that access is an important thing, the proposal is beneficial in a lot of ways.” After: “The proposal would let residents without cars reach the center on Saturday.”

The revision names the benefit and who receives it. It is only legitimate if the writer has evidence for that specific claim; precision does not authorize invention.

New original task

Fictional instructional material — not actual research or an official exam question.

Facts: A volunteer group proposes one Saturday map-reading workshop at a park. Attendance is unknown. Beginners are welcome, and a trained leader has agreed to supervise. The group wants a clear, welcoming notice, not a guarantee that nobody will become lost. Draft: “Walking through the park, the map can be confusing. We will teach beginners map skills, it will make everyone perfectly safe. Bring a map pencil and water. The leader spoke to the volunteers after they arrived, which was helpful.”

Try independently

Repair the opening’s misplaced relationship: who walks and who finds the map confusing? Replace the unsupported safety promise with an accurate purpose and repair the clause boundary. Punctuate the supply list. Clarify or remove the ambiguous final sentence without inventing who arrived or what was helpful. Produce a 3–4 sentence notice with welcoming diction and one deliberate sentence-style choice; explain that choice.

Hint 1

A map does not walk. The facts support an opportunity to learn, not perfect safety. If a referent is unknown, removing a nonessential sentence is more honest than guessing.

Hint 2

You can begin, “If you find park maps confusing…” Use a period or appropriate conjunction instead of a comma splice. “Bring a map, a pencil, and water” clarifies the list. The final sentence can be omitted because its intended meaning is not supplied.

Compare / One explained response

Try your reasoning first. Paper or another document works too.

“If you find park maps confusing, join our Saturday beginner workshop. A trained leader has agreed to supervise. Bring a map, a pencil, and water.” The conditional opening identifies readers without embarrassing them. The short supply sentence makes the practical instructions clear. The revision removes the safety guarantee and ambiguous anecdote instead of inventing certainty. Another clear version could be equally effective.

Transfer to a different task

Adapt the notice for experienced hikers who might volunteer as peer helpers. Keep the same confirmed facts; do not invent a credential requirement, time, or registration link. Explain one stylistic change and proofread your clause boundaries.
