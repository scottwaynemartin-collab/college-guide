Period One — The Unmaking of Christendom

Europe stopped being one Christian world and became a set of rival sovereign states with ships, presses, and colonies.

01
The argument

The Throughline

The single argument here is that authority moved, and Christendom — the idea that Latin Europe was one Christian society under a pope and an emperor — did not survive the move. In 1450 a person’s loyalty was claimed at once by the Church, a distant emperor, a local lord, a guild, and a town, and none of them clearly outranked the rest. By 1648 the ruler of a defined territory had absorbed most of those claims: he taxed, judged, raised armies, licensed printers, and in much of Europe decided which church his subjects attended. Humanism, printing, the Reformation, and the Atlantic empires look like four separate stories, but each one weakened an institution that claimed universal authority and handed the pieces to states. That is why the period ends at a treaty rather than a cultural date — Westphalia is the moment Europe agreed to sort itself by sovereign borders instead of by one faith.

What is in this period

Renaissance and Exploration

Age of Reformation

03
Before and after

What Actually Changed

Each row is a concrete before-and-after you could defend on an exam.

At the start

A single Latin Church that defined Christian truth for all of western Europe, with the pope as its final judge.

→
By the end

A map of competing confessions — Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Anabaptist, Catholic — in which the ruler of each territory largely decided which one his subjects practiced.

What drove it. Luther’s 1517 protest spread through print faster than the Church could suppress it, and the Peace of Augsburg (1555) and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) turned the prince’s choice into law.
At the start

Books copied by hand, mostly in Latin, held by monasteries and universities and read by a small clerical elite.

→
By the end

Cheap printed books, pamphlets, and woodcuts in vernacular languages, produced for profit and read aloud to people who could not read themselves.

What drove it. Movable type from the 1450s collapsed the cost of a text and created a commercial market that no church or university controlled — which is why Luther’s ideas outran the machinery built to stop them.
At the start

Europe as the far end of trade routes it did not control, buying Asian goods through Muslim and Italian middlemen.

→
By the end

Europe as the operator of its own ocean routes — Portuguese trading posts in Asia, Spanish silver from the Americas, Dutch and English joint-stock companies chartered in 1600–02.

What drove it. Better hulls, the compass and astrolabe, printed charts, and shipboard cannon let Atlantic states go around the middlemen, and the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) shows they expected to divide the results in advance.
At the start

Wealth measured mostly in land and rents, in a continent whose population was still below what it had been before the Black Death.

→
By the end

Wealth measured increasingly in coin, credit, and shares, with population back to pre-plague levels and a Price Revolution driven by American silver.

What drove it. Bullion from Potosí plus population recovery pushed prices up faster than wages, which enriched merchants and landlords and squeezed anyone living on a wage.
At the start

Serfdom loosening across western Europe, because plague deaths had left survivors enough bargaining power to demand better terms.

→
By the end

A split continent — wage labor and freer tenancy in the west, serfdom hardened into law in Poland, Prussia, and Russia, and hereditary chattel slavery expanding in the Atlantic colonies.

What drove it. Western lords lost that bargaining fight; eastern lords, selling grain into western markets, won it — and colonial sugar and silver created a demand for permanent, heritable, racialized bondage that European law then defined.
At the start

Kings who had to bargain with nobles, church courts, and towns for every tax and every soldier, under overlapping claims from pope and Holy Roman emperor.

→
By the end

Sovereign states with royal taxation, permanent armies, and courts that outranked church and noble jurisdiction inside fixed borders.

What drove it. War became ruinously expensive — the Italian Wars, the Dutch Revolt, and the Thirty Years’ War all forced rulers to build tax and army machinery, and deals like the Concordat of Bologna (1516) and the Act of Supremacy (1534) put the church’s money and appointments under the crown.
04
Exam patterns

What to Watch For

  • Causation chains that run printing → Reformation → religious war → stronger states. The exam rewards the mechanism — cheap pamphlets, vernacular Bibles, princely enforcement — not a list of events in date order.
  • Comparison prompts, constantly. Italian versus Northern Renaissance; Lutheran versus Calvinist versus Anabaptist versus Catholic reform; Spanish versus Dutch versus English colonial models. Compare on stated criteria — doctrine, organization, who benefits — rather than describing each in turn.
  • The continuity half of continuity-and-change prompts. Most Europeans still farmed, the family was still the economic unit, and hierarchy still held. An answer that says everything changed after 1450 throws away half the question.
  • Art as a paid argument. Renaissance perspective, Holbein’s The Ambassadors, Cranach’s Luther woodcuts, and Baroque drama were all commissioned by someone. Ask who paid and what the image wants a viewer to believe — that is where sourcing points live.
  • The economics of expansion, not the voyages. The Columbian Exchange, the Price Revolution, joint-stock companies, and the Atlantic slave trade appear as DBQ documents far more often than Columbus does.
Where this leads. Westphalia settled that authority belongs to whoever rules the territory, but not how much of it one person should hold. Period 2 opens on exactly that argument — Louis XIV’s answer against Parliament’s — and on the colonial trade and warfare that paid for both.
