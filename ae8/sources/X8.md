EXCERPT IN THE BANK:

> We civilizations now know that we are mortal. … We feel that a civilization is as fragile as a life. …
> … So many horrors would have been impossible without so many virtues. Without doubt, it needed much science to kill so many men, waste so many possessions, and annihilate so many towns in so little time; but it needed no less moral qualities. Are Knowledge and Duty then suspect? …
> … The military crisis is perhaps at an end; the economic crisis is visibly at its zenith; but the intellectual crisis, more subtle, … it is with difficulty that we can seize the true centre, the exact phase, of this crisis. …
> … The facts, however, are clear and pitiless: there are thousands of young writers and young artists who are dead. There is the lost illusion of a European culture, and the demonstration of the impotence of knowledge to save anything whatever; there is science, mortally wounded in its moral ambitions, and as it were dishonoured by its applications …
— Paul Valéry, French poet and essayist, "The Spiritual Crisis," the first of two "Letters from France" published in English in a London magazine, April 1919 (English translation (translator not named) in The Athenaeum (London), no. 4641, April 11, 1919, pp. 182–183; adapted)

CONTEXT NOTES:
Valéry wrote the letters for the London Athenaeum, then edited by John Middleton Murry, in spring 1919, while the peace conference met in Paris. The opening sentence became one of the best-known statements of postwar European disillusion.
