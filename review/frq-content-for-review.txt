--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of thematic map used in the county-scale panel of the stimulus.
MODEL ANSWER: It is a choropleth map: each county is shaded one color based on which percent class it falls in.
RUBRIC: Names it: a choropleth map / Describes the method: areas shaded or colored by data class
MINI-LESSON (Spotting a choropleth):
 - A choropleth map fills in whole areas (countries, states, counties) with a color based on their data value.
 - The values are grouped into classes, and the legend tells you which color means which range.
 - It's great for rates and percents, like percent without internet, because it compares places fairly regardless of their size.
 - Its weakness: a whole county gets one color, even if conditions vary a lot inside it.
KEY WORDS: Thematic map = A map about one topic, like income or internet access.; Choropleth map = Areas shaded by data class.; Class = A range of values that share one color on the legend.
QUICK CHECK: Which map type shades whole areas by data class? → Choropleth (Choropleth maps color areas by value class. Dot density uses dots; graduated symbols use sized circles.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Using the map, describe how the pattern of households without home internet changes between the state scale and the county scale.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- At the state scale there is no pattern to see, because the whole state is one shade with a single value of 18%. E2- At the county scale the same data range from 6% in Calder City and 9% in Westbrook, clustered around the capital, up to 41% in Northfork and 44% in Dry Creek along the western and northern edges. A3- Zooming in reveals a clustered pattern of rural counties where more than a third of households lack internet, which the single state number completely hides.
RUBRIC: Describes the state scale as one value or one shade (18%) for the whole state / Describes the county-scale pattern with evidence: a wide range (6% to 44%), low near the capital or metro, high in rural or edge counties
MINI-LESSON (Reading a pattern at two scales):
 - Scale of analysis is the size of the units the data are divided into. Here: 1 state vs. 12 counties.
 - At the bigger unit, everything is averaged into one number, so you can't see a pattern at all.
 - At the smaller unit, you can describe the pattern: clustered (grouped together) or dispersed (spread out), and which places are high or low.
 - A good description names the range (lowest to highest), where the highs and lows sit, and what word fits the arrangement, like 'clustered around the capital.'
KEY WORDS: Scale of analysis = The size of the units you study: national, state, county, local.; Clustered = Grouped close together.; Dispersed = Spread out evenly.
QUICK CHECK: A pattern that looks uniform at the state scale but clustered at the county scale is an example of… → how scale of analysis changes what you see (Changing the size of the units (the scale of analysis) can reveal or hide patterns.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason rural counties in Calderon have higher rates of households without home internet than the counties around the capital.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Rural counties have higher rates of households without internet because their low population density makes internet infrastructure very expensive per customer. E2- In Dry Creek (44%) homes are spread miles apart, so a company must lay far more fiber-optic cable to reach each household than it does in dense Calder City (6%), where one line serves hundreds of homes. A3- Because the company earns less for every mile of cable in a rural county, it builds there last or not at all, so many rural households are left without service and the rate stays high.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: low population density / homes spread far apart, high cost of infrastructure per customer, distance from the city, or lower incomes / Explains the cause-and-effect chain to the higher rate
MINI-LESSON (Density, distance, and the cost of a connection):
 - Population density is the number of people per square mile. Rural counties have low density: homes sit far apart.
 - Internet service needs physical infrastructure, like fiber-optic cable or cell towers, and it costs money for every mile built.
 - In a dense city, one mile of cable can serve hundreds of paying customers. In a rural county it might serve five, so the company earns far less per mile.
 - Companies build where they make money first, so rural areas get service later, slower, or never. This is related to distance decay: the farther from the center, the weaker the connection.
 - Lower rural incomes can add to it: even where service exists, fewer families can afford it.
KEY WORDS: Population density = People per unit of land.; Infrastructure = The physical systems a place needs, like roads, pipes, and cables.; Distance decay = Interaction weakens as distance grows.
QUICK CHECK: Why does one mile of internet cable cost MORE per customer in a rural county? → Fewer homes are spread along that mile (Low population density means each mile of cable reaches fewer paying customers.)

--- Part F (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE way a dot density map or a graduated symbol map of the same data would show the pattern differently from the county choropleth.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A dot density map would show the number of households without internet rather than the percent in each county. E2- If each dot stood for 500 households and the dots were placed where people live, Calder City might have many dots even though its rate is only 6%, while Dry Creek at 44% might have only a few dots because so few people live there. A3- This matters because it shows where the largest total number of unconnected households is, which can be a different place from where the rate is highest, and the dots can hint at clustering inside a county instead of coloring the whole county one shade.
RUBRIC: Names the map type: dot density or graduated/proportional symbol / Describes a real difference: it shows counts (number of households) instead of a rate, so metro counties could look bigger; or dots show where people cluster inside a county
MINI-LESSON (Rate vs. count: why the map type matters):
 - A choropleth map is best for rates and percents, because it compares places fairly no matter how big or crowded they are.
 - A dot density map places one dot per set number of things (say, 500 households). Dots pile up where there are more people and can show clustering inside a county.
 - A graduated (proportional) symbol map puts a circle on each county and sizes it by the number, so the biggest circle goes to the county with the most unconnected households.
 - On the Calderon map, Calder City has the lowest percent (6%) but the most people, so it could have lots of dots or a large circle, while rural Dry Creek (44%) might look small.
 - Always ask: does this map show how many, or what share?
KEY WORDS: Dot density map = One dot for each set number of things, placed where they are.; Graduated symbol map = Symbols sized by the data value.; Rate = A share or percent, not a raw count.
QUICK CHECK: Which map type is usually used to show COUNTS placed where things actually are? → Dot density (Dot density maps put one dot per set number of things. Choropleths shade areas by rate.)

MODEL ANSWER: T1- Better internet is an example of time-space compression because it shrinks the time it takes to connect a rural place with distant ones, so distance matters less. E2- A family in Dry Creek that once drove two hours to see a specialist in Calder City could have a video visit in minutes, and a student there could take an online course from a university anywhere. A3- One effect is that rural residents can reach jobs, health care, and education without leaving, which could help young people stay in the community and let local businesses sell to customers far away, slowing rural population loss.

MINI-LESSON (Time-space compression, explained with Dry Creek):
 - Time-space compression is the idea that new transportation and communication technology cuts the time it takes to interact across distance, so the world feels smaller.
 - It doesn't change the miles. It changes relative distance: how connected places feel.
 - Without broadband, a Dry Creek family is far from everything: doctors, colleges, jobs, and customers. With it, those things are a click away.
 - Possible effects: remote work and online classes, telehealth, local businesses selling nationwide, and young people staying instead of leaving.
 - It can cut both ways: local stores may lose shoppers to big online companies.
KEY WORDS: Time-space compression = Technology shrinking the time it takes to connect distant places.; Relative distance = How far apart places feel based on time, cost, or connection, not miles.; Telehealth = Seeing a doctor by video or phone.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Types of thematic maps
   - A thematic map shows one topic (a theme), like income or internet access, instead of just roads and borders.
   - A choropleth map shades whole areas, like counties or countries, by class: darker or hotter colors usually mean higher values. It works best with rates and percents.
   - A dot density map places one dot for every set number of things (say, 500 households), so you see counts and where they cluster inside an area.
   - A graduated (proportional) symbol map puts a circle on each place and makes the circle bigger when the number is bigger.
   - An isoline map connects points of equal value (like elevation or temperature), and a cartogram stretches or shrinks places by a data value instead of land area.
• Why patterns change with scale
   - Scale of analysis is the size of the units you look at: one country, 50 states, 3,000 counties, or individual neighborhoods.
   - When data for many places are rolled into one bigger unit, the values get averaged, and places with lots of people pull the average toward their number.
   - That is why a state can look 'fine' at 18% while some of its counties are over 40%: the crowded metro counties with low rates outweigh the thinly populated rural ones.
   - Zooming in to a smaller unit reveals clusters, gaps, and outliers that the bigger unit hides. Zooming out smooths them away.
   - On the AP exam, always ask: at what scale is this data, and what could be hiding inside each unit?
• Distance decay & population density
   - Distance decay is the idea that interaction between places drops off as the distance between them grows. Farther away usually means fewer connections and fewer services.
   - Population density is the number of people per unit of land. Rural counties have low density: homes are spread far apart.
   - Networks like roads, water pipes, and fiber-optic internet cost money per mile. In a dense city one mile of cable can serve hundreds of homes; in a rural county it may serve five.
   - Because companies earn less per mile in low-density places, they build there last or not at all, which leaves rural areas with weaker service.
   - Lower average incomes in many rural areas can add to the problem, since fewer households can afford a monthly plan even where service exists.
• GIS, GPS & remote sensing
   - GIS (geographic information system) is computer software that stores map data in layers, like clear sheets stacked on a light table, so you can combine and analyze them.
   - A city might stack layers for roads, population density, income, flood zones, and existing utilities, then ask the software to find spots where several conditions overlap.
   - GPS (global positioning system) uses satellites to give the exact location of a device. It collects points; GIS analyzes them.
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites or aircraft, like images of crops, forests, or city growth.
   - Governments use these tools to plan services and respond to disasters; businesses use them to choose store locations and plan delivery routes.
• Time-space compression
   - Time-space compression is the idea that new transportation and communication technology shrinks the time it takes to connect distant places, so the world feels smaller.
   - The telegraph, jet planes, and the internet each made far-away places easier and faster to reach.
   - It does not shrink actual distance; it reduces the 'friction' of distance. Geographers call this a change in relative distance between places.
   - Places with weak connections, like rural areas without broadband, are left out of this shrinking world, which is a form of spatial inequality.
   - Better connections can let people in remote places reach jobs, doctors, and schools without moving, but they can also pull local shoppers toward big online companies.
• Three types of regions
   - A region is an area that shares one or more features. Geographers sort regions into three kinds.
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or a shared measurable trait everywhere inside it, like a county, a state, or the French-speaking part of Canada.
   - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a center, or node, like a metro area tied to its downtown, a pizza delivery zone, or the area a radio station reaches.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like 'the South' or 'upstate.'
   - On the Calderon map, each county is a formal region, while the capital and the suburbs it serves form a functional region.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Total Fertility Rate (TFR)
   - TFR is the average number of children a woman is expected to have in her lifetime.
   - A TFR of about 2.1 is replacement level, just enough for each generation to replace itself. The extra 0.1 covers children who die young.
   - Above 2.1, population tends to grow. Below 2.1, it eventually shrinks unless immigrants arrive.
   - TFR is one of the best single numbers for predicting a country's future population.
• Demographic Transition Model (DTM)
   - The DTM describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops.
   - Stage 1: both rates are high, so population barely grows.
   - Stage 2: death rates drop (better food, medicine, sanitation) but birth rates stay high, so population grows fast.
   - Stage 3: birth rates fall as the country urbanizes and women gain education.
   - Stages 4–5: both rates are low, and population levels off or even shrinks.
• Women's education & fertility
   - Worldwide, one of the strongest predictors of lower fertility is how many years of school girls complete.
   - Staying in school tends to delay marriage and first births.
   - Education opens paid jobs, so each extra child has a higher opportunity cost (the income a mother gives up).
   - Educated women usually know more about health care and family planning and have more say in family decisions.
• Urbanization & family size
   - On farms, children can help with work, so a big family can be an economic asset.
   - In cities, children usually don't earn income, and housing, food, and school cost more.
   - Cities also offer more clinics, contraception, and paid jobs for women.
   - That's why fertility usually falls as a country becomes more urban.
• Population pyramids
   - A population pyramid is a bar graph of a population by age and sex, with the youngest at the bottom.
   - A wide base means many births, which is common in Stage 2 countries.
   - When fertility falls, the base narrows and the shape turns more like a beehive or column.
   - Large age groups (cohorts) move up the pyramid over time like a bulge.
• Dependency ratio
   - The dependency ratio compares people who usually aren't working (children under 15 and adults 65+) with the working-age population (15–64).
   - A high elderly dependency ratio means fewer workers paying taxes to support each retiree's pension and health care.
   - Very-low-fertility countries such as Japan and Italy face rising elderly dependency ratios.
   - Common responses include encouraging immigration, raising the retirement age, and supporting families who want children.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Lingua franca
   - A lingua franca is a shared language people use when they don't speak the same first language.
   - The name comes from an old trade language used around the Mediterranean Sea.
   - Swahili in East Africa and Arabic across the Middle East have both served as lingua francas.
   - Today English is the most widely used global lingua franca: in business, science, air travel, and the internet.
• Types of diffusion
   - Relocation diffusion: people move and bring their language or culture with them.
   - Expansion diffusion: an idea spreads outward while staying strong where it started.
   - Its 3 kinds: hierarchical (from powerful people or big cities first), contagious (person to person, like a virus), and stimulus (the idea spreads but gets changed to fit local culture).
   - English spread by relocation (British colonists) and hierarchical diffusion (elites, schools, and businesses first).
• Outsourcing & offshoring
   - Outsourcing means hiring another company to do part of your work. Offshoring means moving work to another country.
   - Companies offshore to cut labor costs, since wages are lower in many developing countries.
   - India and the Philippines became call-center hubs because they have many English-speaking workers.

• Language families

• Measuring development
   - GDP per capita is the value of everything a country produces, divided by its population.
   - HDI (Human Development Index) combines income, education, and life expectancy.
   - Cultural data, like which language people speak, doesn't directly measure development.
   - Always ask: does this data actually measure what the question is asking about?
• Cultural homogenization
   - Globalization spreads the same brands, music, movies, and languages around the world.
   - When global culture replaces local culture, places can start to look and feel alike.
   - Ethnologue estimates that about 40% of the world's 7,000+ languages are endangered.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Sequent occupance
   - Sequent occupance is the idea that a place is shaped by every group that has ever lived there, one layer at a time.
   - Each group leaves its mark on the cultural landscape: buildings, signs, foods, places of worship, street names.
   - The next group doesn't erase everything. It reuses and adds to what's already there, like a Lutheran church becoming a Catholic parish.
   - Cultural landscape just means the visible, human-made look of a place.
• Ethnic enclaves
   - An ethnic enclave (or ethnic neighborhood) is an area where one ethnic group clusters together in a city.
   - Immigrants settle near people who share their language, religion, and food because it feels safer and makes daily life easier.
   - Enclaves fill with businesses that serve the group: bakeries, groceries, newspapers, and radio stations in the home language.
   - Many U.S. cities have had a “Little Italy,” a Chinatown, or a German district. Over time one group moves out and another moves in: ethnic succession.
• Acculturation, assimilation, syncretism
   - Acculturation: a group adopts some traits of the surrounding culture but keeps its own identity (speaking English at work, Spanish at home).
   - Assimilation: a group blends in so fully that its original culture mostly disappears, often by the third generation.
   - Syncretism: two cultures mix and create something new. Día de los Muertos itself blends Indigenous Aztec traditions with Catholic All Souls' Day.
   - Commodification: a cultural practice is turned into something to sell, like a festival run for tourists. When a practice spreads but changes to fit the new group, that's also stimulus diffusion.
• Religion in the landscape
   - Religion is one of the easiest cultural traits to spot in a landscape: churches, mosques, temples, shrines, and cemeteries.
   - Universalizing religions like Christianity and Islam try to appeal to everyone, so they spread widely and build in many places.
   - Ethnic religions like Hinduism and Judaism are tied to one group or place and spread mostly by migration.
   - Even a single building can show history: a church may change denomination when the neighborhood changes. Festivals and shrines count too.
• Centripetal vs. centrifugal forces
   - Centripetal forces pull a group together: a shared language, religion, media, or celebration.
   - Centrifugal forces push a group apart: tension between newcomers and old-timers, different languages, or feeling like outsiders in your own area.
   - An ethnic radio station or newspaper is a classic centripetal force for an immigrant community.
   - Rapid neighborhood change can be centrifugal when long-time residents feel their culture is being replaced. These terms return in Unit 4.
• Popular vs. local culture
   - Local (folk) culture belongs to a specific place or group, is passed down in person, and changes slowly.
   - Popular culture spreads fast through media and big companies and looks similar everywhere: chain coffee shops, trending music, the same apps.
   - Globalization helps popular culture spread, which can crowd out local traditions, a process called cultural homogenization.
   - But the same technology can preserve local culture: communities record recipes, stream festivals, and post heritage-language videos online.
• How to read a text stimulus
   - Read the passage twice. The first time, just follow the story. The second time, hunt for geography terms you know.
   - Underline every place, group, building, and date. FRQ questions almost always point back to those details.
   - Notice what CHANGES between the time periods and what STAYS. That's where most of the points hide.
   - When a question says “using the passage,” quote or paraphrase a specific detail as your E2 evidence.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Cash crops & plantations
   - A cash crop is grown to sell, often for export, rather than to feed the farmer's own family.
   - Colonial powers pushed colonies to specialize in a few crops their home factories needed, like cotton, jute (for sacks and rope), and tea.
   - Plantations are large estates that grow one crop for export, often with cheap or forced labor. British-owned tea estates in Assam are an example.
   - Depending on one or two exports leaves a region exposed to world prices it can't control.
• Colonial port cities
   - Colonizers built port cities as gateways, where raw materials left the colony and manufactured goods came in.
   - Bombay (Mumbai), Calcutta (Kolkata), and Madras (Chennai) grew from small trading posts into major cities under British rule.
   - Calcutta was the capital of British India until 1911, when the capital moved to Delhi.
   - Many former colonies still have a primate city, one city far larger than all the others, that began as a colonial port.
• Famines in colonial India
   - Several deadly famines struck British India in the late 1800s. The Great Famine of 1876–78 alone killed millions of people.
   - Droughts caused the crop failures, but colonial policy made them worse: grain kept being exported, and relief was limited.
   - Farmers who grew cash crops had little stored food. They depended on wages and market prices to buy it.
   - Historians point to these famines as evidence that colonial economic choices shaped life-and-death outcomes.
• Wallerstein's world-system theory
   - Immanuel Wallerstein divided the world economy into the core, the semi-periphery, and the periphery.
   - Core countries are wealthy and industrialized, and they make high-value goods.
   - Periphery countries supply raw materials and cheap labor, and they earn far less.
   - Colonialism helped build this pattern by locking colonies into shipping raw materials to the core.
   - The theory's big idea: countries are linked, and the core's wealth depends partly on the periphery.
• Uneven development
   - Development is rarely spread evenly inside a country.
   - Places that get early investment, such as ports, railways, and factories, attract more businesses and workers, which attracts even more investment.
   - Geographers call this snowball effect cumulative causation.
   - The result is a core region that keeps growing while peripheral regions fall behind, and the gap can last for generations.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The von Thünen model
   - Johann Heinrich von Thünen was a German landowner who published The Isolated State in 1826 to explain why farms near a city grow different things than farms far away.
   - He imagined one market city on a flat plain with the same soil and climate everywhere, no rivers or roads, and farmers hauling goods by wagon.
   - His rings, from the center out: market gardening and dairy, forest (for firewood and lumber), grains and field crops, then livestock ranching.
   - The big idea: the farther you are from the market, the more it costs to get your product there, so land use changes with distance.
   - Real places never match the rings exactly, but the logic of distance and transport cost still shows up everywhere.
• Bid-rent & transport cost
   - Bid-rent is the most a user is willing to pay for land at a given distance from the market.
   - Land near the city is expensive because many people want it; land far away is cheap.
   - A farmer whose product is perishable (milk, lettuce) or bulky (timber) saves the most money by being close, so they can outbid others for near-city land.
   - A rancher's cattle can walk or ride to market cheaply, so the rancher can't outbid anyone near the city and ends up on cheap distant land.
   - Put together, those bidding wars create the rings.
• Global food supply chains
   - A commodity chain is every step a product takes from the farm to your plate: growing, processing, shipping, selling.
   - Refrigerated trucks and containers (the “cold chain”) keep food fresh for thousands of miles, so perishables no longer have to be grown nearby.
   - Interstate highways, container ships, and air freight make long-distance shipping cheap and fast.
   - That's why a U.S. grocery store sells Chilean grapes in January and Mexican tomatoes year-round.
   - Large agribusiness companies often own several steps of the chain, which is called vertical integration.
• The Green Revolution
   - Starting in the 1940s–1960s, scientists like Norman Borlaug bred high-yield varieties of wheat and rice.
   - Those seeds only produce big harvests with lots of chemical fertilizer, pesticides, and irrigation water.
   - Mexico, India, and the Philippines sharply raised their food output, and famine deaths dropped.
   - Costs: polluted water, drained aquifers, lost crop diversity, and debt for poor farmers who couldn't afford the inputs.
   - Sub-Saharan Africa benefited least, partly because its main crops and dry climates didn't fit the new seeds.
• Farming's environmental costs
   - Fertilizer runoff carries nitrogen and phosphorus into rivers, feeding algae blooms that create oxygen-poor “dead zones,” like the one in the Gulf of Mexico fed by the Mississippi River.
   - Pumping groundwater for irrigation faster than rain refills it depletes aquifers, such as the Ogallala Aquifer under the U.S. Great Plains.
   - Irrigating in dry climates leaves salt behind as water evaporates (salinization), which can ruin soil.
   - Plowing and bare fields speed up soil erosion; the 1930s Dust Bowl is the classic example.
   - Clearing forest for cattle pasture and soybeans drives deforestation in the Amazon.
• Intensive vs. extensive, commercial vs. subsistence
   - Intensive agriculture puts lots of labor or money into a small piece of land: dairy farms, vegetable farms, rice paddies.
   - Extensive agriculture uses a lot of land with little labor per acre: cattle ranching, shifting cultivation, nomadic herding.
   - Commercial farmers grow crops to sell; subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families.
   - Most farms in wealthy countries are commercial; many farms in less developed countries are still subsistence or a mix.
   - Von Thünen's model assumes commercial farmers selling to one market, which is why it fits some places better than others.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Suburbanization & urban sprawl
   - Suburbanization is the movement of people and homes from the central city outward to the suburbs that ring it.
   - In the United States it boomed after World War II: cheap cars, the Interstate Highway System (begun 1956), and FHA and VA government-backed mortgages made a new house far from downtown easy to afford.
   - Urban sprawl is what that growth looks like on the ground: low-density, car-dependent subdivisions, strip malls, and parking lots spreading over former farmland.
   - Consequences include long commutes and traffic, air pollution, loss of farmland and habitat, the high cost of stretching roads and sewers outward, and metros sorted by income.
   - Edge cities (big clusters of offices and malls at highway exits) grew where the sprawl met the beltways.
• Urban land-use models
   - Burgess concentric zone model (1920s Chicago): rings around a central business district (CBD): a zone of transition with old housing and factories, then working-class homes, then nicer homes, then a commuter zone.
   - Hoyt sector model (1939): land uses grow in wedges along railroads and major roads, so a high-rent sector can stretch from downtown to the edge.
   - Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model (1945): a city has several centers (airport, university, industrial park, suburban business district), not just one downtown.
   - Galactic or peripheral model: an old core surrounded by a highway beltway with edge cities and suburbs scattered along it, the classic post-1970 American metro.
   - Latin American city model: a wealthy spine runs out from the CBD, while squatter settlements and disamenity zones sit on the far edge.
• Gentrification
   - Gentrification happens when wealthier, often younger, residents move into an older lower-income neighborhood near downtown, renovate the housing, and open new businesses.
   - It is pulled by cheap older homes, short commutes to downtown jobs, walkable streets, and a taste for city life among young professionals and retirees.
   - Upsides: higher property values, more tax money for the city, new shops, and reinvestment in buildings that had been neglected.
   - Downsides: rents and property taxes climb, so long-time lower-income residents, especially renters, are displaced, and the neighborhood's culture and businesses change.
   - Many gentrifying neighborhoods were once redlined, which is why their housing was cheap enough to attract investors in the first place.
• Zoning & single-family-only rules
   - Zoning is the set of local laws that say what can be built on each piece of land: homes, shops, factories, and how big or tall.
   - Single-family-only zoning allows just one detached house per lot, so duplexes, townhouses, and apartment buildings are illegal there.
   - In many U.S. cities most residential land is zoned this way, which caps how many homes can fit even when demand is high.
   - Because it holds down supply and keeps out cheaper housing types, critics call it exclusionary zoning; it is one reason housing is so expensive in many metros.
   - Minneapolis and the state of Oregon have ended single-family-only zoning so that duplexes and triplexes can be built on any residential lot.
• Smart growth & New Urbanism
   - Smart growth is a set of planning policies that fight sprawl by making metros compact, walkable, and transit-friendly.
   - Tools include urban growth boundaries (Portland, Oregon has had one since 1979) and greenbelts that protect open land at the edge, transit-oriented development around stations, mixed-use zoning, and infill building on vacant or brownfield (former industrial) lots.
   - New Urbanism is the design movement behind many of these ideas: walkable blocks, homes close to shops and schools, front porches, and a mix of housing types, as in Seaside, Florida and Kentlands, Maryland.
   - Supporters say these policies cut driving and protect farmland; critics worry that limiting land for building can push housing prices up unless cities also allow more homes inside the boundary.
• Redlining, blockbusting & white flight
   - In the 1930s a federal agency (the HOLC) drew maps grading neighborhoods for mortgage risk; areas with Black and immigrant residents were colored red and labeled 'hazardous,' so banks refused to lend there. That is redlining.
   - Blockbusting: real-estate agents frightened white homeowners into selling cheap by warning that Black families were moving in, then resold the homes to Black buyers at high prices.
   - White flight: millions of white families left central cities for the suburbs from the 1950s to the 1970s, helped by highways and FHA loans that redlined neighborhoods could not get.
   - The Fair Housing Act of 1968 banned housing discrimination, but the legacy remains: disinvested neighborhoods, lower home values, and a large racial wealth gap.
   - Today many formerly redlined neighborhoods near downtown are the ones gentrifying.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Measures of development
   - GNI per capita is a country's total income divided by its population. “PPP” (purchasing power parity) adjusts for the fact that a dollar buys more in some countries than in others.
   - HDI, the Human Development Index, combines income, years of schooling, and life expectancy into one score from 0 to 1. Above 0.80 counts as “very high” development.
   - Other common measures: literacy rate, infant mortality rate, and the share of workers in each economic sector.
   - Income measures what a country earns; HDI measures whether that money reaches people's health and education. When the two disagree, something interesting is going on.
• Rostow's stages of growth
   - W. W. Rostow (1960) said every country moves through five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and high mass consumption.
   - Take-off is the big moment: a few industries grow fast, cities swell, and the country starts to industrialize.
   - High mass consumption is the top stage: most workers are in services, and people spend on cars, electronics, and vacations.
   - Critics say the model is based on the history of the United States and Western Europe, assumes every country can follow the same path, and ignores how colonialism and global trade can hold countries back.
   - Wallerstein's world-system theory answers that criticism by looking at the whole world at once: a wealthy core, a semi-periphery, and a periphery that supplies raw materials and cheap labor.
• Gender Inequality Index
   - The Gender Inequality Index (GII) is a UN measure from 0 to 1 that compares women and men. Lower is better; 0 would mean full equality.
   - It combines three things: reproductive health (maternal deaths and teen births), empowerment (seats in parliament and secondary schooling), and participation in the paid workforce.
   - Countries with a high GII usually have fewer girls finishing school, fewer women in paid jobs, and more early marriages.
   - Development experts argue that educating and employing women is one of the fastest ways to raise a country's HDI, which is why microloans to women, like those from Bangladesh's Grameen Bank, are so popular.
• Commodity dependence
   - A commodity is a raw product sold in bulk at a world price: oil, copper, coffee, cocoa, cotton.
   - A country is commodity-dependent when most of its export earnings come from one or two commodities. Nigeria and Venezuela, for example, get most of theirs from oil.
   - World prices for commodities swing wildly. Oil fell from over $100 a barrel in 2014 to under $30 in early 2016.
   - When the price drops, the country's income, government budget, and jobs all drop at once. Some geographers call this trap the “resource curse.”
   - The usual advice is to diversify: build manufacturing and services so that one price can't sink the whole economy.
• Special economic zones
   - A special economic zone (SEZ) is an area where a government relaxes its normal rules to attract foreign factories: lower taxes, fewer tariffs, less paperwork.
   - An export processing zone (EPZ) is a type of SEZ focused on assembling goods for export. Mexico's maquiladoras along the U.S. border are a famous example.
   - China's first SEZ, Shenzhen, grew from a fishing town of about 30,000 people in 1980 into a city of more than 17 million.
   - Companies get cheap labor and easy exporting; the host country gets jobs, technology, and foreign investment.
   - Downsides can include low wages, weak worker protections, pollution, and companies that leave once the tax breaks end.
• Economic sectors
   - Primary sector: taking things from the earth (farming, fishing, mining). Secondary: making things (factories, construction). Tertiary: services (stores, schools, hospitals, banks).
   - Some books add quaternary (information and research) and quinary (top-level decision-making).
   - As a country develops, workers shift from primary to secondary to tertiary. Rich countries usually have under 5% of workers in farming and over 70% in services.
   - A big share of workers in agriculture usually signals low incomes, because farms in poor countries produce little per worker and most of it is eaten, not sold.
