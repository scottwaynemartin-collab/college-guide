Unit 1 · Mapping the Storm (Practice FRQ · No stimulus)
======================================================================
STIMULUS: (no stimulus)
You're interning at a Gulf Coast county's emergency-management office before hurricane season. Your supervisor wants you to master the geographer's toolkit: maps, locations, regions, and how people and the environment shape each other.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of region that “the South” in the United States is an example of.
MODEL ANSWER: “The South” is a perceptual (vernacular) region: it exists in people's minds, so its borders are fuzzy and change depending on who you ask.
RUBRIC: Names it: a perceptual (vernacular) region / Describes it: exists in people's minds, with fuzzy borders
MINI-LESSON (The three types of regions):
 - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana.
 - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a central node, like a city's commuter zone.
 - A perceptual (vernacular) region is based on what people believe and feel, so its borders are fuzzy, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
 - Clue: if people argue about its edges and it's tied to identity, it's perceptual.
KEY WORDS: Perceptual (vernacular) region = A region based on shared identity and feelings, with fuzzy borders.; Formal region = A region with an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is a PERCEPTUAL region? → “The Midwest” (The Midwest's borders depend on who you ask. Ohio is formal; a delivery zone is functional.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe how the Mercator projection distorts the size of Greenland compared with the size of Africa.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Mercator projection stretches the area of places near the poles, so they look much bigger than they really are. E2- On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Greenland is about 2.2 million km² while Africa is about 30 million km², roughly 14 times larger. A3- Because Greenland is near the North Pole and Africa straddles the equator, people who use only Mercator maps can badly underestimate Africa's true size.
RUBRIC: Describes the distortion: Greenland looks about as big as Africa / Gives the truth or the reason: Africa is about 14 times bigger, or Mercator stretches areas near the poles
MINI-LESSON (Why Mercator makes Greenland huge):
 - Every map projection distorts shape, area, distance, or direction when it flattens the globe.
 - Gerardus Mercator made his map in 1569 for sailors: it keeps compass directions and local shapes correct.
 - To do that, it stretches places more and more toward the poles, so Greenland looks as big as Africa, which is really about 14 times larger.
 - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes; Robinson compromises, keeping distortion fairly small except near the poles.
KEY WORDS: Map projection = A way of showing the curved Earth on a flat map.; Distortion = A change in shape, area, distance, or direction caused by flattening the globe.
QUICK CHECK: Which projection keeps the AREA of every country correct? → Gall-Peters (Gall-Peters is an equal-area projection. Mercator keeps shape and direction; Robinson compromises.)

--- Part C (Describe) ---
Q: Describe the difference between a city's site and its situation, using New Orleans or another real city as your example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A city's site is the physical land it sits on, while its situation is its location compared to other places. E2- New Orleans's site is low, swampy land in a bend of the Mississippi River, and about half the city is below sea level. Its situation is near the river's mouth on the Gulf of Mexico, which makes it a port linking the central United States to world trade. A3- The site explains New Orleans's flood risk, while the situation explains why people built a major city there anyway.
RUBRIC: Describes SITE: the physical features of your real city itself / Describes SITUATION: that same city's location relative to other places and its connections / Uses ONE named real city for both site and situation
MINI-LESSON (Site vs. situation):
 - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
 - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: nearby rivers, roads, trade routes, and cities.
 - The French founded New Orleans in 1718 on a bend of the Mississippi surrounded by swamps, so its site is low and flood-prone.
 - Its situation is excellent: near the river's mouth, it became a port shipping goods from the middle of the country to the world.
KEY WORDS: Site = The physical features of a place's exact location.; Situation = A place's location relative to other places, and its connections to them.
QUICK CHECK: “Chicago grew because canals and railroads connected it to the rest of the country.” This describes Chicago's… → situation (Connections to other places are situation. Site would be the flat lakeshore land itself.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how a county government could use remote sensing to respond to a hurricane after it makes landfall.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A county could use remote sensing, which collects images of Earth from satellites, planes, or drones, to see storm damage without sending people into danger. E2- After a hurricane, before-and-after images show flooded neighborhoods, destroyed homes, and blocked roads; NOAA, for example, flies planes over the coast to photograph hurricane damage. A3- Knowing where the worst damage is lets the county send rescue crews, water, and generators there first, saving time and lives.
RUBRIC: Says what remote sensing collects: images from satellites, aircraft, or drones / Explains the response: finding flooding or damage, then sending rescue, supplies, or repairs there
MINI-LESSON (Remote sensing, GPS, and GIS):
 - Remote sensing collects information about Earth from a distance, usually with satellites, airplanes, or drones.
 - Before-and-after images show where floods, fires, or storms did damage, even where roads are cut off.
 - GPS is different: it uses satellite signals to find a device's location, like a rescue boat's latitude and longitude, usually to within a few meters.
 - GIS is software that layers map data, like damage images on top of road maps.
KEY WORDS: Remote sensing = Collecting information about Earth's surface from a distance.; GPS = A satellite system that finds a device's location (latitude and longitude).; GIS = Software that layers and analyzes map data.
QUICK CHECK: A rescue boat's screen shows its current latitude and longitude. Which tool is that? → GPS (GPS uses satellite signals to find a device's location. Remote sensing gathers images from a distance.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason a functional region, such as a hospital's service area or a city's commuter zone, can change in size over time.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A functional region can grow when new transportation makes its node faster to reach. E2- For example, when Washington, D.C.'s Metro Silver Line was extended to Dulles Airport and Loudoun County, Virginia, in 2022, people in those outer suburbs could ride a train into the city. A3- Because a functional region is defined by connections to its node, faster connections let people come from farther away, so the commuter zone's edge moves outward and the region grows.
RUBRIC: Names a cause: new transportation, a competing node, or new technology / Explains how the edge moves: people come from farther (it grows) or switch to a closer node (it shrinks)
MINI-LESSON (Functional regions on the move):
 - A functional (nodal) region is held together by connections to a central node, like commuters going downtown.
 - Its edge is wherever those connections fade out, not a line set by law.
 - New highways or train lines let people reach the node from farther away, so the region grows.
 - A new competing node, like a new hospital in the next town, pulls people away, so the old region shrinks.
KEY WORDS: Node = The central point a functional region is organized around.; Functional (nodal) region = An area tied together by connections to a node.
QUICK CHECK: What holds a functional region together? → Connections to a central node (Official borders mean formal; shared feelings mean perceptual.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE difference between environmental determinism and possibilism, using a real-world example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Environmental determinism says the environment controls how people live and develop, while possibilism says it only sets limits and people choose how to respond. E2- For example, about a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, but the Dutch built dikes, pumps, and polders (low land walled in by dikes and pumped dry, often a former lake, marsh, or seabed) to farm and live there. A3- A determinist would expect such flood-prone land to hold a society back, but the Netherlands became one of the world's wealthiest countries, which supports possibilism.
RUBRIC: Describes determinism: the environment controls what people and societies can do / Describes possibilism with an example: people choose how to adapt, often with technology
MINI-LESSON (Who's in charge: nature or people?):
 - Environmental determinism claims climate and landforms control people's behavior and development.
 - Geographers rejected it by the mid-1900s: it ignored culture and choice and was used to justify colonialism.
 - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to use it, like the Dutch farming land below sea level.
 - Sustainability adds a warning: changing nature has costs, so meet today's needs without ruining the environment for the future.
KEY WORDS: Environmental determinism = The idea that the environment controls human behavior and development.; Possibilism = The idea that nature sets limits, but people choose how to adapt.; Sustainability = Meeting today's needs without harming future generations.
QUICK CHECK: “Hot climates make people lazy, so tropical countries can't develop.” This claim is an example of… → environmental determinism (Claiming climate controls behavior is determinism, which geographers reject.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE limitation of using only quantitative data, such as census numbers, to understand a place after a hurricane, and how qualitative data could help.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One limitation of using only quantitative data, like census numbers, is that they show how many people were affected, but not why those people made their choices. E2- For example, the 2010 census counted about 140,000 fewer people in New Orleans than in 2000, after Hurricane Katrina, but it can't show why some families returned and others never did. A3- Qualitative data, like interviews with residents and field observation, could reveal reasons such as rebuilding costs or ties to family and church, helping leaders plan a better recovery.
RUBRIC: Names a limitation: census counts alone don't show people's reasons, feelings, or experiences / Explains what qualitative data adds: interviews, field observation, or stories
MINI-LESSON (Two kinds of data):
 - Quantitative data are numbers: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
 - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and oral histories.
 - Field observation means going to a place in person to look, listen, and take notes or photos.
 - Counts like census totals show the pattern; qualitative data explain the reasons and human experience behind it, in people's own words. (A survey can also ask people to pick a reason from a list, which turns reasons into numbers.)
KEY WORDS: Quantitative data = Information in numbers that can be counted or measured.; Qualitative data = Descriptive information, like interviews, observations, and stories.; Field observation = Studying a place in person.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is QUALITATIVE data? → An interview with a family about why they rebuilt (Interviews describe experiences and reasons. Counts and averages are quantitative.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Map projections & distortion
   - Every map projection, a way of drawing the round Earth on flat paper, distorts shape, area, distance, or direction.
   - Mercator (1569) keeps shapes and compass directions true for sailors, but stretches areas more and more toward the poles.
   - On a Mercator map, Greenland (about 2.2 million km²) looks about as big as Africa (about 30 million km²), though Africa is about 14 times larger.
   - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes. Robinson compromises: it keeps every kind of distortion fairly small across most of the map, but shapes and sizes get more stretched near the poles.
• Formal, functional & perceptual regions
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana or the Corn Belt.
   - A functional (nodal) region is tied together by connections to a central node, like a TV station's viewing area or a city's commuter zone.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
   - New Orleans is in all three: Louisiana (formal), its own commuter zone (functional), and “the South” (perceptual).
• Location, site & situation
   - Absolute location is a place's exact spot, given by latitude and longitude: New Orleans is at about 30°N, 90°W.
   - Relative location describes a place compared to other places: New Orleans is near the mouth of the Mississippi River.
   - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
   - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: its connections to rivers, roads, trade routes, and other cities.
• Remote sensing vs. GPS vs. GIS
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites, airplanes, or drones.
   - Landsat satellites have photographed Earth since 1972, so before-and-after images can reveal floods, burned forests, or growing cities.
   - GPS uses satellite signals to find a device's location (its latitude and longitude), usually to within a few meters. GIS is software that layers and analyzes map data.
   - Remote sensing takes the picture, GPS finds the spot, and GIS puts it all together.
• Determinism, possibilism & sustainability
   - Environmental determinism is the old, rejected idea that the environment, especially climate, controls how people behave and develop.
   - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to respond through culture and technology.
   - It goes both ways: levees protect New Orleans but stop the Mississippi from spreading new mud on its delta, one reason coastal Louisiana has lost about 2,000 square miles of land since the 1930s.
   - Sustainability means meeting today's needs without ruining the environment for future generations, like restoring coastal wetlands that soften storm surges.
• Quantitative vs. qualitative data
   - Quantitative data are numbers you can count or measure: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
   - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and people's stories.
   - Field observation means visiting a place in person to look, listen, and record what you find.
   - Counts like census totals show how much and where; qualitative data help explain why, in people's own words.

--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of thematic map used in the county-scale panel of the stimulus.
MODEL ANSWER: It is a choropleth map: each county is shaded one color based on which percent class it falls in.
RUBRIC: Names it: a choropleth map / Describes the method: areas shaded or colored by data class
MINI-LESSON (Spotting a choropleth):
 - A choropleth map fills in whole areas (countries, states, counties) with a color based on their data value.
 - The values are grouped into classes, and the legend tells you which color means which range.
 - It's great for rates and percents, like percent without internet, because it compares places fairly regardless of their size.
 - Its weakness: a whole county gets one color, even if conditions vary a lot inside it.
KEY WORDS: Thematic map = A map about one topic, like income or internet access.; Choropleth map = Areas shaded by data class.; Class = A range of values that share one color on the legend.

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason rural counties in Calderon have higher rates of households without home internet than the counties around the capital.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Rural counties have higher rates of households without internet because their low population density makes internet infrastructure very expensive per customer. E2- In Dry Creek (44%) homes are spread miles apart, so a company must lay far more fiber-optic cable to reach each household than it does in dense Calder City (6%), where one line serves hundreds of homes. A3- Because the company earns less for every mile of cable in a rural county, it builds there last or not at all, so many rural households are left without service and the rate stays high.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: low population density / homes spread far apart, high cost of infrastructure per customer, distance from the city, or lower incomes / Explains the cause-and-effect chain to the higher rate
MINI-LESSON (Density, distance, and the cost of a connection):
 - Population density is the number of people per unit of land area, for example per square mile. Rural counties have low density: homes sit far apart.
 - Internet service needs physical infrastructure, like fiber-optic cable or cell towers, and it costs money for every mile built.
 - In a dense city, one mile of cable can serve hundreds of paying customers. In a rural county it might serve five, so the company earns far less per mile.
 - Companies build where they make money first, so rural areas get service later, slower, or never. Distance decay plays a role too: places far from the metro interact with it less and get new services later.
 - Lower rural incomes can add to it: even where service exists, fewer families can afford it.
KEY WORDS: Population density = People per unit of land.; Infrastructure = The physical systems a place needs, like roads, pipes, and cables.; Distance decay = Interaction weakens as distance grows.
QUICK CHECK: Why does one mile of internet cable cost MORE per customer in a rural county? → Fewer homes are spread along that mile (Low population density means each mile of cable reaches fewer paying customers.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Types of thematic maps
   - A thematic map shows one topic (a theme), like income or internet access, instead of just roads and borders.
   - A choropleth map shades whole areas, like counties or countries, by class: darker or hotter colors usually mean higher values. It works best with rates and percents.
   - A dot density map places one dot for every set number of things (say, 500 households), so areas with more of them fill up with more dots. When the data are county totals, the dots are scattered inside each county, so they show how many, not exactly where.
   - A graduated (proportional) symbol map puts a circle on each place and makes the circle bigger when the number is bigger.
   - An isoline map connects points of equal value (like elevation or temperature), and a cartogram stretches or shrinks places by a data value instead of land area.
• Why patterns change with scale
   - Scale of analysis is the size of the units you look at: one country, 50 states, 3,000 counties, or individual neighborhoods.
   - When data for many places are rolled into one bigger unit, the values get averaged, and places with lots of households pull the average toward their number.
   - That is why a state can look 'fine' at 18% while some of its counties are over 40%: the crowded metro counties, with low rates and most of the households, outweigh the thinly populated rural ones.
   - Zooming in to a smaller unit reveals clusters, gaps, and outliers that the bigger unit hides. Zooming out smooths them away.
   - On the AP exam, always ask: at what scale is this data, and what could be hiding inside each unit?
• Distance decay & population density
   - Distance decay is the idea that interaction between places drops off as the distance between them grows. Farther away usually means fewer connections and fewer services.
   - Population density is the number of people per unit of land. Rural counties have low density: homes are spread far apart.
   - Networks like roads, water pipes, and fiber-optic internet cost money per mile. In a dense city one mile of cable can serve hundreds of homes; in a rural county it may serve five.
   - Because companies earn less per mile in low-density places, they build there last or not at all, which leaves rural areas with weaker service.
   - Lower average incomes in many rural areas can add to the problem, since fewer households can afford a monthly plan even where service exists.
• GIS, GPS & remote sensing
   - GIS (geographic information system) is computer software that stores map data in layers, like clear sheets stacked on a light table, so you can combine and analyze them.
   - A city might stack layers for roads, population density, income, flood zones, and existing utilities, then ask the software to find spots where several conditions overlap.
   - GPS (global positioning system) uses satellite signals to find a device's location (its latitude and longitude), usually to within a few meters. It collects points; GIS analyzes them.
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites or aircraft, like images of crops, forests, or city growth.
   - Governments use these tools to plan services and respond to disasters; businesses use them to choose store locations and plan delivery routes.
• Time-space compression
   - Time-space compression is the idea that new transportation and communication technology shrinks the time it takes to connect distant places, so the world feels smaller.
   - The telegraph, jet planes, and the internet each made far-away places easier and faster to reach.
   - It does not shrink actual distance; it reduces the 'friction' of distance. Geographers call this a change in relative distance between places.
   - Places with weak connections, like rural areas without broadband, are left out of this shrinking world, which is a form of spatial inequality.
   - Better connections can let people in remote places reach jobs, doctors, and schools without moving, but they can also pull local shoppers toward big online companies.
• Three types of regions
   - A region is an area that shares one or more features. Geographers sort regions into three kinds.
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or a shared measurable trait everywhere inside it, like a county, a state, or the French-speaking part of Canada.
   - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a center, or node, like a metro area tied to its downtown, a pizza delivery zone, or the area a radio station reaches.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like 'the South' or 'upstate.'
   - On the Calderon map, each county is a formal region, while the capital and the suburbs it serves form a functional region.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Total Fertility Rate (TFR)
   - TFR is the average number of children a woman is expected to have in her lifetime.
   - In countries with good health care, a TFR of about 2.1 is replacement level, just enough for each generation to replace itself. It is a bit more than 2 because slightly more boys than girls are born, and some girls die before they grow up to have children.
   - Above 2.1, population tends to grow. Below 2.1, it eventually shrinks unless immigrants arrive.
   - TFR is one of the best single numbers for predicting a country's future population.
• Demographic Transition Model (DTM)
   - The DTM describes how birth and death rates change as a country develops.
   - Stage 1: both rates are high, so population barely grows.
   - Stage 2: death rates drop (better food, medicine, sanitation) but birth rates stay high, so population grows fast.
   - Stage 3: birth rates fall as the country urbanizes and women gain education.
   - Stages 4–5: both rates are low, and population levels off or even shrinks.
• Women's education & fertility
   - Worldwide, one of the strongest predictors of lower fertility is how many years of school girls complete.
   - Staying in school tends to delay marriage and first births.
   - Education opens paid jobs, so each extra child has a higher opportunity cost (the income a mother gives up).
   - Educated women usually know more about health care and family planning and have more say in family decisions.
• Urbanization & family size
   - On farms, children can help with work, so a big family can be an economic asset.
   - In cities, children usually don't earn income, and housing, food, and school cost more.
   - Cities also offer more clinics, contraception, and paid jobs for women.
   - That's why fertility usually falls as a country becomes more urban.
• Population pyramids
   - A population pyramid is a bar graph of a population by age and sex, with the youngest at the bottom.
   - A wide base means many births, which is common in Stage 2 countries.
   - When fertility falls, the base narrows. If fertility stays low for many years, the shape slowly turns more like a beehive or column.
   - Large age groups (cohorts) move up the pyramid over time like a bulge.
• Dependency ratio
   - The dependency ratio compares people who usually aren't working (children under 15 and adults 65+) with the working-age population (15–64).
   - A high elderly dependency ratio means fewer workers paying taxes to support each retiree's pension and health care.
   - Very-low-fertility countries such as Japan and Italy face rising elderly dependency ratios.
   - Common responses include encouraging immigration, raising the retirement age, and supporting families who want children.

• Push and pull factors
   - Push factors make people want to leave a place. Pull factors attract them to a new one.

   - Economic factors are the most common reason people migrate.
   - Most moves combine both: a push at home AND a pull somewhere else.
• Ravenstein's laws of migration
   - In the 1880s, geographer E. G. Ravenstein studied British census records and wrote “laws of migration.”
   - Most migrants move only a short distance, so the number of migrants drops as distance grows: distance decay.
   - Long-distance migrants usually head for big cities and job centers.
   - Many people move in steps (step migration), like village → town → city.
   - Each big migration flow produces a smaller counterflow going the other way, including some people who return home.
• Chain migration
   - Chain migration happens when people move somewhere because relatives or neighbors from home already live there.
   - Earlier migrants send back news and money and help newcomers find jobs and housing.
   - Each newcomer can help the next one, so the “chain” keeps growing.
   - Over time this can build ethnic enclaves, like Little Havana in Miami.
• Intervening obstacles & opportunities
   - An intervening obstacle is a barrier between a migrant's origin and destination that makes the move harder.
   - It can be physical (rivers, oceans, deserts, mountains), political (border walls, visas, immigration limits), or economic (the cost of the trip).
   - As transportation improves, political obstacles like visas and walls matter more than physical ones.
   - An intervening opportunity is the opposite: a job or safe place found along the way that convinces a migrant to stop there instead.
• Remittances
   - Remittances are money that migrants send back to family in their home country.
   - Migrants send more than $600 billion a year to low- and middle-income countries, more than those countries receive in foreign aid.
   - In Nepal, Honduras, and Tajikistan, remittances equal more than a fifth of GDP.
   - Families spend the money on food, school, health care, and housing, which boosts local businesses.
   - Migrants who keep close ties to home like this are called transnational migrants.
• Voluntary vs. forced migration
   - Voluntary migration is a choice. Forced migration is caused by violence, persecution, disaster, or slavery.
   - A refugee has crossed an international border to escape persecution for their race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or social group.
   - An asylum seeker has asked another country for protection and is still waiting for a decision.
   - An internally displaced person (IDP) was forced to flee but stayed inside their own country.
   - A slow drought, like Tavira's, blurs the line between the two.
• Migration, age, and sex
   - Most migrants are young adults, roughly ages 20 to 35.
   - When many leave, the origin's population pyramid gets “pinched” in the young-adult bars, leaving children and older people behind.
   - That raises the dependency ratio and can lower the birth rate.
   - Destinations gain young workers: in Qatar and the UAE, so many male guest workers have arrived that men far outnumber women.
   - When skilled people like doctors and engineers leave, the origin suffers brain drain.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Lingua franca
   - A lingua franca is a shared language people use when they don't speak the same first language.
   - The name comes from an old trade language used around the Mediterranean Sea.
   - Swahili in East Africa and Arabic across the Middle East have both served as lingua francas.
   - Today English is the most widely used global lingua franca: in business, science, air travel, and the internet.
• Types of diffusion
   - Relocation diffusion: people move and bring their language or culture with them.
   - Expansion diffusion: an idea spreads outward while staying strong where it started.
   - Its 3 kinds: hierarchical (from powerful people or big cities first), contagious (person to person, like a virus), and stimulus (the idea spreads but gets changed to fit local culture).
   - English spread by relocation (British colonists) and hierarchical diffusion (elites, schools, and businesses first).
• Outsourcing & offshoring
   - Outsourcing means hiring another company to do part of your work. Offshoring means moving work to another country.
   - Companies offshore to cut labor costs, since wages are lower in many developing countries.
   - India and the Philippines became call-center hubs because they have many English-speaking workers.
   - This is part of the new international division of labor: different steps of a business happen in different countries.
• Language families
   - A language family is a group of languages that grew from one ancient ancestor language.
   - Indo-European is the biggest family by number of speakers. Its Germanic branch includes English, Dutch, German, and Swedish.
   - Mandarin belongs to the Sino-Tibetan family and uses tones and characters.
   - Languages in the same branch share words and grammar, so each is easier for the others' speakers to learn.
• Measuring development
   - GDP per capita is the value of everything a country produces, divided by its population.
   - HDI (Human Development Index) combines income, education, and life expectancy.
   - Cultural data, like which language people speak, doesn't directly measure development.
   - Always ask: does this data actually measure what the question is asking about?
• Cultural homogenization
   - Globalization spreads the same brands, music, movies, and languages around the world.
   - When global culture replaces local culture, places can start to look and feel alike.
   - Ethnologue estimates that about 40% of the world's 7,000+ languages are endangered.
   - Many communities fight back with revival programs, like teaching Welsh, Māori, or Hawaiian in schools.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Sequent occupance
   - Sequent occupance is the idea that a place is shaped by every group that has ever lived there, one layer at a time.
   - Each group leaves its mark on the cultural landscape: buildings, signs, foods, places of worship, street names.
   - The next group doesn't erase everything. It reuses and adds to what's already there, like a Lutheran church becoming a Catholic parish.
   - Cultural landscape just means the visible, human-made look of a place.
• Ethnic enclaves
   - An ethnic enclave (or ethnic neighborhood) is an area where one ethnic group clusters together in a city.
   - Immigrants settle near people who share their language, religion, and food because it feels safer and makes daily life easier.
   - Enclaves fill with businesses that serve the group: bakeries, groceries, newspapers, and radio stations in the home language.
   - Many U.S. cities have had a “Little Italy,” a Chinatown, or a German district. Over time one group moves out and another moves in: ethnic succession.
• Acculturation, assimilation, syncretism
   - Acculturation: a group adopts some traits of the surrounding culture but keeps its own identity (speaking English at work, Spanish at home).
   - Assimilation: a group blends in so fully that it loses most of its original culture traits, such as its language. How fast that happens varies from group to group.
   - Syncretism: two cultures mix and create something new. Día de los Muertos itself blends Indigenous Aztec traditions with Catholic All Souls' Day.
   - Commodification: a cultural practice is turned into something to sell, like a festival marketed to tourists. Stimulus diffusion is different: an idea spreads to a new group, and that group changes it to fit its own culture.
• Religion in the landscape
   - Religion is one of the easiest cultural traits to spot in a landscape: churches, mosques, temples, shrines, and cemeteries.
   - Universalizing religions like Christianity and Islam try to appeal to everyone, so they spread widely and build in many places.
   - Ethnic religions like Hinduism and Judaism are tied to one group or place and spread mostly by migration.
   - Even a single building can show history: a church may change denomination when the neighborhood changes. Festivals and shrines count too.
• Centripetal vs. centrifugal forces
   - Centripetal forces pull a group together: a shared language, religion, media, or celebration.
   - Centrifugal forces push a group apart: tension between newcomers and old-timers, different languages, or feeling like outsiders in your own area.
   - An ethnic radio station or newspaper is a classic centripetal force for an immigrant community.
   - Rapid neighborhood change can be centrifugal when long-time residents feel their culture is being replaced. These terms return in Unit 4.
• Popular vs. local culture
   - Local (folk) culture belongs to a specific place or group, is passed down through generations (traditionally in person), and changes slowly.
   - Popular culture spreads fast through media and big companies and looks similar everywhere: chain coffee shops, trending music, the same apps.
   - Globalization helps popular culture spread, which can crowd out local traditions, a process called cultural homogenization.
   - But the same technology can preserve local culture: communities record recipes, stream festivals, and post heritage-language videos online.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Scramble for Africa & the Berlin Conference
   - At the Berlin Conference (1884–85), European powers set the rules for claiming African land. No Africans were invited.
   - Its “effective occupation” rule said a power had to actually control land to claim it, which helped set off the Scramble for Africa.
   - Only Liberia and Abyssinia (Ethiopia) stayed independent. Ethiopia defeated an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa in 1896.
• Superimposed & geometric boundaries
   - A superimposed boundary is drawn by an outside power over an existing cultural landscape, like most colonial borders in Africa.
   - A geometric boundary is a straight line, often along latitude or longitude, like Egypt and Sudan's border at 22°N.
   - Other types: antecedent (drawn before an area was heavily settled), subsequent (drawn after, often following cultural lines), and relic (no longer used but still visible).
   - One border can be two types: a straight colonial line across the Sahara is both geometric and superimposed.
• Ethnocentrism & the “civilizing mission”
   - Ethnocentrism is judging other cultures by your own culture's standards and believing your own is superior.
   - Many Europeans claimed a duty to “civilize” Africans. The French called it the mission civilisatrice, and racist theories like Social Darwinism were used to justify conquest.
   - Colonizers often dismissed powerful African states, like the Ashanti Empire and the Sokoto Caliphate, as “primitive.”
• Colonies built for extraction
   - Colonial economies in Africa were built to send raw materials to Europe: Congo rubber, Gold Coast gold and cocoa, Nigerian palm oil, and South African diamonds and gold.
   - The Belgian Congo began as King Leopold II's personal colony (1885–1908), where forced rubber collection led to the deaths of huge numbers of Congolese.
   - Railways usually ran from mines and plantations straight to a port, not between African regions.
• When borders and nations don't match
   - A nation is a people with a shared identity; a state is an independent country. Africa's colonial borders rarely matched its nations.
   - At independence, mostly in the 1950s and 1960s, African states kept the colonial borders, leaving many multinational states.
   - Nigeria has more than 250 ethnic groups. In 1967 the Igbo-led southeast declared independence as Biafra, and the civil war (1967–70) killed hundreds of thousands to millions, many from starvation.
   - Sudan's mostly Muslim north and mostly Christian and traditional-religion south fought two long civil wars (1955–72 and 1983–2005). After a 2011 vote, South Sudan became independent.
• Languages in Africa today
   - Africa has about 2,000 languages, nearly a third of the world's total, and most Africans grow up speaking an African language at home.
   - French is an official or working language in about 20 African countries, English is official in over 20, and Portuguese in six, including Angola and Mozambique.
   - Swahili and Hausa grew as lingua francas through trade long before European rule, and Arabic spread across North Africa with Islam from the 600s.
   - Ethiopia, never colonized except for a brief Italian occupation (1936–41), governs mainly in Amharic.
• Religion in Africa today
   - Christianity and Islam, both universalizing religions, are Africa's two largest faiths.
   - Islam spread across North Africa from the 600s and into West Africa along trans-Saharan trade routes. Christianity spread most widely through European missionaries in the 1800s and 1900s.
   - Traditional religions are still practiced, often blended with Christianity or Islam (syncretism).
   - One culture group can hold several faiths: many Yoruba are Muslim, many are Christian, and some follow traditional Yoruba religion.

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe the concept of sovereignty as it relates to the state.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Sovereignty is a state's supreme authority to govern its own territory and people without interference from other states. E2- A sovereign state like Japan makes its own laws, controls its borders, prints its own money, and signs treaties, and other countries recognize that right. A3- Sovereignty is what makes a place a true state: Puerto Rico has its own government but is not sovereign, because the U.S. Congress has the final say over it.

--- Part C (Compare) ---
Q: Compare ONE difference between unitary states and federal states.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- In a unitary state the central government holds most of the power, while in a federal state power is shared between the national government and states or provinces. E2- For example, in unitary France the national government in Paris sets most policy, including one national school curriculum, while in the federal United States each of the 50 states makes many of its own laws, like speed limits. A3- This matters because a federal system lets a large, diverse country give regions control over local issues, while a unitary system keeps the final say in the capital.
RUBRIC: Unitary side: the central government holds most of the power (e.g., France, Japan, China) / Federal side: power is shared with states or provinces (e.g., the U.S., Germany, India, Canada)
MINI-LESSON (Unitary vs. federal):
 - In a unitary state, the central government holds most of the power. France, Japan, and China are unitary.
 - In a federal state, the constitution divides power between the national government and states or provinces. The U.S., Germany, India, and Canada are federal.
 - Federal systems often fit large, diverse countries; unitary systems often fit smaller or more culturally similar ones.
KEY WORDS: Unitary state = A state where the central government holds most of the power.; Federal state = A state where power is divided between national and regional governments.
QUICK CHECK: Which pair lists a unitary state and then a federal state? → Japan, then Germany (Japan keeps power central (unitary); Germany shares it with its 16 states (federal).)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE possible reason why territoriality would play a role in devolution.

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how redrawing district boundary lines may affect election results within a city.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Redrawing district lines can change which group wins seats on a city council, even if no one changes how they vote. E2- For example, if a Latino neighborhood with enough voters to control one district is cracked, or split among three districts, its voters become a minority in each and may be unable to elect a candidate they prefer. A3- Because votes only count inside each district, where the lines fall decides whether a group's votes add up to a win or get wasted.

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain why neighboring local governments may face challenges providing transportation services in a growing metro area.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Territoriality
   - Territoriality is people's connection to a specific piece of land and their effort to claim, defend, and control it.
   - Geographer Robert Sack described it as controlling people and things by controlling an area.
   - It shows up at every scale: a country guarding its border, a city zoning its land, a neighborhood's “residents only” parking.
   - It helps explain fights over land with deep meaning, like Kashmir, which India and Pakistan both claim.
• Sovereignty & the state
   - A state is an independent country: a defined territory with a permanent population, a government, and sovereignty.
   - Sovereignty is a state's supreme authority to govern its own territory without interference from other states.
   - The idea is often traced to the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which ended the Thirty Years' War in Europe.
• Unitary vs. federal states
   - A unitary state concentrates power in the central government: France, Japan, China.
   - A federal state divides power between the national government and states or provinces, and the constitution protects each level's powers: the U.S., Germany, India, Canada.
   - Federal systems often fit large, diverse countries, since regions can make laws that fit local needs.
   - The United Kingdom is unitary, but it has devolved some powers to Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.
• Supranationalism & Brexit
   - Supranationalism is when three or more states give up some sovereignty to an organization, like the European Union (EU), for shared goals.
   - EU members follow EU law and allow free movement of EU citizens, and the European Court of Justice has the final word on EU law.
   - In a June 23, 2016 referendum, about 52% of UK voters chose to leave, after a campaign promising to “take back control” of laws, borders, trade, and money.
   - The UK left the EU on January 31, 2020.
• Devolution
   - Devolution moves power from a state's central government to its regions, often because of differences in culture, wealth, or distance from the capital. At the extreme, a state breaks apart, like the Soviet Union.
   - Scotland got its own parliament in 1999. In a 2014 referendum, about 55% of Scots voted to stay in the UK.
   - Catalonia held a 2017 independence vote that Spain's courts ruled illegal, and French-speaking Quebec voted by a hair to stay in Canada in 1995.
   - Belgium became a federal state in 1993, largely because of tension between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia.
• City elections: at-large vs. districts
   - In at-large elections, voters citywide choose the council seats, so a citywide majority can win them all.
   - In district (ward) elections, each district elects one member, so where the lines fall decides who wins.
   - Voting rights lawsuits, especially under California's Voting Rights Act (2001), have pushed many cities to switch to district elections, which often helps minority neighborhoods win seats.
   - City maps are redrawn after each census to keep districts equal in population, and they can be packed or cracked too.
• Political fragmentation
   - A metropolitan (metro) area is a central city plus its suburbs, tied together by commuting.
   - Political fragmentation means a metro area is split among many separate local governments, each with its own budget and priorities. The Chicago area alone has hundreds.
   - That makes transit, roads, and water hard to plan across boundaries.
   - One fix is a regional authority: WMATA, created in 1967 by Maryland, Virginia, and Washington, D.C., runs one Metro system for the whole region.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Gerrymandering: packing & cracking
   - Gerrymandering is drawing district lines on purpose to help one party or group win more seats.
   - It's named for Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry, who in 1812 signed a map with a salamander-shaped district.
   - Packing crowds the other side's voters into a few districts, which they win by huge margins.
   - Cracking splits the other side's voters across many districts so they're a minority in each. Both tricks waste the other side's votes.
• Census, reapportionment & redistricting
   - The U.S. Constitution requires a census, a count of every person, every ten years.
   - After each census, the House's 435 seats are reapportioned among the states by population. After 2020, Texas gained 2 seats and New York lost 1.
   - Each state with more than one seat then redistricts: it redraws its lines so every district has about the same population (“one person, one vote”).
   - Some states, like Texas, have also redrawn maps mid-decade.
• Voting Rights Act & majority-minority districts
   - The Voting Rights Act of 1965 outlawed racial discrimination in voting, such as literacy tests used to keep Black Southerners from voting.
   - Section 2 of the law has been used to challenge maps that dilute minority voting power, for example by cracking a minority community.
   - Starting in the 1980s, courts used it to require some majority-minority districts, where a minority group makes up most of the voters.
   - Courts have also ruled that race can't be the main factor in drawing lines without a very strong reason. In Louisiana v. Callais (April 2026), the Supreme Court (6–3) struck down Louisiana's map with a second majority-Black district and narrowed Section 2.
• Commissions, compactness & contiguity
   - In most states, the legislature draws congressional lines, so the party in control can help itself.
   - An independent redistricting commission is a separate panel that draws the lines instead, as in Arizona, Colorado, and Michigan.
   - Compactness means a tight, simple shape. Contiguity means every part of a district touches the rest.
• States: federal vs. unitary
   - In AP Human Geography, a state is an independent country with sovereignty. U.S. states like Westmoor are units inside one country.
   - A nation is a people with a shared culture. A nation-state is mostly one nation (Japan), a multinational state holds several (the United Kingdom), and a stateless nation (the Kurds) has no state.
   - A federal state splits power between national and regional governments (the U.S., Germany, India); a unitary state keeps it national (France, China).
   - Because the U.S. is federal, each state runs its own redistricting, so the rules differ from state to state.
• Safe seats, turnout & polarization
   - A safe seat is a district one party is almost certain to win. A swing district could go either way.
   - Voters who always lose may feel their votes don't count, which can lower turnout.
   - In safe seats, the real race is often the party primary, which can pull candidates toward the extremes and add to polarization.
• Centrifugal & centripetal forces
   - Centrifugal forces divide a country: conflict between groups, unequal treatment, economic gaps, or distrust of government.
   - Centripetal forces unify it: a shared identity, national symbols, and trusted institutions like fair elections.
   - Strong centrifugal forces can lead to devolution, where power shifts to regions, as when Scotland got its own parliament inside the United Kingdom.

• Cash crops & plantations

   - Plantations are large estates that grow one crop for export, often with cheap or forced labor. British-owned tea estates in Assam are an example.
   - Depending on one or two exports leaves a region exposed to world prices it can't control.
• Colonial port cities
   - Colonizers built port cities as gateways, where raw materials left the colony and manufactured goods came in.
   - Bombay (Mumbai), Calcutta (Kolkata), and Madras (Chennai) grew from small trading posts into major cities under British rule.
   - Calcutta was the capital of British India until 1911, when the capital moved to Delhi.
   - Many former colonies still have a primate city, one city far larger than all the others, that began as a colonial port.
• Famines in colonial India
   - Several deadly famines struck British India in the late 1800s. The Great Famine of 1876–78 alone killed millions of people.
   - Droughts caused the crop failures, but colonial policy made them worse: grain kept being exported, and relief was limited.
   - Farmers who grew cash crops had little stored food. To buy food, they depended on what their crops sold for (or on wages) and on food prices at the market.
   - Historians point to these famines as evidence that colonial economic choices shaped life-and-death outcomes.
• Wallerstein's world-system theory
   - Immanuel Wallerstein divided the world economy into the core, the semi-periphery, and the periphery.
   - Core countries are wealthy and industrialized, and they make high-value goods.
   - Periphery countries supply raw materials and cheap labor, and they earn far less.
   - Colonialism helped build this pattern by locking colonies into shipping raw materials to the core.
   - The theory's big idea: countries are linked, and the core's wealth depends partly on the periphery.
• Uneven development
   - Development is rarely spread evenly inside a country.
   - Places that get early investment, such as ports, railways, and factories, attract more businesses and workers, which attracts even more investment.
   - Geographers call this snowball effect cumulative causation.
   - The result is a core region that keeps growing while peripheral regions fall behind, and the gap can last for generations.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Green Revolution
   - The Green Revolution spread high-yield seeds, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation, and farm machines to developing countries in the mid-1900s.
   - It began in Mexico in the 1940s, where American plant scientist Norman Borlaug bred short, sturdy, high-yield wheat.
   - In the 1960s it reached India and Pakistan, and in 1966 a research institute in the Philippines released IR8, nicknamed “miracle rice.”
   - Borlaug won the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize because the extra food helped prevent famines.
• The three agricultural revolutions
   - First (Neolithic), about 10,000 years ago: people domesticated plants and animals in hearths like the Fertile Crescent and settled in villages.
   - Second, 1700s–1800s, alongside the Industrial Revolution: the seed drill, new crop rotations, and better transportation let fewer farmers feed more people, freeing workers for factories.
   - Third, from the mid-1900s: the Green Revolution's seeds, chemicals, irrigation, and machines, later joined by GMOs.
• Agribusiness & vertical integration
   - Agribusiness is farming run as a commercial business, plus all the companies that make seeds and chemicals and that process, ship, and sell food.
   - Vertical integration means one company controls several steps of the same supply chain.
   - Tyson Foods, for example, supplies chicks and feed to contract farmers, then processes and sells the chicken under its own brand.
   - Green Revolution farmers came to depend on buying fertilizer and pesticides from these companies.
• GMOs
   - A GMO (genetically modified organism) has DNA changed in a lab, often by adding a gene from another species.
   - Bt corn makes its own insect-killing protein, and Golden Rice was engineered to provide vitamin A.
   - Supporters say GMOs raise yields; critics worry about corporate control of patented seeds.
   - GMO crops spread from the mid-1990s, after the original Green Revolution.
• Subsistence farming & staple crops
   - Subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families, not to sell.
   - Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn): clear and burn a rainforest plot, farm it a few years, then move on so it can recover, as in the Amazon and Central Africa.
   - Pastoral nomadism: moving herds of camels, goats, or cattle across dry lands like the Sahel and Mongolia.
   - In much of sub-Saharan Africa, small rain-fed farms grow staples like cassava, yams, sorghum, and millet, not the irrigated wheat and rice the Green Revolution was built for.
• Environmental costs of high-input farming
   - Irrigating thirsty high-yield crops can pump groundwater faster than rain refills it; in India's Punjab the water table has been falling for decades.
   - In dry places, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salt in the soil (salinization), which can ruin fields.
   - Heavy pesticide use can poison water and breed resistant pests.
   - Replacing thousands of local varieties with a few high-yield ones shrinks crops' genetic diversity, so a disease that attacks those few varieties can wipe out more of the harvest.
• Sustainable farming practices
   - Sustainable agriculture grows food today while protecting the soil, water, and wildlife farms need tomorrow.
   - Soil savers: no-till, cover crops, contour plowing, and terracing, like the centuries-old rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras.
   - Crop rotation with legumes helps rebuild soil on any farm, and organic farming also uses compost and manure instead of synthetic fertilizer.
   - Community-supported agriculture (CSA) and farmers' markets shorten the distance food travels and support small farms.
• Food deserts & food insecurity
   - Food insecurity means lacking reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food, and it happens in rich and poor countries alike.
   - A food desert is an area, often low-income, where residents live far from a supermarket and healthy food is hard to get.
   - In cities, many supermarket chains closed stores in poorer neighborhoods and moved to the suburbs, a pattern tied to disinvestment and redlining.
   - In rural areas, like the Mississippi Delta or the Navajo Nation, too few customers live nearby to support a big store, so families drive far for groceries.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The von Thünen model
   - Johann Heinrich von Thünen was a German landowner who published The Isolated State in 1826 to explain why farms near a city grow different things than farms far away.
   - He imagined one market city on a flat plain with the same soil and climate everywhere, no rivers or roads, and farmers hauling goods by wagon.
   - His rings, from the center out: market gardening and dairy, forest (for firewood and lumber), grains and field crops, then livestock ranching.
   - The big idea: the farther you are from the market, the more it costs to get your product there, so land use changes with distance.
   - Real places never match the rings exactly, but the logic of distance and transport cost still shows up everywhere.
• Bid-rent & transport cost
   - Bid-rent is the most a user is willing to pay for land at a given distance from the market.
   - Land near the city is expensive because many people want it; land far away is cheap.
   - A farmer whose product is perishable (milk, lettuce) or bulky (timber) saves the most money by being close, so they can outbid others for near-city land.
   - A rancher's cattle can walk or ride to market cheaply, so the rancher can't outbid anyone near the city and ends up on cheap distant land.
   - Put together, those bidding wars create the rings.
• Global food supply chains
   - A commodity chain is every step a product takes from the farm to your plate: growing, processing, shipping, selling.
   - Refrigerated trucks and containers (the “cold chain”) keep food fresh for thousands of miles, so perishables no longer have to be grown nearby.
   - Interstate highways and container ships make long-distance shipping cheap, and air freight rushes the most perishable foods, like berries and fresh fish, across oceans fast, at a much higher price.
   - That's why a U.S. grocery store sells Chilean grapes in January and Mexican tomatoes year-round.
   - Large agribusiness companies often own several steps of the chain, which is called vertical integration.
• The Green Revolution
   - Starting in the 1940s–1960s, scientists like Norman Borlaug bred high-yield varieties of wheat and rice.
   - Those seeds produce their biggest harvests with plenty of chemical fertilizer, irrigation water, and pesticides to control pests.
   - Mexico, India, and the Philippines sharply raised their food output, and famine deaths dropped.
   - Costs: polluted water, drained aquifers, lost crop diversity, and debt for poor farmers who couldn't afford the inputs.
   - Sub-Saharan Africa benefited least, partly because its main crops (like cassava, sorghum, and millet) and mostly rain-fed farms didn't fit the new seeds.
• Farming's environmental costs
   - Fertilizer runoff carries nitrogen and phosphorus into rivers, feeding algae blooms that create oxygen-poor “dead zones,” like the one in the Gulf of Mexico fed by the Mississippi River.
   - Pumping groundwater for irrigation faster than rain refills it depletes aquifers, such as the Ogallala Aquifer under the U.S. Great Plains.
   - Irrigating in dry climates leaves salt behind as water evaporates (salinization), which can ruin soil.
   - Plowing and bare fields speed up soil erosion; the 1930s Dust Bowl is the classic example.
   - Clearing forest for cattle pasture and soybeans drives deforestation in the Amazon.
• Intensive vs. extensive, commercial vs. subsistence
   - Intensive agriculture puts lots of labor or money into each acre of land: dairy farms, vegetable farms, rice paddies.
   - Extensive agriculture puts little labor or money into each acre, so it usually spreads across lots of land: cattle ranching, shifting cultivation, nomadic herding.
   - Commercial farmers grow crops to sell; subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families.
   - Most farms in wealthy countries are commercial; many farms in less developed countries are still subsistence or a mix.
   - Von Thünen's model assumes commercial farmers selling to one market, which is why it fits some places better than others.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Latin American city model
   - Geographers Ernst Griffin and Larry Ford created this model in 1980 (Ford updated it in 1996).
   - A commercial spine runs out from the CBD and market, lined by the elite residential sector, where the wealthiest families live.
   - Rings get poorer with distance: the zone of maturity (solid homes, full services), the zone of in situ accretion (modest homes improved bit by bit), and the zone of peripheral squatter settlements at the edge. (Ford's 1996 update added the periférico, a ring highway around the city.)
   - Disamenity sectors are wedges of the very poorest housing along rivers, ravines, rail lines, or dumps, with few or no services.
   - Rich near the center, poor at the edge: roughly the reverse of the classic U.S. pattern.
• Squatter settlements
   - Squatter settlements are homes residents build on land they don't legally own or rent, often from scrap wood and metal.
   - Local names include favelas (Brazil) and pueblos jóvenes (Peru); Dharavi in Mumbai and Kibera in Nairobi are famous examples.
   - Many lack piped water, sewers, paved roads, and legal land titles, and sit on hillsides or floodplains.
   - UN-Habitat estimates that more than 1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements.
• The informal economy
   - The informal economy is work that isn't taxed, licensed, or regulated: street vending, waste picking, day labor.
   - The International Labour Organization estimates that about 60% of the world's workers earn a living informally, mostly in developing countries.
   - It's usually easier for migrants to get into, but pay is often low and unsteady, with few benefits or legal protections.
• Rural-to-urban migration
   - Rural-to-urban migration (moving from countryside to city) and natural increase together drive fast urban growth in developing countries.
   - Push factors (farm machines, too little land, poverty, drought, violence) drive people out; pull factors (jobs, schools, hospitals) draw them in.
   - Latin America is now about 80% urban; the fastest urban growth today is in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
• Primate cities
   - Geographer Mark Jefferson described the primate city in 1939: a country's leading city, usually defined as more than twice the size of the second-largest.
   - A primate city dominates its country's government, economy, and culture, and draws a much bigger share of its migrants and investment than any other city.
   - Examples: Lima, Buenos Aires, Bangkok, and Paris; many in developing countries were colonial capitals or ports.
• The rank-size rule
   - The rank-size rule says the nth-largest city has 1/n the population of the largest: if the largest has 6 million, the 2nd should have 3 million and the 3rd 2 million.
   - Countries that roughly follow it, like the United States, have a balanced set of cities.
   - When the smaller cities fall far below the prediction, the country has a primate city instead.
• Megacities & world cities
   - A megacity has more than 10 million people; the UN counted 33 in 2018, most in developing countries, like Delhi, Dhaka, Cairo, and Lagos.
   - Many grew so fast that housing, water, sewers, and transit couldn't keep up.
   - A world city (global city) is a command center of the global economy, full of banks and corporate headquarters: London, New York, Tokyo.
   - Size isn't power: Lagos is huge but has far less global influence than London.
• Improving informal settlements
   - Slum upgrading adds paving, water, sewers, and power to settlements where they stand, as Rio de Janeiro's Favela-Bairro program did starting in the 1990s.
   - Land titles give residents legal ownership, which makes eviction much less likely and encourages them to invest in their homes.
   - Site-and-service programs, backed by the World Bank since the 1970s, give migrants plots with water, sewer, and road connections to build on.
   - Bus rapid transit (BRT) gives buses their own lanes: Curitiba, Brazil, pioneered it in 1974; Bogotá, Colombia, opened TransMilenio in 2000.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Suburbanization & urban sprawl
   - Suburbanization is the movement of people and homes from the central city outward to the suburbs that ring it.
   - In the United States it boomed after World War II: cheap cars, the Interstate Highway System (begun 1956), and FHA and VA government-backed mortgages made a new house far from downtown easy to afford.
   - Urban sprawl is what that growth looks like on the ground: low-density, car-dependent subdivisions, strip malls, and parking lots spreading over former farmland.
   - Consequences include long commutes and traffic, air pollution, loss of farmland and habitat, the high cost of stretching roads and sewers outward, and metros sorted by income.
   - Edge cities (big clusters of offices and malls at highway exits) grew where the sprawl met the beltways.
• Urban land-use models
   - Burgess concentric zone model (1920s Chicago): rings around a central business district (CBD): a zone of transition with old housing and factories, then working-class homes, then nicer homes, then a commuter zone.
   - Hoyt sector model (1939): land uses grow in wedges along railroads and major roads, so a high-rent sector can stretch from downtown to the edge.
   - Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model (1945): a city has several centers (airport, university, industrial park, suburban business district), not just one downtown.
   - Galactic or peripheral model: an old core surrounded by a highway beltway with edge cities and suburbs scattered along it, the classic post-1970 American metro.
   - Latin American city model: a wealthy spine runs out from the CBD, squatter settlements ring the far edge, and disamenity sectors (along rivers or rail lines) cut through the city.
• Gentrification
   - Gentrification happens when wealthier, often younger, residents move into an older lower-income neighborhood near downtown, renovate the housing, and open new businesses.
   - It is pulled by cheap older homes, short commutes to downtown jobs, walkable streets, and a taste for city life among young professionals and retirees.
   - Upsides: higher property values, more tax money for the city, new shops, and reinvestment in buildings that had been neglected.
   - Downsides: rents and property taxes climb, so long-time lower-income residents, especially renters, are displaced, and the neighborhood's culture and businesses change.
   - Many gentrifying neighborhoods were once redlined, which is one reason their housing was cheap enough to attract investors in the first place.
• Zoning & single-family-only rules
   - Zoning is the set of local laws that say what can be built on each piece of land: homes, shops, factories, and how big or tall.
   - Single-family-only zoning allows just one detached house per lot, so duplexes, townhouses, and apartment buildings are illegal there.
   - In many U.S. cities most residential land is zoned this way, which caps how many homes can fit even when demand is high.
   - Because it holds down supply and keeps out cheaper housing types, critics call it exclusionary zoning; it is one reason housing is so expensive in many metros.
   - Some places have loosened these rules: Minneapolis now allows up to three homes on residential lots, and a 2019 Oregon law makes its larger cities allow duplexes and other 'middle housing' where single houses are allowed.
• Smart growth & New Urbanism
   - Smart growth is a set of planning policies that fight sprawl by making metros compact, walkable, and transit-friendly.
   - Tools include urban growth boundaries (Portland, Oregon has had one since 1979) and greenbelts that protect open land at the edge, transit-oriented development around stations, mixed-use zoning, and infill building on vacant lots or brownfields (old industrial sites that may be polluted and need cleanup).
   - New Urbanism is the design movement behind many of these ideas: walkable blocks, homes close to shops and schools, front porches, and a mix of housing types, as in Seaside, Florida and Kentlands, Maryland.
   - Supporters say these policies cut driving and protect farmland; critics worry that limiting land for building can push housing prices up unless cities also allow more homes inside the boundary.
• Redlining, blockbusting & white flight
   - In the 1930s a federal agency, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC), drew maps grading neighborhoods for mortgage risk; areas where Black families or many immigrants lived were usually graded lowest, colored red, and labeled 'hazardous.' Redlining means refusing loans, or charging unfair terms, because of the neighborhood a person lives in. For decades, federal housing agencies and many banks did this to Black and mixed neighborhoods.
   - Blockbusting: real-estate agents frightened white homeowners into selling cheap by warning that Black families were moving in, then resold the homes to Black buyers at high prices.
   - White flight: as Black families moved into city neighborhoods, millions of white families left central cities for the suburbs from the 1950s to the 1970s, helped by highways and FHA loans that redlined neighborhoods could not get.
   - The Fair Housing Act of 1968 banned housing discrimination, but the legacy remains: disinvested neighborhoods, lower home values, and a large racial wealth gap.
   - Today many formerly redlined neighborhoods near downtown are the ones gentrifying.

E2- Coal fueled the steam engines James Watt improved in the 1760s and 1770s, iron built the machines, and canals like the Bridgewater Canal (1761) moved coal cheaply to Manchester. MINI-LESSON (Why Britain went first):
 - Britain had coal and iron ore: coal fueled steam engines and iron built the machines.
 - Colonies and trade supplied raw cotton, profits to invest (capital), and markets; rivers and canals made shipping cheap.
 - The Agricultural Revolution pushed rural workers off the land and into factory jobs.
KEY WORDS: Industrial Revolution = The shift from making goods by hand to making them with machines and new power sources like steam.; Capital = Money saved up and invested to build businesses, like factories and machines.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Weber's least-cost theory
   - Alfred Weber's least-cost theory (1909) says a factory locates where its total costs are lowest.
   - His three factors: transportation (moving raw materials in and products out), labor (wages), and agglomeration (savings from clustering near similar firms).
   - Transportation usually matters most. Weber drew it as a triangle between two raw-material sources and one market.
• Bulk-reducing, bulk-gaining & break-of-bulk
   - A bulk-reducing (weight-losing) industry makes a product lighter than its inputs, so it locates near its raw materials, like copper smelting.
   - A bulk-gaining industry makes a product heavier, so it locates near its market, like soft-drink bottling, which adds water.
   - A break-of-bulk point, like a seaport, is where goods switch from one kind of transportation to another. Factories locate there to avoid loading and unloading twice.
• The Industrial Revolution
   - The Industrial Revolution, the shift from hand tools to machines powered by water and steam, began in Great Britain in the late 1700s.
   - Britain had coal and iron, capital from trade and colonies, rivers and canals, and workers pushed off farms by the Agricultural Revolution.
   - It spread to Western Europe and the U.S. in the early 1800s, Japan after 1868, and after World War II to newly industrialized countries like South Korea, then China.
• Deindustrialization & the Rust Belt
   - Deindustrialization is the loss of factory jobs in a region that once depended on them, like the U.S. Rust Belt around the Great Lakes.
   - U.S. factory jobs peaked at about 19.5 million in 1979, then fell by about a third because of automation, offshoring, and foreign competition.
   - Some Rust Belt cities, like Pittsburgh, shifted to service and quaternary jobs in hospitals, universities, and tech.
• The new international division of labor
   - The new international division of labor (NIDL) splits the steps of making one product among different countries.
   - Design, research, and marketing usually stay in wealthy core countries, while assembly moves to lower-wage countries.
   - Nike designs shoes in Oregon but has them made in Vietnam, Indonesia, and China, through outsourcing (hiring other companies) and offshoring (moving work abroad).
• Microloans
   - A microloan is a very small loan, often a few hundred dollars or less, for people too poor to borrow from regular banks.
   - Muhammad Yunus founded Grameen Bank in Bangladesh in 1983. About 97 percent of its borrowers are women, and he and the bank won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
   - Critics warn about high interest rates and tiny businesses that never grow, so microloans can't end poverty alone.
• Ecotourism
   - Ecotourism is travel to natural areas that aims to protect the environment and benefit local people.
   - Costa Rica protects about a quarter of its land in parks and reserves. Rwanda charges $1,500 for a mountain gorilla permit and shares park revenue with nearby villages.
   - Drawbacks: crowds damage fragile places, flights add carbon emissions, and profits can leak away to foreign-owned companies.
• The UN Sustainable Development Goals
   - Sustainable development means meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
   - In 2015, UN members adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for 2030, like no poverty (Goal 1), gender equality (Goal 5), decent work (Goal 8), and life on land (Goal 15).
   - Microloans to women support Goals 1 and 5; well-run ecotourism supports Goals 8 and 15.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Measures of development
   - GNI per capita is a country's total income divided by its population. “PPP” (purchasing power parity) adjusts for the fact that a dollar buys more in some countries than in others.
   - HDI, the Human Development Index, combines income, years of schooling, and life expectancy into one score from 0 to 1. A score of 0.800 or higher counts as “very high” human development.
   - Other common measures: literacy rate, infant mortality rate, and the share of workers in each economic sector.
   - Income shows what a country earns; HDI adds health and education, so it says more about how people live (but not who gets the money). When the two disagree, something interesting is going on.
• Rostow's stages of growth
   - W. W. Rostow (1960) said every country moves through five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and high mass consumption.
   - Take-off is the big moment: a few industries grow fast, cities swell, and the country starts to industrialize.
   - High mass consumption is the top stage: most workers are in services, and people spend on cars, electronics, and vacations.
   - Critics say the model is based on the history of the United States and Western Europe, assumes every country can follow the same path, and ignores how colonialism and global trade can hold countries back.
   - Wallerstein's world-system theory answers that criticism by looking at the whole world at once: a wealthy core, a semi-periphery, and a periphery that supplies raw materials and cheap labor.
• Gender Inequality Index
   - The Gender Inequality Index (GII) is a UN measure from 0 to 1 that compares women and men. Lower is better; 0 would mean full equality.
   - It combines three things: reproductive health (maternal deaths and teen births), empowerment (seats in parliament and secondary schooling), and the labor force (people working or looking for work).
   - Countries with a high GII usually have fewer girls finishing school, fewer women in the labor force, and more teen births.
   - Development experts argue that educating and employing women is one of the fastest ways to raise a country's HDI, which is why microloans to women, like those from Bangladesh's Grameen Bank, are so popular.
• Commodity dependence
   - A commodity is a raw product sold in bulk at a world price: oil, copper, coffee, cocoa, cotton.
   - The UN agency UNCTAD calls a country commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its goods exports. Often one product leads: Nigeria and Venezuela, for example, get most of their export earnings from oil.
   - World prices for commodities swing wildly. Oil fell from over $100 a barrel in 2014 to under $30 in early 2016.
   - When the price drops, the country's export income, government budget, and jobs can all drop together. Some geographers call this trap the “resource curse.”
   - The usual advice is to diversify: build manufacturing and services so that one price can't sink the whole economy.
• Special economic zones
   - A special economic zone (SEZ) is an area where a government relaxes its normal rules to attract foreign factories: lower taxes, fewer tariffs, less paperwork.
   - An export processing zone (EPZ) is a type of SEZ focused on assembling goods for export. Mexico's maquiladoras, many near the U.S. border, work in a similar way: they import parts tax-free and export the finished goods.
   - Shenzhen, one of China's first SEZs (1980), grew from a small border town of about 30,000 people into a manufacturing city of more than 17 million.
   - Companies get cheap labor and easy exporting; the host country gets jobs, technology, and foreign investment.
   - Downsides can include low wages, weak worker protections, pollution, and companies that leave once the tax breaks end.
• Economic sectors
   - Primary sector: taking things from the earth (farming, fishing, mining). Secondary: making things (factories, construction). Tertiary: services (stores, schools, hospitals, banks).
   - AP Human Geography also uses two more: quaternary (information and research) and quinary (top-level decision-making).
   - As a country develops, workers shift from primary to secondary to tertiary. Rich countries usually have under 5% of workers in farming and over 70% in services.
   - A big share of workers in agriculture usually signals low incomes, because farms in poor countries produce little per worker and most of it is eaten, not sold.
