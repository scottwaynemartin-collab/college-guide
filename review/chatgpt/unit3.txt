The English language is spoken in many countries, but at very different rates. India and China each have more than 1.3 billion people. India has more English speakers than China, but the number of English speakers is growing in both countries.
Country | Number of English speakers | Percent of population who speak English |

Netherlands | 15 million | 90% | 
Nigeria | 79 million | 53% |

Source: Ethnologue. Remember the class directions: label each part, and write Describe and Explain parts as T1- (your reason in general words), E2- (the specific proof), A3- (why it matters).

--- Part A (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE historical reason for India's current number of English speakers.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- India has many English speakers today because it was a British colony. E2- Under the British Raj (1858–1947), English was the language of government, courts, and universities, so educated Indians had to learn it. A3- After independence, India kept English as an official language, partly as a neutral link between its many regional languages, so it is still widely spoken.
RUBRIC: Names a historical reason: British colonial rule of India / Describes how English spread or stayed: used in government, schools, or business, and kept after independence
MINI-LESSON (Colonialism spreads languages):
 - Colonizers usually made their language the language of power: government, courts, schools, and business.
 - Britain ruled India for nearly 200 years, first through the East India Company, then the British Raj (1858–1947).
 - Educated Indians learned English to get government jobs and attend universities.
 - After independence, India kept English as an official language. With hundreds of languages, English works as a neutral “link” language.
KEY WORDS: Colonialism = One country taking political control of another place.; Official language = The language a government uses for laws, courts, and schools.
QUICK CHECK: Which country colonized India and spread English there? → Great Britain (Britain ruled India until 1947, and English stayed.)

--- Part B (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE contemporary reason why people in China and India are learning English.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- People in China and India are learning English because it is the global lingua franca of business. E2- Multinational companies, tech firms, and call centers hire workers who can speak English with customers and partners around the world. A3- Because English skills open these better-paying jobs, learning English can raise a person's income and help the country compete in the global economy.
RUBRIC: Names a present-day reason: globalization, business, jobs, studying abroad, the internet, or tourism / Explains how English helps (more opportunities, higher pay, communicating with the world)
MINI-LESSON (English and globalization):
 - Globalization links economies, so companies, scientists, and websites need a shared language.
 - English is the most widely used lingua franca for international business, science, air travel, and the internet.
 - In India, English skills open jobs in tech and in customer-service centers that serve the US and UK.
 - In China, English is a required school subject, and students who want to study abroad need it.
KEY WORDS: Lingua franca = A shared language used by people who speak different first languages.; Globalization = The growing connection of the world's economies and cultures.
QUICK CHECK: A “lingua franca” is… → a shared language used by people with different first languages (It's a common “bridge” language, and today English is the biggest one.)

--- Part C (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE barrier to the diffusion of English throughout China.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One barrier to English spreading in China is government control over information. E2- China's “Great Firewall” blocks many English-language websites, and the government promotes Mandarin as the national language. A3- Because people in China have fewer chances to see and use English online or in daily life, English spreads slowly, which helps explain why fewer than 1% of people there speak it.
RUBRIC: Names a barrier: government policy, Mandarin emphasis, internet limits, language difference, few teachers, rural access, no British colonial history, or cultural pride / Describes how it slows English from spreading in China
MINI-LESSON (Barriers to diffusion):
 - Diffusion is how ideas, languages, and products spread from place to place.
 - Barriers slow diffusion. They can be physical (oceans, mountains, deserts), political (laws, censorship), economic (cost of school), or cultural (pride in your own language).
 - China's government promotes Mandarin and blocks many English websites.
 - Mandarin uses characters and tones, so it's very different from English and harder to switch between.
 - Unlike India, China was never a British colony (except Hong Kong), so English never became a language of government.
KEY WORDS: Diffusion = The spread of something from one place to others.; Barrier to diffusion = Anything that slows or stops that spread.
QUICK CHECK: Which is a POLITICAL barrier to diffusion? → A government blocking foreign websites (Political barriers come from governments: laws, censorship, and language policies.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Using the table, explain ONE reason for the difference in the percentage of English speakers in the Netherlands compared with the percentage in China.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Netherlands has a much higher share of English speakers (90%) than China (less than 1%) partly because Dutch and English are closely related languages. E2- Both are Germanic languages in the Indo-European family, so they share many words and grammar patterns, while Mandarin uses tones and characters. A3- Because English is much easier for Dutch speakers to learn, and they hear it constantly through trade and English-language TV, a far larger percentage of Dutch people become fluent.
RUBRIC: Uses the table: the Netherlands is 90% vs. China under 1% / Explains a reason: similar language, trade/globalization, closeness to the UK, media, schools, or China's huge Mandarin-speaking market
MINI-LESSON (Why some places pick up English fast):
 - English and Dutch are both Germanic languages, so many words look and sound alike (water/water, house/huis).
 - Mandarin is a Sino-Tibetan language with tones and characters, so it's much further from English.
 - The Netherlands is small and depends on trade and tourism, so English is useful every day.
 - Dutch TV shows English-language shows with subtitles instead of dubbing, so kids hear English constantly.
KEY WORDS: Language family = A group of languages that came from one common ancestor.; Germanic branch = Part of the Indo-European family: English, Dutch, German, Swedish.
QUICK CHECK: English and Dutch are both part of which language branch? → Germanic (Germanic languages share roots, which makes English easier for Dutch speakers.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE economic reason why a corporation based in an English-speaking country would locate a call center in a country where English is not the dominant language.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A corporation might put a call center in a country like India because labor costs are much lower there. E2- India has about 125 million English speakers, so companies can hire many workers who speak English but earn far lower wages than workers in the United States. A3- Paying lower wages for the same service cuts the company's costs, which raises its profits.

MINI-LESSON (Outsourcing and offshoring):
 - Outsourcing means a company hires another company to do part of its work. Offshoring means moving that work to another country.
 - Wages in India are much lower than in the US or UK.
 - The table shows India has 125 million English speakers: a huge pool of workers.
 - Time zones help too: when it's night in the US, it's daytime in India, so calls can be answered around the clock.
KEY WORDS: Offshoring = Moving business work to another country.; Labor costs = How much a company pays its workers.
QUICK CHECK: The MAIN economic reason companies offshore call centers is… → lower labor costs (Lower wages mean lower costs and higher profits.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---

E2- For example, the Philippines is 95% English-speaking, but it has a much lower GDP per capita than China, where less than 1% speak English. MINI-LESSON (Measuring development):
 - Geographers measure development with indicators like GDP per capita (income per person) and HDI (income, education, and life expectancy).
 - Speaking English is a cultural trait, not a measure of wealth.

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE negative local impact of a global lingua franca on places where a different language is spoken.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One negative local impact of English as a global lingua franca is the loss of local languages. E2- When young people use English for school, jobs, and the internet, they may stop speaking their family's language, so fewer children learn it. A3- As a local language fades, the stories, traditions, and knowledge tied to it can be lost, weakening the place's cultural identity.
RUBRIC: Names a negative impact: language loss or extinction, loss of local culture, cultural homogenization, or inequality for non-English speakers / Explains how the lingua franca causes it locally
MINI-LESSON (When a lingua franca spreads):
 - A global lingua franca helps people trade and communicate across borders.
 - But when English becomes the language of school, jobs, and the internet, local languages can lose status.
 - Young people may stop learning their heritage language, so languages can become endangered or extinct.
 - Losing a language can mean losing traditions and local knowledge. That's part of cultural homogenization.
KEY WORDS: Language extinction = When no one speaks a language anymore.; Cultural homogenization = When cultures around the world become more alike.
QUICK CHECK: When cultures around the world become more alike, it's called… → cultural homogenization (Homogenization means becoming the same. A global lingua franca can speed it up.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Lingua franca
   - A lingua franca is a shared language people use when they don't speak the same first language.
   - The name comes from an old trade language used around the Mediterranean Sea.
   - Swahili in East Africa and Arabic across the Middle East have both served as lingua francas.
   - Today English is the most widely used global lingua franca: in business, science, air travel, and the internet.
• Types of diffusion
   - Relocation diffusion: people move and bring their language or culture with them.
   - Expansion diffusion: an idea spreads outward while staying strong where it started.
   - Its 3 kinds: hierarchical (from powerful people or big cities first), contagious (person to person, like a virus), and stimulus (the idea spreads but gets changed to fit local culture).
   - English spread by relocation (British colonists) and hierarchical diffusion (elites, schools, and businesses first).
• Outsourcing & offshoring
   - Outsourcing means hiring another company to do part of your work. Offshoring means moving work to another country.
   - Companies offshore to cut labor costs, since wages are lower in many developing countries.
   - India and the Philippines became call-center hubs because they have many English-speaking workers.
   - This is part of the new international division of labor: different steps of a business happen in different countries.
• Language families
   - A language family is a group of languages that grew from one ancient ancestor language.
   - Indo-European is the biggest family by number of speakers. Its Germanic branch includes English, Dutch, German, and Swedish.
   - Mandarin belongs to the Sino-Tibetan family and uses tones and characters.
   - Languages in the same branch share words and grammar, so each is easier for the others' speakers to learn.
• Measuring development
   - GDP per capita is the value of everything a country produces, divided by its population.
   - HDI (Human Development Index) combines income, education, and life expectancy.
   - Cultural data, like which language people speak, doesn't directly measure development.
   - Always ask: does this data actually measure what the question is asking about?
• Cultural homogenization
   - Globalization spreads the same brands, music, movies, and languages around the world.
   - When global culture replaces local culture, places can start to look and feel alike.
   - Ethnologue estimates that about 40% of the world's 7,000+ languages are endangered.
   - Many communities fight back with revival programs, like teaching Welsh, Māori, or Hawaiian in schools.

STIMULUS: A Century on Maple Street: One Neighborhood, Three Communities
The passage below describes how a single neighborhood in a mid-sized U.S. city changed over one hundred years as different groups moved in and out. Read it closely before you answer.
1920s. In the 1920s, Maple Street District was the German heart of Ridgeway, a mid-sized Midwestern city. Immigrants from Saxony and Hanover filled its brick row houses. They built St. Johannes Lutheran Church on the corner of Maple and Third, opened beer gardens along the avenue, and printed a weekly German-language newspaper, the Ridgeway Zeitung.
1970s. By the 1970s, most German families had moved to the suburbs. Mexican and Central American families arrived, drawn by factory jobs. The old church building reopened as Our Lady of Guadalupe Catholic Parish. Taquerías and a panadería replaced the beer gardens, a Spanish-language radio station went on the air, and residents began an annual Día de los Muertos festival on Maple Street.
2020s. In the 2020s, young professionals moved in. Coffee shops and a yoga studio opened, and rents rose sharply. The festival is now sponsored by the city and draws thousands of tourists, though several long-running restaurants have closed.

Maple Street District and the city of Ridgeway are fictional and were created for practice.

--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of diffusion that brought German culture to Maple Street District in the 1920s.
MODEL ANSWER: Relocation diffusion: German immigrants moved to Ridgeway and brought their language, religion, and food with them.
RUBRIC: Relocation diffusion / Spread through migration: people moved and brought their culture with them
MINI-LESSON (Relocation vs. expansion diffusion):
 - Relocation diffusion happens when people migrate and carry their culture to a new place. The culture may fade where they left.
 - Expansion diffusion happens when an idea spreads outward and stays strong at its source. It has three types: contagious, hierarchical, and stimulus.
 - Immigrant neighborhoods are the classic example of relocation diffusion: a German church and newspaper appear in a U.S. city because Germans moved there.
 - Quick test: if the sentence has the word “immigrants” or “moved,” it's almost always relocation.
KEY WORDS: Relocation diffusion = Culture spreads because people move and bring it along.; Expansion diffusion = An idea spreads outward while staying strong where it began.
QUICK CHECK: Which situation is relocation diffusion? → Irish immigrants open a Catholic church in Boston (Relocation diffusion needs people to MOVE. The other two are expansion diffusion (hierarchical and contagious).)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Using the passage, describe ONE way the cultural landscape of Maple Street District changed between the 1920s and the 1970s.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The cultural landscape changed as the German community was replaced by a Mexican and Central American one, an example of sequent occupance. E2- The passage says St. Johannes Lutheran Church reopened as Our Lady of Guadalupe Catholic Parish, and taquerías and a panadería replaced the beer gardens along the avenue. A3- The buildings stayed on the same corners, but their names, foods, and religion changed to match the new residents, so each group left its own visible layer on Maple Street.
RUBRIC: Names a specific landscape feature from the passage (church, beer gardens, taquerías, newspaper, radio, signs, foods) / Describes the change: what it was before and what it became
MINI-LESSON (Reading a changing cultural landscape):
 - The cultural landscape is the visible, human-made look of a place: buildings, signs, places of worship, businesses, and street life.
 - When a new group moves into an old neighborhood, it reuses the buildings but changes what's inside and on the signs.
 - Sequent occupance is the name for these stacked layers: Germans built the church, and Latinos reused it as a Catholic parish.
 - For a Describe question, name the feature, then give the before and the after. That's your evidence.
KEY WORDS: Cultural landscape = The human-made look of a place.; Sequent occupance = Each group that lives in a place adds its own layer to the landscape.; Ethnic succession = One ethnic group replacing another in a neighborhood over time.
QUICK CHECK: A Lutheran church that later becomes a Catholic parish is the best example of… → sequent occupance (Sequent occupance means each group leaves its own layer on the same place, often reusing older buildings.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how the Día de los Muertos festival in the 2020s is an example of a cultural practice changing as it spreads to new groups of people.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Día de los Muertos festival shows how a cultural practice can change as it spreads to new groups, a process called stimulus diffusion, along with commodification. E2- The passage says residents began the festival for their own neighborhood in the 1970s, but by the 2020s it is sponsored by the city and draws thousands of tourists. A3- As outsiders adopt the celebration, its purpose shifts from honoring family ancestors to entertaining visitors and bringing money into the city, so the festival keeps its name but loses some of its original religious meaning.
RUBRIC: Names how the festival changed: city-sponsored, tourists, commercial/commodified, mainstream, bigger, or adopted by outsiders (acculturation or stimulus diffusion) / Explains why spreading to new groups changed its meaning or purpose (cause → effect)
MINI-LESSON (When a tradition goes mainstream):
 - Acculturation is when a group adopts parts of another culture. Stimulus diffusion is when an idea spreads but gets changed to fit the new group that adopts it, which is what happened to the festival.
 - Commodification means turning a cultural practice into something to sell or market, like a festival that attracts tourist dollars.
 - Stimulus diffusion is when an idea spreads but gets changed to fit the new group. A tourist festival keeps the skeleton of the original but swaps out some of its meaning.
 - Día de los Muertos is itself syncretic: it blends Indigenous Aztec traditions with Catholic All Souls' Day.
KEY WORDS: Commodification = Turning culture into a product to sell.; Acculturation = Adopting traits of another culture while keeping your own identity.; Stimulus diffusion = An idea spreads but changes to fit the new place.
QUICK CHECK: Turning a cultural tradition into something sold to tourists is called… → commodification (Commodification means making culture into a product. A city-sponsored tourist festival is a classic case.)

--- Part D (Describe) ---
Q: Using the passage, describe ONE way religion is visible in the cultural landscape of Maple Street District.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Religion is visible on Maple Street through its place of worship. E2- The passage says German immigrants built St. Johannes Lutheran Church at the corner of Maple and Third, and the same building later reopened as Our Lady of Guadalupe Catholic Parish. A3- A church building and its name tell anyone walking by which faith the residents practice, so the landscape shows the shift from German Protestants to Mexican and Central American Catholics.
RUBRIC: Names a religious feature from the passage: the church/parish (Lutheran, Catholic, Guadalupe) or the Día de los Muertos festival / Describes how it is visible or what it shows about the residents (building, corner, name, celebrated in the street, which faith)
MINI-LESSON (Spotting religion in a landscape):
 - Religion is one of the most visible cultural traits: churches, mosques, temples, shrines, cemeteries, and religious festivals all shape the look of a place.
 - Christianity is a universalizing religion, so it builds places of worship wherever its followers settle. Catholicism and Protestantism are two branches of it, and Lutheran is a denomination within the Protestant branch.
 - Our Lady of Guadalupe, the Mexican image of the Virgin Mary, is Mexico's most important Catholic symbol, so a parish with that name signals a Mexican community.
 - Día de los Muertos blends Indigenous and Catholic traditions and fills the street with altars, marigolds, and painted skulls for a few days each year.
KEY WORDS: Universalizing religion = A religion that seeks followers everywhere (Christianity, Islam, Buddhism).; Ethnic religion = A religion tied to one group or place (Hinduism, Judaism).; Denomination = A division within a branch, like Lutheran or Baptist within Protestantism.
QUICK CHECK: Which is the BEST example of religion in the cultural landscape? → A Catholic parish building on a street corner (The landscape is what you can see outdoors. A church building is religion made visible.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE centrifugal effect that the changes of the 2020s could have on long-time residents of Maple Street District.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The changes of the 2020s could act as a centrifugal force by making long-time residents feel their community is being taken from them. E2- The passage says rents rose sharply, coffee shops and a yoga studio opened, and several long-running restaurants closed as young professionals moved in. A3- Because the new businesses serve newcomers and tourists rather than the families who built the neighborhood, older residents may feel like outsiders on their own street, which creates tension between the two groups and divides the community, a pattern geographers connect to gentrification.
RUBRIC: Names a dividing effect on long-time residents: displacement, being pushed or priced out, losing gathering places, feeling like outsiders, tension or resentment with newcomers, loss of identity (gentrification) / Explains the cause → effect using the 2020s changes (rising rents, new businesses, closures, newcomers, tourists)
MINI-LESSON (When change pulls a community apart):
 - A centrifugal force is anything that divides a group or weakens its unity.
 - Rising rents can push long-time residents and family businesses out, a process called gentrification (Unit 6).
 - When the taquería where everyone met closes and a yoga studio opens, the community loses a gathering place and may feel the neighborhood is no longer theirs.
 - Newcomers and old-timers may clash over prices and who the festival is for. Keep the focus on division and identity, not just dollars.
KEY WORDS: Centrifugal force = Something that pushes a group apart or weakens unity.; Gentrification = Wealthier people moving into a lower-income area, raising rents and changing businesses.; Displacement = When residents are forced to leave because they can no longer afford to stay.
QUICK CHECK: Which is a CENTRIFUGAL force in a neighborhood? → Long-time residents feeling pushed out by newcomers and rising rents (Centrifugal forces divide. The radio station and the festival are centripetal: they bring people together.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how the Spanish-language radio station is an example of a centripetal force for the community that arrived in the 1970s.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Spanish-language radio station is a centripetal force because it unites the Mexican and Central American community around a shared language and identity. E2- The station broadcasts news, music, and announcements in Spanish, so residents from different countries all hear about neighborhood events like the Día de los Muertos festival and local businesses in their own language. A3- Because people share the same information and culture every day, the station strengthens their sense of belonging and keeps the community connected, even as the neighborhood changes around them.
RUBRIC: Names the unifying effect: unites or connects people, builds shared identity or belonging, preserves language and culture, strengthens community / Explains how the station does it: Spanish language, news, music, information, events, listening together
MINI-LESSON (Ethnic media as glue):
 - A centripetal force is anything that unites a group: a shared language, religion, celebration, or media.
 - Immigrant communities have long built their own media, from the German Ridgeway Zeitung in the 1920s to Spanish radio in the 1970s.
 - Ethnic media spreads news about jobs, church events, and festivals, and it does it in the language people feel at home in.
 - It helps people from different countries, like Mexico and Guatemala, feel like one community, and it keeps their language in daily use.
KEY WORDS: Centripetal force = Something that unites a group and strengthens its identity.; Ethnic media = Newspapers, radio, or TV made by and for one ethnic group.
QUICK CHECK: A newspaper or radio station in an immigrant group's language is best described as a… → centripetal force (Shared media in a shared language pulls a group together. That's centripetal.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE way globalization or popular culture could threaten the local culture of Maple Street District, AND ONE way technology could help residents preserve it.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Globalization and popular culture can threaten Maple Street's local culture, but technology can also help residents protect it. E2- The passage shows coffee shops and a yoga studio opening for newcomers and tourists while long-running family restaurants close; if global chains keep arriving, the district could start to look like any other trendy neighborhood, a step toward cultural homogenization. A3- At the same time, residents could stream the Spanish-language radio station online and post festival videos and family recipes on social media, so young people and families who moved away can still take part, keeping the traditions alive even if the storefronts change.
RUBRIC: Names a threat from globalization or popular culture: chains or global brands, tourists, commercialization, homogenization, local businesses replaced or lost / Names a way technology could preserve local culture: internet, social media, streaming, recording, online archive, apps, videos, podcasts
MINI-LESSON (Globalization: threat and tool):
 - Popular culture spreads fast through companies and media and tends to look the same everywhere. When it crowds out local traditions, geographers call it cultural homogenization.
 - On Maple Street, chain-style coffee shops and tourist crowds could replace the family restaurants and the festival's local meaning.
 - But the same technology that spreads popular culture can protect local culture: communities stream festivals, record elders' stories, and post heritage-language videos.
 - On the exam, a two-part question needs two clearly labeled answers.
KEY WORDS: Cultural homogenization = Places and cultures becoming more alike because of globalization.; Popular culture = Culture spread widely through media and business; similar everywhere.; Local (folk) culture = Culture tied to one place or group, passed down in person.
QUICK CHECK: Globalization spreading the same brands and styles everywhere, so places look alike, is called… → cultural homogenization (Homogenization means becoming the same. Technology can speed it up, but it can also help local cultures survive.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Sequent occupance
   - Sequent occupance is the idea that a place is shaped by every group that has ever lived there, one layer at a time.
   - Each group leaves its mark on the cultural landscape: buildings, signs, foods, places of worship, street names.
   - The next group doesn't erase everything. It reuses and adds to what's already there, like a Lutheran church becoming a Catholic parish.
   - Cultural landscape just means the visible, human-made look of a place.
• Ethnic enclaves
   - An ethnic enclave (or ethnic neighborhood) is an area where one ethnic group clusters together in a city.
   - Immigrants settle near people who share their language, religion, and food because it feels safer and makes daily life easier.
   - Enclaves fill with businesses that serve the group: bakeries, groceries, newspapers, and radio stations in the home language.
   - Many U.S. cities have had a “Little Italy,” a Chinatown, or a German district. Over time one group moves out and another moves in: ethnic succession.
• Acculturation, assimilation, syncretism
   - Acculturation: a group adopts some traits of the surrounding culture but keeps its own identity (speaking English at work, Spanish at home).
   - Assimilation: a group blends in so fully that its original culture mostly disappears, often by the third generation.
   - Syncretism: two cultures mix and create something new. Día de los Muertos itself blends Indigenous Aztec traditions with Catholic All Souls' Day.
   - Commodification: a cultural practice is turned into something to sell, like a festival run for tourists. When a practice spreads but changes to fit the new group, that's also stimulus diffusion.
• Religion in the landscape
   - Religion is one of the easiest cultural traits to spot in a landscape: churches, mosques, temples, shrines, and cemeteries.
   - Universalizing religions like Christianity and Islam try to appeal to everyone, so they spread widely and build in many places.
   - Ethnic religions like Hinduism and Judaism are tied to one group or place and spread mostly by migration.
   - Even a single building can show history: a church may change denomination when the neighborhood changes. Festivals and shrines count too.
• Centripetal vs. centrifugal forces
   - Centripetal forces pull a group together: a shared language, religion, media, or celebration.
   - Centrifugal forces push a group apart: tension between newcomers and old-timers, different languages, or feeling like outsiders in your own area.
   - An ethnic radio station or newspaper is a classic centripetal force for an immigrant community.
   - Rapid neighborhood change can be centrifugal when long-time residents feel their culture is being replaced. These terms return in Unit 4.
• Popular vs. local culture
   - Local (folk) culture belongs to a specific place or group, is passed down in person, and changes slowly.
   - Popular culture spreads fast through media and big companies and looks similar everywhere: chain coffee shops, trending music, the same apps.
   - Globalization helps popular culture spread, which can crowd out local traditions, a process called cultural homogenization.
   - But the same technology can preserve local culture: communities record recipes, stream festivals, and post heritage-language videos online.
