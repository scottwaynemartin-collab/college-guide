BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Latin American city model
   - Geographers Ernst Griffin and Larry Ford created this model in 1980 (Ford updated it in 1996).
   - A commercial spine runs out from the CBD and market, lined by the elite residential sector, where the wealthiest families live.
   - Rings get poorer with distance: the zone of maturity (solid homes, full services), the zone of in situ accretion (modest homes improved bit by bit), and the zone of peripheral squatter settlements at the edge. (Ford's 1996 update added the periférico, a ring highway around the city.)
   - Disamenity sectors are wedges of the very poorest housing along rivers, ravines, rail lines, or dumps, with few or no services.
   - Rich near the center, poor at the edge: roughly the reverse of the classic U.S. pattern.
• Squatter settlements
   - Squatter settlements are homes residents build on land they don't legally own or rent, often from scrap wood and metal.
   - Local names include favelas (Brazil) and pueblos jóvenes (Peru); Dharavi in Mumbai and Kibera in Nairobi are famous examples.
   - Many lack piped water, sewers, paved roads, and legal land titles, and sit on hillsides or floodplains.
   - UN-Habitat estimates that more than 1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements.
• The informal economy
   - The informal economy is work that isn't taxed, licensed, or regulated: street vending, waste picking, day labor.
   - The International Labour Organization estimates that about 60% of the world's workers earn a living informally, mostly in developing countries.
   - It's easy for migrants to enter, but pay is low and unsteady, with no benefits or legal protection.
• Rural-to-urban migration
   - Rural-to-urban migration (moving from countryside to city) and natural increase together drive fast urban growth in developing countries.
   - Push factors (farm machines, too little land, poverty, drought, violence) drive people out; pull factors (jobs, schools, hospitals) draw them in.
   - Latin America is now about 80% urban; the fastest urban growth today is in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
• Primate cities
   - Geographer Mark Jefferson described the primate city in 1939: a country's leading city, usually defined as more than twice the size of the second-largest.
   - A primate city dominates its country's government, economy, and culture, and draws most of its migrants and investment.
   - Examples: Lima, Buenos Aires, Bangkok, and Paris; many in developing countries were colonial capitals or ports.
• The rank-size rule
   - The rank-size rule says the nth-largest city has 1/n the population of the largest: if the largest has 6 million, the 2nd should have 3 million and the 3rd 2 million.
   - Countries that roughly follow it, like the United States, have a balanced set of cities.
   - When the smaller cities fall far below the prediction, the country has a primate city instead.
• Megacities & world cities
   - A megacity has more than 10 million people; the UN counted 33 in 2018, most in developing countries, like Delhi, Dhaka, Cairo, and Lagos.
   - Many grew so fast that housing, water, sewers, and transit couldn't keep up.
   - A world city (global city) is a command center of the global economy, full of banks and corporate headquarters: London, New York, Tokyo.
   - Size isn't power: Lagos is huge but has far less global influence than London.
• Improving informal settlements
   - Slum upgrading adds paving, water, sewers, and power to settlements where they stand, as Rio de Janeiro's Favela-Bairro program did starting in the 1990s.
   - Land titles give residents legal ownership, so they can't be evicted and invest more in their homes.
   - Site-and-service programs, backed by the World Bank since the 1970s, give migrants plots with water, sewer, and road connections to build on.
   - Bus rapid transit (BRT) gives buses their own lanes: Curitiba, Brazil, pioneered it in 1974; Bogotá, Colombia, opened TransMilenio in 2000.

Q: Explain how the zoning data in the table could contribute to high housing prices in Brookhaven.

E2- In Brookhaven, 85% of the outer suburbs' residential land and 71% of the metro's allows only one detached house per lot, so apartments, duplexes, and townhouses that could fit many more families are illegal on most of the land. BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Suburbanization & urban sprawl
   - Suburbanization is the movement of people and homes from the central city outward to the suburbs that ring it.
   - In the United States it boomed after World War II: cheap cars, the Interstate Highway System (begun 1956), and FHA and VA government-backed mortgages made a new house far from downtown easy to afford.
   - Urban sprawl is what that growth looks like on the ground: low-density, car-dependent subdivisions, strip malls, and parking lots spreading over former farmland.
   - Consequences include long commutes and traffic, air pollution, loss of farmland and habitat, the high cost of stretching roads and sewers outward, and metros sorted by income.
   - Edge cities (big clusters of offices and malls at highway exits) grew where the sprawl met the beltways.
• Urban land-use models
   - Burgess concentric zone model (1920s Chicago): rings around a central business district (CBD): a zone of transition with old housing and factories, then working-class homes, then nicer homes, then a commuter zone.
   - Hoyt sector model (1939): land uses grow in wedges along railroads and major roads, so a high-rent sector can stretch from downtown to the edge.
   - Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model (1945): a city has several centers (airport, university, industrial park, suburban business district), not just one downtown.
   - Galactic or peripheral model: an old core surrounded by a highway beltway with edge cities and suburbs scattered along it, the classic post-1970 American metro.
   - Latin American city model: a wealthy spine runs out from the CBD, squatter settlements ring the far edge, and disamenity sectors (along rivers or rail lines) cut through the city.
• Gentrification
   - Gentrification happens when wealthier, often younger, residents move into an older lower-income neighborhood near downtown, renovate the housing, and open new businesses.
   - It is pulled by cheap older homes, short commutes to downtown jobs, walkable streets, and a taste for city life among young professionals and retirees.
   - Upsides: higher property values, more tax money for the city, new shops, and reinvestment in buildings that had been neglected.
   - Downsides: rents and property taxes climb, so long-time lower-income residents, especially renters, are displaced, and the neighborhood's culture and businesses change.
   - Many gentrifying neighborhoods were once redlined, which is why their housing was cheap enough to attract investors in the first place.
• Zoning & single-family-only rules
   - Zoning is the set of local laws that say what can be built on each piece of land: homes, shops, factories, and how big or tall.
   - Single-family-only zoning allows just one detached house per lot, so duplexes, townhouses, and apartment buildings are illegal there.
   - In many U.S. cities most residential land is zoned this way, which caps how many homes can fit even when demand is high.
   - Because it holds down supply and keeps out cheaper housing types, critics call it exclusionary zoning; it is one reason housing is so expensive in many metros.
   - Minneapolis and the state of Oregon have ended single-family-only zoning so that duplexes and triplexes can be built on any residential lot.
• Smart growth & New Urbanism
   - Smart growth is a set of planning policies that fight sprawl by making metros compact, walkable, and transit-friendly.
   - Tools include urban growth boundaries (Portland, Oregon has had one since 1979) and greenbelts that protect open land at the edge, transit-oriented development around stations, mixed-use zoning, and infill building on vacant or brownfield (former industrial) lots.
   - New Urbanism is the design movement behind many of these ideas: walkable blocks, homes close to shops and schools, front porches, and a mix of housing types, as in Seaside, Florida and Kentlands, Maryland.
   - Supporters say these policies cut driving and protect farmland; critics worry that limiting land for building can push housing prices up unless cities also allow more homes inside the boundary.
• Redlining, blockbusting & white flight
   - In the 1930s a federal agency (the HOLC) drew maps grading neighborhoods for mortgage risk; areas with Black and immigrant residents were colored red and labeled 'hazardous,' so banks refused to lend there. That is redlining.
   - Blockbusting: real-estate agents frightened white homeowners into selling cheap by warning that Black families were moving in, then resold the homes to Black buyers at high prices.
   - White flight: millions of white families left central cities for the suburbs from the 1950s to the 1970s, helped by highways and FHA loans that redlined neighborhoods could not get.
   - The Fair Housing Act of 1968 banned housing discrimination, but the legacy remains: disinvested neighborhoods, lower home values, and a large racial wealth gap.
   - Today many formerly redlined neighborhoods near downtown are the ones gentrifying.
