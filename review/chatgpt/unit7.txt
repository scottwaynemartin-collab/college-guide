--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the spatial pattern of the railway network shown on the map.
MODEL ANSWER: The railway lines run from the interior of India and converge on a few coastal port cities, such as Bombay and Calcutta.
RUBRIC: Lines link the interior to coastal ports / Lines converge on a few major cities (e.g., Bombay, Calcutta)
MINI-LESSON (Reading spatial patterns):
 - Geographers describe where things are and how they're arranged: clustered, spread out, in a line, or pointing toward one place.
 - When many lines meet at one spot, they converge. That spot is usually important, like a port or a capital.
 - On this map, ask: do the rails connect interior cities to EACH OTHER, or do they mostly run toward the coast?
KEY WORDS: Spatial pattern = How things are arranged across space.; Converge = Come together at one point.; Interior = Land far from the coast.
QUICK CHECK: When many rail lines meet at one place, geographers say the lines… → converge (Converge means come together. On this map, the lines converge on coastal ports.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe how the pattern of the railway network reflects Great Britain's economic goals in colonial India.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The railway pattern reflects Britain's goal of extracting raw materials from India. E2- The lines mostly run from crop-growing regions in the interior, like cotton in the Deccan and jute in Bengal, to the ports of Bombay and Calcutta. A3- This let raw materials move quickly to ships bound for factories in Britain, which is exactly what a colonial economy was built to do.
RUBRIC: Names the goal: extracting raw materials or cash crops for Britain / Links the rail pattern to moving goods from the interior to ports for export
MINI-LESSON (Colonies as raw-material suppliers):
 - During the Industrial Revolution, British factories needed huge amounts of raw materials, like cotton for cloth mills.
 - Colonies like India supplied those raw materials cheaply and bought back finished British goods.
 - Colonial infrastructure was often built to move resources OUT, from farms to ports, rather than to connect local towns.
KEY WORDS: Raw material = An unprocessed resource (cotton, jute, wheat) used to make products.; Extraction = Taking resources out of a place to use elsewhere.; Export = Goods shipped out to another country.
QUICK CHECK: Why did British factories need colonies like India? → For cheap raw materials like cotton (Colonies supplied raw materials; Britain made the finished goods.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how colonial rule contributed to the growth of port cities such as Bombay and Calcutta.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Colonial rule made Bombay and Calcutta grow because they became the gateways between India and Britain. E2- The rail lines ended there, so these cities handled the exports leaving India and the British goods arriving, which concentrated jobs in shipping, warehouses, banking, and government. A3- Those jobs pulled in rural migrants, and more people brought more businesses, so they became some of South Asia's largest cities.
RUBRIC: Names a colonial function: export/trade hub, rail terminus, or administrative center / Explains growth through jobs, investment, or migration
MINI-LESSON (How colonial port cities grew):
 - Every railway on the map ends at a port, so goods and money funneled through a few cities.
 - Moving goods takes workers: dockworkers, clerks, bankers, merchants, and railway staff.
 - Jobs attract rural-to-urban migrants, and more people bring more businesses, so the city snowballs.
 - Calcutta was also the capital of British India until 1911, so government jobs clustered there too.
KEY WORDS: Rural-to-urban migration = Moving from the countryside to a city, usually for jobs.; Break-of-bulk point = A place where goods switch transport types (train → ship).
QUICK CHECK: What usually pulls rural migrants into a growing port city? → Jobs in trade and shipping (Jobs are the main pull factor, and ports created lots of them.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE way the colonial emphasis on export cash crops affected rural communities in South Asia.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The colonial push for export cash crops made rural families more vulnerable to hunger. E2- Farmers were pressured to grow cotton and jute instead of food crops, so less land produced food and families depended on world prices they couldn't control. A3- Because of this, when prices fell or a drought hit, people couldn't afford food, and drought years turned into deadly famines.
RUBRIC: Names an effect: less food grown, famine, debt, poverty, or dependence on world prices / Explains the cause-and-effect link to cash-crop farming
MINI-LESSON (Cash crops vs. food crops):
 - Subsistence farmers grow food for their own families. Cash-crop farmers grow crops to sell.
 - When land switches from rice or millet to cotton or jute, families have to BUY food with their crop money.
 - If world prices drop or a drought hits, they can't afford food. Famines in late-1800s British India killed millions.
 - Many farmers also borrowed for seed and taxes, which trapped them in debt.
KEY WORDS: Cash crop = A crop grown to sell, often for export.; Subsistence agriculture = Farming to feed your own family, not to sell.
QUICK CHECK: A cash crop is grown mainly to… → sell, often for export (Cash crops are for selling. Food for the family is subsistence farming.)

--- Part E (Describe) ---
Q: Describe how the economic relationship shown on the map illustrates Wallerstein's world-system theory.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- In world-system theory, Britain was the core and India the periphery. E2- The map shows India sending low-value raw materials like cotton and jute to ports for export to Britain. A3- Britain turned those materials into manufactured goods and kept the higher profits and industrial jobs, which is how the core grows wealthy from the periphery.
RUBRIC: Places Britain as the core and India as the periphery / Describes the flow: raw materials out to the core, manufactured goods or profits kept by the core
MINI-LESSON (World-system theory in 60 seconds):
 - Wallerstein said the world economy is one connected system with three tiers.
 - Core: rich, industrial countries that make high-value goods (colonial Britain).
 - Periphery: poorer places that supply raw materials and cheap labor (colonial India).
 - Wealth flows toward the core, because turning raw materials into products is where the money is made.
KEY WORDS: Core = Wealthy, industrial, high-skill economies.; Periphery = Poorer economies supplying raw materials and low-wage labor.; Semi-periphery = In-between countries with some industry.
QUICK CHECK: In world-system theory, colonial India was part of the… → periphery (The periphery supplies raw materials; the core (Britain) made the high-value goods.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE way the colonial transportation network continues to affect patterns of economic development in South Asia today.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The colonial transportation network still concentrates development in the old port cities. E2- Because colonial railways tied the economy to a few ports, cities like Mumbai and Kolkata got a head start in infrastructure, industry, and finance. A3- That infrastructure keeps attracting new businesses and migrants (cumulative causation), so development is still uneven, with many interior rural regions lagging behind.
RUBRIC: Names a present-day pattern: old ports remain top economic hubs, or coast/interior development is uneven / Explains why: existing infrastructure keeps attracting investment, industry, and migrants
MINI-LESSON (Colonial legacy & cumulative causation):
 - Mumbai (old Bombay) is India's financial center today, and Kolkata, Chennai, and Karachi are still major hubs.
 - Places that got railways, ports, and businesses early had a head start, and that advantage tends to grow.
 - Geographers call this cumulative causation: investment attracts more investment.
 - Interior rural regions with fewer old connections often lag behind. That's uneven development.
KEY WORDS: Uneven development = Some regions develop much faster than others.; Cumulative causation = Growth in a place attracts more growth (a snowball).
QUICK CHECK: Cumulative causation means… → places that grow early attract even more growth (It's a snowball: investment attracts more investment.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Cash crops & plantations
   - A cash crop is grown to sell, often for export, rather than to feed the farmer's own family.
   - Colonial powers pushed colonies to specialize in a few crops their home factories needed, like cotton, jute (for sacks and rope), and tea.
   - Plantations are large estates that grow one crop for export, often with cheap or forced labor. British-owned tea estates in Assam are an example.
   - Depending on one or two exports leaves a region exposed to world prices it can't control.
• Colonial port cities
   - Colonizers built port cities as gateways, where raw materials left the colony and manufactured goods came in.
   - Bombay (Mumbai), Calcutta (Kolkata), and Madras (Chennai) grew from small trading posts into major cities under British rule.
   - Calcutta was the capital of British India until 1911, when the capital moved to Delhi.
   - Many former colonies still have a primate city, one city far larger than all the others, that began as a colonial port.
• Famines in colonial India
   - Several deadly famines struck British India in the late 1800s. The Great Famine of 1876–78 alone killed millions of people.
   - Droughts caused the crop failures, but colonial policy made them worse: grain kept being exported, and relief was limited.
   - Farmers who grew cash crops had little stored food. They depended on wages and market prices to buy it.
   - Historians point to these famines as evidence that colonial economic choices shaped life-and-death outcomes.
• Wallerstein's world-system theory
   - Immanuel Wallerstein divided the world economy into the core, the semi-periphery, and the periphery.
   - Core countries are wealthy and industrialized, and they make high-value goods.
   - Periphery countries supply raw materials and cheap labor, and they earn far less.
   - Colonialism helped build this pattern by locking colonies into shipping raw materials to the core.
   - The theory's big idea: countries are linked, and the core's wealth depends partly on the periphery.
• Uneven development
   - Development is rarely spread evenly inside a country.
   - Places that get early investment, such as ports, railways, and factories, attract more businesses and workers, which attracts even more investment.
   - Geographers call this snowball effect cumulative causation.
   - The result is a core region that keeps growing while peripheral regions fall behind, and the gap can last for generations.

Unit 7 · The Factory Floor (Practice FRQ · No stimulus)
======================================================================
STIMULUS: (no stimulus)
Every factory sits where it does for a reason: close to its raw materials, its customers, or cheap workers. When those costs change, whole industries move, and the places they leave behind have to find a new way to make a living.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify ONE of the three location factors in Alfred Weber's least-cost theory of industrial location.
MODEL ANSWER: Transportation costs: the cost of moving raw materials to the factory and finished products to the market.
RUBRIC: Transportation costs / Labor costs / Agglomeration
MINI-LESSON (Weber's least-cost theory):
 - Alfred Weber said in 1909 that a factory locates where its total costs are lowest.
 - Transportation (moving raw materials in and goods out) usually matters most, but much cheaper labor elsewhere can pull a factory away.
 - Agglomeration means firms save money by clustering near similar firms, like tech companies in Silicon Valley.
KEY WORDS: Least-cost theory = Weber's idea that factories locate where their total costs are lowest.; Agglomeration = The clustering of similar businesses in one area so they can share resources and save money.
QUICK CHECK: Which is one of the three factors in Weber's least-cost theory? → Agglomeration (Weber's three factors are transportation, labor, and agglomeration.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe why a bulk-reducing (weight-losing) industry, such as copper smelting, usually locates near its source of raw materials.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A bulk-reducing industry locates near its raw materials because its finished product weighs much less than its inputs. E2- Copper ore is usually about 1 percent copper or less, so mills and smelters near mines in places like Arizona and Chile remove tons of waste rock to get a little metal. A3- Processing near the mine means only the lighter copper gets shipped, not heavy waste rock, which keeps transportation costs low.
RUBRIC: Says the finished product weighs less than the raw materials, because waste is removed / Describes the payoff: processing near the mine cuts transportation (shipping) costs
MINI-LESSON (Bulk-reducing vs. bulk-gaining):
 - A bulk-reducing industry makes a product that weighs less than its raw materials, so it locates near them. Copper ore is about 1 percent copper or less.
 - A bulk-gaining industry does the opposite: soft-drink bottlers add water, so they locate near customers (the market).
 - Either way, the goal is to keep transportation costs low.
KEY WORDS: Bulk-reducing industry = An industry whose final product weighs less than its inputs, so it locates near raw materials.; Bulk-gaining industry = An industry whose final product weighs more than its inputs, so it locates near the market.
QUICK CHECK: Which industry is bulk-GAINING and usually locates near its market? → Soft-drink bottling (Bottlers add water, making the product heavier, so they locate near customers.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason the Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain in the 1700s.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One reason the Industrial Revolution began in Great Britain is that it had large deposits of coal and iron ore. E2- Coal fueled the steam engines James Watt improved in the 1760s and 1770s, iron built the machines, and canals like the Bridgewater Canal (1761) moved coal cheaply to Manchester. A3- Because fuel and metal were cheap and close by, British owners could build steam-powered factories before any other country.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: coal, iron, capital, colonies, rivers/canals, workers, inventions, or political stability / Explains how it helped industry start: powered machines, paid for factories, or moved goods cheaply
MINI-LESSON (Why Britain went first):
 - Britain had coal and iron ore: coal fueled steam engines and iron built the machines.
 - Colonies and trade supplied raw cotton, profits to invest (capital), and markets; rivers and canals made shipping cheap.
 - The Agricultural Revolution pushed rural workers off the land and into factory jobs.
KEY WORDS: Industrial Revolution = The shift from making goods by hand to making them with machines and new power sources like steam.; Capital = Money saved up and invested to build businesses, like factories and machines.
QUICK CHECK: Which advantage helped Britain industrialize first? → Large deposits of coal and iron ore (Coal powered steam engines and iron built machines. Oil and electricity came later.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE cause of deindustrialization in the Rust Belt of the United States since the 1970s.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One cause of deindustrialization in the Rust Belt is offshoring: companies moved factory work to countries where labor costs much less. E2- After NAFTA (1994) and China's entry into the World Trade Organization (2001), many makers of auto parts and appliances opened plants in Mexico and China, where wages were far lower than in Ohio or Michigan. A3- Because the same products could be made more cheaply abroad, companies closed or shrank Rust Belt plants, which led to the loss of hundreds of thousands of factory jobs.
RUBRIC: Names a cause: automation, offshoring to lower-wage countries, or foreign competition / Explains how it cut Rust Belt jobs: lower costs elsewhere, fewer workers needed, plants closed or moved
MINI-LESSON (Why the factory jobs left):
 - Automation: robots do many factory tasks, so U.S. factories make far more than in 1980 with millions fewer workers.
 - Offshoring: companies moved production to lower-wage countries, like Mexico after NAFTA (1994) and China after it joined the WTO (2001).
 - Global competition: in the 1970s and 1980s, Japanese cars and cheaper foreign steel took customers from U.S. automakers and mills.
KEY WORDS: Deindustrialization = The loss of manufacturing jobs and factories in a region.; Automation = Using machines, robots, and computers to do work people used to do.; Offshoring = Moving production or other work to another country.
QUICK CHECK: Replacing assembly-line workers with robots is an example of… → automation (Automation means machines do work people used to do, so plants need fewer workers.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Using ONE real Rust Belt city as an example, explain ONE economic or social effect of deindustrialization on that city.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One effect of deindustrialization on Detroit, Michigan, was a huge loss of population, which shrank the city's tax base. E2- As auto plants closed or moved away, workers lost their jobs and left: Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 640,000 in 2020, leaving tens of thousands of houses vacant. A3- Because far fewer people and businesses paid taxes, the city struggled to pay for streetlights and police, which helped push Detroit into bankruptcy in 2013.
RUBRIC: Names an effect: job loss, population loss, a shrinking tax base, vacant buildings, or a shift to service jobs / Uses a real Rust Belt city as the example / Explains how losing factories caused the effect
MINI-LESSON (After the factories close):
 - Economic effects: unemployment and a shrinking tax base, so the city has less money for schools, police, and streetlights.
 - Social effects: people move away, leaving vacant houses, polluted old factory sites (brownfields), and higher poverty. Youngstown, Ohio, shrank from about 170,000 people in 1950 to about 60,000.
 - Some cities reinvent themselves: Pittsburgh replaced steel jobs with service and quaternary jobs in hospitals, universities, and tech.
KEY WORDS: Tax base = The people and businesses in a place that pay taxes to fund local services.; Brownfield = An abandoned industrial site that may be polluted and needs cleanup before reuse.
QUICK CHECK: Which auto-industry city lost about two-thirds of its population after 1950? → Detroit, Michigan (Detroit, the Motor City, shrank from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 640,000 in 2020.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how microloans can promote economic development, especially for women in developing countries.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Microloans promote development by lending small amounts to poor people, especially women, who are shut out of regular banks because they have no collateral. E2- Bangladesh's Grameen Bank lends mostly to women, who use the money to buy a milk cow, a sewing machine, or a mobile phone to rent to neighbors, then repay from their sales. A3- Because she now earns her own income, a borrower can spend more on her children's food, health, and schooling and gains a bigger voice in family decisions, which reduces poverty and empowers women.
RUBRIC: Says what microloans do: small loans that let poor women shut out of banks start small businesses / Explains the payoff: income for her family, children's schooling and health, or empowerment
MINI-LESSON (Microloans: small money, big change):
 - Banks usually want collateral (property they can take if you don't pay), and poor women often own little or none. A microloan skips that.
 - Grameen Bank in Bangladesh lends mostly to women (about 97 percent of borrowers); it and its founder, Muhammad Yunus, won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
 - Women tend to spend earnings on their children's food, health, and schooling, so the benefits reach the next generation.
KEY WORDS: Microloan (microcredit) = A very small loan to start or grow a tiny business.; Collateral = Property a borrower promises to give up if the loan isn't repaid.; Empowerment = Gaining more control over your own life and choices.
QUICK CHECK: Grameen Bank, a pioneer of microloans to women, was founded in which country? → Bangladesh (Muhammad Yunus started Grameen Bank in Bangladesh; about 97 percent of its borrowers are women.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE way ecotourism can support sustainable development, and explain ONE drawback of ecotourism.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Ecotourism can support sustainable development because it turns protecting nature into a source of jobs and income. E2- Costa Rica protects about a quarter of its land in parks and reserves, and tourists pay to see its cloud forests, creating jobs for local guides and lodge owners; in Rwanda, visitors pay $1,500 each to see mountain gorillas. A3- Because living forests and gorillas earn money year after year, locals have a reason to protect them for future generations; however, one drawback is that heavy tourism can damage the places visitors come to see: crowds trample trails, disturb wildlife, and lodges and roads clear forest, so if visitor numbers aren't limited, ecotourism can destroy the nature that earns the money.
RUBRIC: Explains how ecotourism brings money or jobs from visitors who come to see nature / Connects it to sustainability: gives people a reason to protect nature for the future / Explains a drawback: crowds damage nature, travel pollution, or profits leaving the community
MINI-LESSON (Ecotourism and sustainability):
 - Sustainable development balances the economy, society, and the environment so future generations can meet their needs too.
 - Costa Rica's forests have grown back from about a fifth of the country in the 1980s to about half today, helped by tourism money.
 - Drawbacks: too many visitors can damage fragile places, flights add carbon emissions, and profits may leak to foreign-owned companies.
KEY WORDS: Sustainable development = Development that meets today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.; Ecotourism = Tourism based on visiting nature in a way that protects it and helps local communities.
QUICK CHECK: Which BEST describes sustainable development? → Meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs (It balances the economy, society, and the environment, now and for the future.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Weber's least-cost theory
   - Alfred Weber's least-cost theory (1909) says a factory locates where its total costs are lowest.
   - His three factors: transportation (moving raw materials in and products out), labor (wages), and agglomeration (savings from clustering near similar firms).
   - Transportation usually matters most. Weber drew it as a triangle between two raw-material sources and one market.
• Bulk-reducing, bulk-gaining & break-of-bulk
   - A bulk-reducing (weight-losing) industry makes a product lighter than its inputs, so it locates near its raw materials, like copper smelting.
   - A bulk-gaining industry makes a product heavier, so it locates near its market, like soft-drink bottling, which adds water.
   - A break-of-bulk point, like a seaport, is where goods switch from one kind of transportation to another. Factories locate there to avoid loading and unloading twice.
• The Industrial Revolution
   - The Industrial Revolution, the shift from hand tools to machines powered by water and steam, began in Great Britain in the late 1700s.
   - Britain had coal and iron, capital from trade and colonies, rivers and canals, and workers pushed off farms by the Agricultural Revolution.
   - It spread to Western Europe and the U.S. in the early 1800s, Japan after 1868, and after World War II to newly industrialized countries like South Korea, then China.
• Deindustrialization & the Rust Belt
   - Deindustrialization is the loss of factory jobs in a region that once depended on them, like the U.S. Rust Belt around the Great Lakes.
   - U.S. factory jobs peaked at about 19.5 million in 1979, then fell by about a third because of automation, offshoring, and foreign competition.
   - Some Rust Belt cities, like Pittsburgh, shifted to service and quaternary jobs in hospitals, universities, and tech.
• The new international division of labor
   - The new international division of labor (NIDL) splits the steps of making one product among different countries.
   - Design, research, and marketing usually stay in wealthy core countries, while assembly moves to lower-wage countries.
   - Nike designs shoes in Oregon but has them made in Vietnam, Indonesia, and China, through outsourcing (hiring other companies) and offshoring (moving work abroad).
• Microloans
   - A microloan is a very small loan, often a few hundred dollars or less, for people too poor to borrow from regular banks.
   - Muhammad Yunus founded Grameen Bank in Bangladesh in 1983. About 97 percent of its borrowers are women, and he and the bank won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
   - Critics warn about high interest rates and tiny businesses that never grow, so microloans can't end poverty alone.
• Ecotourism
   - Ecotourism is travel to natural areas that aims to protect the environment and benefit local people.
   - Costa Rica protects about a quarter of its land in parks and reserves. Rwanda charges $1,500 for a mountain gorilla permit and shares park revenue with nearby villages.
   - Drawbacks: crowds damage fragile places, flights add carbon emissions, and profits can leak away to foreign-owned companies.
• The UN Sustainable Development Goals
   - Sustainable development means meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
   - In 2015, UN members adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for 2030, like no poverty (Goal 1), gender equality (Goal 5), decent work (Goal 8), and life on land (Goal 15).
   - Microloans to women support Goals 1 and 5; well-run ecotourism supports Goals 8 and 15.

Q: Using the table, explain why a country can have a high GNI per capita but a lower HDI than its income would predict.

 - A high Gender Inequality Index is a clue: when women are kept out of school and work, half the population's health and education lag behind.
 - So when GNI is high but HDI is low, look for concentrated wealth and low spending on people.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Measures of development
   - GNI per capita is a country's total income divided by its population. “PPP” (purchasing power parity) adjusts for the fact that a dollar buys more in some countries than in others.
   - HDI, the Human Development Index, combines income, years of schooling, and life expectancy into one score from 0 to 1. Above 0.80 counts as “very high” development.
   - Other common measures: literacy rate, infant mortality rate, and the share of workers in each economic sector.
   - Income measures what a country earns; HDI measures whether that money reaches people's health and education. When the two disagree, something interesting is going on.
• Rostow's stages of growth
   - W. W. Rostow (1960) said every country moves through five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and high mass consumption.
   - Take-off is the big moment: a few industries grow fast, cities swell, and the country starts to industrialize.
   - High mass consumption is the top stage: most workers are in services, and people spend on cars, electronics, and vacations.
   - Critics say the model is based on the history of the United States and Western Europe, assumes every country can follow the same path, and ignores how colonialism and global trade can hold countries back.
   - Wallerstein's world-system theory answers that criticism by looking at the whole world at once: a wealthy core, a semi-periphery, and a periphery that supplies raw materials and cheap labor.
• Gender Inequality Index
   - The Gender Inequality Index (GII) is a UN measure from 0 to 1 that compares women and men. Lower is better; 0 would mean full equality.
   - It combines three things: reproductive health (maternal deaths and teen births), empowerment (seats in parliament and secondary schooling), and participation in the paid workforce.
   - Countries with a high GII usually have fewer girls finishing school, fewer women in paid jobs, and more early marriages.
   - Development experts argue that educating and employing women is one of the fastest ways to raise a country's HDI, which is why microloans to women, like those from Bangladesh's Grameen Bank, are so popular.
• Commodity dependence
   - A commodity is a raw product sold in bulk at a world price: oil, copper, coffee, cocoa, cotton.
   - A country is commodity-dependent when most of its export earnings come from one or two commodities. Nigeria and Venezuela, for example, get most of theirs from oil.
   - World prices for commodities swing wildly. Oil fell from over $100 a barrel in 2014 to under $30 in early 2016.
   - When the price drops, the country's income, government budget, and jobs all drop at once. Some geographers call this trap the “resource curse.”
   - The usual advice is to diversify: build manufacturing and services so that one price can't sink the whole economy.
• Special economic zones
   - A special economic zone (SEZ) is an area where a government relaxes its normal rules to attract foreign factories: lower taxes, fewer tariffs, less paperwork.
   - An export processing zone (EPZ) is a type of SEZ focused on assembling goods for export. Mexico's maquiladoras along the U.S. border are a famous example.
   - China's first SEZ, Shenzhen, grew from a fishing town of about 30,000 people in 1980 into a city of more than 17 million.
   - Companies get cheap labor and easy exporting; the host country gets jobs, technology, and foreign investment.
   - Downsides can include low wages, weak worker protections, pollution, and companies that leave once the tax breaks end.
• Economic sectors
   - Primary sector: taking things from the earth (farming, fishing, mining). Secondary: making things (factories, construction). Tertiary: services (stores, schools, hospitals, banks).
   - Some books add quaternary (information and research) and quinary (top-level decision-making).
   - As a country develops, workers shift from primary to secondary to tertiary. Rich countries usually have under 5% of workers in farming and over 70% in services.
   - A big share of workers in agriculture usually signals low incomes, because farms in poor countries produce little per worker and most of it is eaten, not sold.
