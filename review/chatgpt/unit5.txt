Unit 5 · Miracle Seeds (Practice FRQ · No stimulus)
======================================================================
STIMULUS: (no stimulus)
Since 1950 the world's population has more than tripled to over 8 billion, and farmers now grow enough food to feed everyone, yet hundreds of millions of people still go hungry. This FRQ asks how farming changed to feed a growing world, who got left out, and how to keep it going without wearing out the land.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify ONE agricultural innovation associated with the Green Revolution.
MODEL ANSWER: High-yield varieties (HYVs) of wheat and rice, like the short, sturdy wheat Norman Borlaug bred in Mexico.
RUBRIC: Names high-yield seed varieties (HYVs) / Names another input: fertilizer, pesticides, irrigation, or machines
MINI-LESSON (The Green Revolution package):
 - The Green Revolution spread new farm technology to developing countries, from Mexico in the 1940s to India and Pakistan in the 1960s.
 - Its core was high-yield varieties (HYVs): wheat and rice bred to grow much more grain per acre.
 - Those seeds needed chemical fertilizer, pesticides, and steady irrigation, and many farms also bought tractors and pumps.
KEY WORDS: High-yield variety (HYV) = A crop bred to produce much more food per acre.; Mechanization = Replacing human or animal labor with machines.
QUICK CHECK: Which was NOT part of the original Green Revolution of the 1960s? → Genetically modified (GMO) crops (GMOs came later, in the 1990s. The original Green Revolution used crossbred high-yield seeds plus chemicals and water.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE way the Green Revolution changed food production in a specific country, such as India or Mexico.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Green Revolution greatly increased how much food India's farms produced per acre. E2- Starting in the mid-1960s, farmers in the Punjab region planted high-yield wheat with chemical fertilizer and irrigation, and India's wheat harvest about doubled in less than ten years. A3- This turned India from a country that depended on U.S. wheat shipments into one that, by the late 1970s, grew enough grain to feed its own people.
RUBRIC: Names a change: higher yields, more food, or self-sufficiency / Gives proof from a real country (India, Mexico…)
MINI-LESSON (Before and after):
 - Mexico once imported much of its wheat; with Borlaug's seeds it grew enough for itself by the mid-1950s.
 - In the mid-1960s India faced droughts and relied on U.S. wheat shipments.
 - Punjab farmers planted the new wheat and rice with fertilizer and tube-well irrigation, and India's wheat harvest about doubled in less than a decade.
 - Faster-growing varieties let many farmers raise two crops a year.
KEY WORDS: Yield = How much crop a piece of land produces.; Food self-sufficiency = Growing enough food to feed your own people without relying on imports.
QUICK CHECK: Which region became India's “breadbasket” during the Green Revolution? → Punjab (Punjab farmers adopted high-yield wheat and rice early, making it India's top grain region.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE negative environmental consequence of the Green Revolution.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One negative environmental consequence of the Green Revolution is the depletion of groundwater. E2- In India's Punjab region, high-yield rice and wheat need heavy irrigation, so farmers pump water from tube wells faster than rain can refill the aquifer, and the water table has dropped year after year. A3- Because the farms depend on that water, a falling water table threatens to make the high yields impossible to keep up, putting future food production at risk.
RUBRIC: Names a harm: groundwater depletion, salty soil, pollution, or lost crop diversity / Explains how Green Revolution farming causes it
MINI-LESSON (The hidden costs of high yields):
 - High-yield seeds need lots of irrigation water and fertilizer to produce big harvests.
 - In India's Punjab, farmers pump out more groundwater than rain puts back, so the water table keeps falling.
 - In dry places, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salt behind (salinization), which can ruin fields.
 - Planting the same few varieties everywhere (monoculture) reduces crop diversity, so one disease can spread farther.
KEY WORDS: Aquifer = An underground layer of rock or sand that holds water.; Monoculture = Growing just one crop over a large area.
QUICK CHECK: Pumping groundwater faster than rain can refill it causes… → aquifer depletion (The underground water supply shrinks, and wells have to go deeper.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason the Green Revolution benefited large farms more than small farms.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Green Revolution helped large farms more because its high-yield package was expensive, and wealthy farmers could afford it more easily. E2- In India, the new seeds only paid off with bought fertilizer, pesticides, and irrigation pumps, and large landowners could get bank loans using their land as collateral, while small farmers often borrowed from moneylenders at high interest. A3- So large farms earned bigger profits and bought more land, while many small farmers fell into debt and some sold their land, widening the gap between rich and poor farmers.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: the inputs were costly, and big farmers had more money or credit / Explains how that helped large farms or hurt small ones
MINI-LESSON (Why the gains weren't equal):
 - The high-yield package (seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, water, machines) had to be paid for before the harvest.
 - Large landowners could use land as collateral for cheaper bank loans; small farmers often paid moneylenders high interest.
 - Some small farmers fell into debt, sold their land, and became landless laborers, so rural inequality grew.
KEY WORDS: Collateral = Property, like land, that a borrower gives up if they can't repay a loan.; Landless laborer = A farmworker who owns no land and works others' fields for wages.
QUICK CHECK: Why did many small farmers fall into debt during the Green Revolution? → They borrowed to buy seeds, fertilizer, and irrigation (The package cost money up front, so poorer farmers had to borrow, often at high interest.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason the Green Revolution had less impact in sub-Saharan Africa than in South Asia.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Green Revolution had less impact in sub-Saharan Africa because its high-yield seeds were bred for crops and conditions that didn't match most African farms. E2- Scientists focused on irrigated wheat and rice, but many farmers in sub-Saharan Africa grow staples like cassava, sorghum, and millet on rain-fed fields, and only a small share of the region's farmland is irrigated. A3- Since the new seeds didn't fit Africa's main crops or its dependence on rainfall, yields there rose much less than in South Asia.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: different staples, little irrigation, high costs, or poor roads / Explains how that limited the benefits
MINI-LESSON (Why Africa missed most of the boom):
 - Early Green Revolution seeds were wheat and rice bred for irrigated land.
 - Much of sub-Saharan Africa relies on staples like cassava, sorghum, millet, and yams, which got far less research attention.
 - Most farms there are rain-fed: well under 10 percent of the region's cropland is irrigated.
 - Poor roads, little credit, and costly fertilizer made the package hard to get, especially for small farmers.
KEY WORDS: Staple crop = The main food that makes up most of what people eat.; Rain-fed farming = Farming that depends only on rainfall, with no irrigation.
QUICK CHECK: Which crop is a major staple in much of sub-Saharan Africa? → Cassava (Cassava, like sorghum, millet, and yams, wasn't a focus of the early Green Revolution, which centered on wheat and rice.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE example of a sustainable farming practice and how it reduces environmental harm caused by agriculture.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One sustainable farming practice is conservation tillage, such as no-till farming. E2- Instead of plowing the whole field each year, many farmers in places like the U.S. Midwest, Brazil, and Argentina plant seeds directly into the stalks and leaves left over from last year's crop. A3- Because that leftover plant cover protects the ground from wind and rain, much less soil erodes away, which keeps fields fertile and keeps mud out of nearby rivers.
RUBRIC: Names a sustainable practice (no-till, crop rotation, terracing, organic, CSA…) / Explains how it reduces a specific harm (erosion, water use, chemicals)
MINI-LESSON (Farming that can last):
 - Sustainable agriculture protects soil, water, and ecosystems so farms can keep producing in the future.
 - Conservation tillage (such as no-till) leaves crop leftovers on the field instead of plowing, which cuts soil erosion.
 - Crop rotation with legumes like soybeans or clover adds nitrogen back to the soil naturally.
 - Terracing cuts steps into hillsides so rain can't carry soil downhill.
KEY WORDS: Conservation tillage = Planting with little or no plowing, leaving crop leftovers on the soil.; Community-supported agriculture (CSA) = Customers pay a local farm up front for a share of its harvest.
QUICK CHECK: How does terracing reduce environmental harm? → It slows water running downhill, so less soil erodes (Steps on a hillside hold water and soil in place.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain what a food desert is and ONE reason food deserts exist in urban or rural areas of more developed countries.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A food desert is an area where people have little access to affordable, healthy food because there are few or no full-service grocery stores nearby. E2- In many low-income city neighborhoods, such as parts of Detroit and Chicago's South Side, supermarket chains closed stores and opened new ones in the suburbs, where customers had more money to spend. A3- Because grocery companies locate where they expect the highest profits, residents without cars must rely on corner stores and fast food, which leads to poorer diets.
RUBRIC: Defines a food desert: little access to affordable, healthy, or fresh food / Explains a reason: stores avoid poor or sparse areas, or residents lack cars
MINI-LESSON (Food deserts in rich countries):
 - A food desert is an area with little access to affordable, healthy food, especially fresh fruits and vegetables.
 - The USDA counts an area as low-access if many residents live over 1 mile from a supermarket (10 miles in rural areas).
 - Supermarkets follow sales, so many left low-income city neighborhoods for the suburbs, and few open in thinly populated places.
 - This is a scale issue: a country can grow plenty of food and still have local pockets of food insecurity.
KEY WORDS: Food desert = An area with little access to affordable, healthy food.; Food insecurity = Lacking reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
QUICK CHECK: Which BEST describes a food desert? → An area with little access to affordable, healthy food (It's about access to healthy food, not climate.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Green Revolution
   - The Green Revolution spread high-yield seeds, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation, and farm machines to developing countries in the mid-1900s.
   - It began in Mexico in the 1940s, where American plant scientist Norman Borlaug bred short, sturdy, high-yield wheat.
   - In the 1960s it reached India and Pakistan, and in 1966 a research institute in the Philippines released IR8, nicknamed “miracle rice.”
   - Borlaug won the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize because the extra food helped prevent famines.
• The three agricultural revolutions
   - First (Neolithic), about 10,000 years ago: people domesticated plants and animals in hearths like the Fertile Crescent and settled in villages.
   - Second, 1700s–1800s, alongside the Industrial Revolution: the seed drill, new crop rotations, and better transportation let fewer farmers feed more people, freeing workers for factories.
   - Third, from the mid-1900s: the Green Revolution's seeds, chemicals, irrigation, and machines, later joined by GMOs.
• Agribusiness & vertical integration
   - Agribusiness is farming run as a big business, from seeds and chemicals to processing, shipping, and selling.
   - Vertical integration means one company controls several steps of the same supply chain.
   - Tyson Foods, for example, supplies chicks and feed to contract farmers, then processes and sells the chicken under its own brand.
   - Green Revolution farmers came to depend on buying fertilizer and pesticides from these companies.
• GMOs
   - A GMO (genetically modified organism) has DNA changed in a lab, often by adding a gene from another species.
   - Bt corn makes its own insect-killing protein, and Golden Rice was engineered to provide vitamin A.
   - Supporters say GMOs raise yields; critics worry about corporate control of patented seeds.
   - GMO crops spread from the mid-1990s, after the original Green Revolution.
• Subsistence farming & staple crops
   - Subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families, not to sell.
   - Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn): clear and burn a rainforest plot, farm it a few years, then move on so it can recover, as in the Amazon and Central Africa.
   - Pastoral nomadism: moving herds of camels, goats, or cattle across dry lands like the Sahel and Mongolia.
   - In much of sub-Saharan Africa, small rain-fed farms grow staples like cassava, yams, sorghum, and millet, not the irrigated wheat and rice the Green Revolution was built for.
• Environmental costs of high-input farming
   - Irrigating thirsty high-yield crops can pump groundwater faster than rain refills it; in India's Punjab the water table has been falling for decades.
   - In dry places, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salt in the soil (salinization), which can ruin fields.
   - Heavy pesticide use can poison water and breed resistant pests.
   - Replacing thousands of local varieties with a few high-yield ones (monoculture) shrinks crop diversity, so one disease can wipe out more.
• Sustainable farming practices
   - Sustainable agriculture grows food today while protecting the soil, water, and wildlife farms need tomorrow.
   - Soil savers: no-till, cover crops, contour plowing, and terracing, like the centuries-old rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras.
   - Crop rotation and organic farming rebuild soil with legumes, compost, and manure instead of synthetic fertilizer.
   - Community-supported agriculture (CSA) and farmers' markets shorten the distance food travels and support small farms.
• Food deserts & food insecurity
   - Food insecurity means lacking reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food, and it happens in rich and poor countries alike.
   - A food desert is an area, often low-income, where residents live far from a supermarket and healthy food is hard to get.
   - In cities, many supermarket chains closed stores in poorer neighborhoods and moved to the suburbs, a pattern tied to disinvestment and redlining.
   - In rural areas, like the Mississippi Delta or the Navajo Nation, too few customers live nearby to support a big store, so families drive far for groceries.

--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the agricultural activity found in ring 1, the ring closest to the market city.
MODEL ANSWER: Ring 1 is dairy farming and market gardening (growing fresh vegetables) for the city.
RUBRIC: Names dairy farming or vegetable/market gardening / Describes it as perishable or intensive farming near the city
MINI-LESSON (Identify = read it off the diagram):
 - Identify questions ask you to name something, not explain it. One clear phrase earns the point.
 - On a von Thünen-style diagram, the ring touching the city holds the products that must reach the market fastest.
 - Here ring 1 is labeled dairy and vegetables. Geographers call vegetable farming for a nearby city market gardening (or truck farming).
 - Writing “milk and fresh vegetables for the city” is also fine. Specific beats vague.
KEY WORDS: Market gardening = Growing vegetables and fruit to sell fresh in a nearby city.; Dairy farming = Raising cows (or goats) for milk, cheese, and butter.
QUICK CHECK: Growing fresh vegetables to sell in a nearby city is called… → market gardening (Market gardening (truck farming) is the fresh-produce zone right outside a city.)

--- Part B (Explain) ---
Q: Explain why the agricultural products grown closest to the market are the most perishable or the most expensive to transport.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Products that spoil quickly or are heavy to haul are grown closest to the market because transport cost rises with distance from the city. E2- Milk and fresh vegetables can rot within a day or two of harvest, and timber is bulky and heavy to carry by wagon, while grain keeps for months and cattle can walk to market. A3- Because dairy farmers and market gardeners lose the most money from a long trip, they gain the most from being near the city and can afford to pay (bid) more for the expensive land there, which pushes grain and ranching farther out.
RUBRIC: Names the trait: perishable (spoils fast) or heavy/bulky products like milk, vegetables, or timber / Explains the logic: transport cost rises with distance, so these farmers gain the most from being close and can outbid others for near-city land (bid-rent)
MINI-LESSON (Bid-rent: who can pay for land near the city?):
 - Every mile a product travels costs money (fuel, wagons, drivers), so transport cost rises with distance from the market.
 - Land near the market is expensive because lots of people want it; land far away is cheap.
 - A dairy farmer whose milk spoils in a day saves the most by being close, so they can outbid a wheat farmer for near-city land.
 - A rancher's cattle can walk to market, so the rancher gains little from being close and settles for cheap distant land.
 - Those bidding wars sort farmers into rings by distance.
KEY WORDS: Transport cost = The money it takes to move a product to market.; Bid-rent = The most a land user is willing to pay for land at a given distance from the market.; Perishable = Spoils quickly.
QUICK CHECK: In von Thünen's model, which cost rises as a farm gets farther from the market? → transport cost (Farther away means a longer haul. Land cost actually falls with distance.)

--- Part C (Describe) ---
Q: Using the diagram, describe how the river changes the pattern of land use compared with the classic von Thünen model.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- In the classic model the rings are perfect circles, but on this diagram the river stretches the rings into long ovals. E2- The lighter shading shows ring 1 (dairy and vegetables) extending north and south of Aldmere along the Tarn River into the timber ring, and ring 3 (grains) extending along the river into the ranching ring. A3- This happens because moving goods by boat along the river is cheaper and faster than hauling them by wagon, so a farm that is far away in miles but on the river is “close” in transport cost and can grow the same products as farms next to the city.
RUBRIC: Describes the change with diagram evidence: rings (1 and 3) are stretched/elongated into ovals along the river instead of perfect circles / Links it to cheaper or faster transport along the river (boats/barges lower transport cost)
MINI-LESSON (Rivers and the rings):
 - Von Thünen's original model assumed a flat plain with no rivers or roads, so every direction cost the same and the rings came out as perfect circles.
 - He himself noted that a navigable river would change things: boats carry heavy loads far more cheaply than wagons.
 - Along the river, transport cost per mile is lower, so each ring stretches outward in a long oval following the water.
 - On this diagram, rings 1 and 3 are stretched along the Tarn River, so dairy farms and grain fields reach farther north and south than east and west.
KEY WORDS: Navigable river = A river deep and calm enough for boats to carry cargo.; Elongated = Stretched out into a long shape.
QUICK CHECK: Why does a navigable river stretch the von Thünen rings outward along its course? → Boats move goods more cheaply than wagons, so river farms are “closer” in cost (The model is about transport cost. Cheap boat transport lowers the cost of distance along the river.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how the secondary market town of Crossford along the highway shows how modern transportation changes the von Thünen model.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Crossford shows that modern transportation breaks the model's assumption of a single market with one set of rings. E2- On the diagram, a small dairy-and-vegetable zone surrounds Crossford where the highway crosses ring 3, forming a miniature ring 1 inside the grain zone. A3- Because trucks on the highway move goods quickly and cheaply, Crossford grew into a second market of its own, so the region now has overlapping sets of rings instead of one neat set of circles around Aldmere.
RUBRIC: Identifies what Crossford shows: a second market with its own mini dairy/vegetable zone (ring), breaking the one-market assumption / Explains the role of modern transport: highways/trucks make travel faster and cheaper, so markets and rings form in new places
MINI-LESSON (Highways, trucks, and extra markets):
 - Von Thünen's model assumes one market city, no roads, and wagons, so there is one set of rings.
 - Modern highways let trucks move goods fast and cheaply in straight lines, so places along a highway are “closer” in time than their miles suggest.
 - A town on the highway, like Crossford, can collect farm products and grow into a secondary market with its own small rings around it.
 - In the real world, cities have many suburbs and towns, each pulling the rings in its own direction, so the pattern looks more like overlapping blobs than circles.
KEY WORDS: Secondary market = A smaller town where farmers also sell, in addition to the main city.; Assumption = Something a model treats as true to keep it simple.
QUICK CHECK: Which assumption of the von Thünen model does the town of Crossford break? → There is only one market (Crossford is a second market, so the one-market assumption is broken. The other two choices are things the model assumes and this diagram still shows. The highway and river bend the rings too, but the question asks about the town.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE way refrigerated trucking and global shipping have weakened the von Thünen pattern at the national or global scale.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Refrigeration has weakened the model because perishable products no longer have to be grown next to the city that eats them. E2- Refrigerated trucks and container ships keep produce fresh for weeks, so a U.S. supermarket in January sells grapes from Chile and tomatoes from Mexico that traveled thousands of miles. A3- At the national and global scale, this means the ring 1 logic breaks down: the dairy-and-vegetable zone is not a ring around each city but far-off regions like California, Florida, or another country, so the whole world acts as one giant hinterland.
RUBRIC: Names the technology: refrigeration/cold chain, refrigerated trucks, container ships, air freight, highways / Explains the effect with scale: perishables are grown far away (e.g., Chile, Mexico, California) so the near-city rings weaken at the national or global scale
MINI-LESSON (The cold chain and scale):
 - The “cold chain” is the chain of refrigerated trucks, warehouses, and containers that keeps food cold from farm to store.
 - It lets perishables travel for weeks, so milk, meat, and vegetables can be grown thousands of miles from the city that eats them.
 - In winter, U.S. stores sell grapes from Chile and tomatoes from Mexico, and California and Florida supply fresh produce to the whole country.
 - At the local scale, you may still find dairy farms around a city (a “milkshed”), but at the national and global scale the rings mostly disappear.
 - Always say which scale you mean: local, national, or global.
KEY WORDS: Cold chain = Refrigerated transport and storage that keeps food fresh over long distances.; Scale = The level of analysis: local, national, or global.; Milkshed = The area around a city that supplies its milk.
QUICK CHECK: Which technology MOST directly lets perishable food be grown far from the city that eats it? → refrigeration (the cold chain) (Refrigerated trucks and containers keep food fresh for weeks, so distance matters far less.)

--- Part F (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE environmental consequence of intensive commercial agriculture in ring 1 or ring 3 of the diagram.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One environmental consequence of intensive farming in ring 3 is water pollution from fertilizer runoff. E2- Grain farmers spread large amounts of nitrogen fertilizer on their fields, and when it rains the extra fertilizer washes off the fields and flows into the Tarn River. A3- The nitrogen feeds huge algae blooms downstream that use up the oxygen in the water and kill fish, creating a “dead zone” like the one at the mouth of the Mississippi River in the Gulf of Mexico.
RUBRIC: Names a specific environmental consequence: fertilizer/pesticide/manure runoff, water pollution, aquifer or groundwater depletion, soil salinization, soil erosion, or loss of soil fertility / Describes how it happens or what it does to the environment (cause → result)
MINI-LESSON (What intensive commercial farming does to land and water):
 - Intensive commercial farms put lots of inputs (fertilizer, pesticides, water, machinery) on each acre to get big harvests.
 - Fertilizer runoff: rain carries nitrogen and phosphorus into rivers and lakes, feeding algae that use up oxygen and create dead zones. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone comes from farms along the Mississippi.
 - Aquifer depletion: pumping groundwater for irrigation faster than rain refills it drains aquifers like the Ogallala under the Great Plains.
 - Salinization: in dry areas, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salt in the soil, which eventually kills crops.
 - Soil erosion: bare, plowed fields lose topsoil to wind and rain, as in the 1930s Dust Bowl.
KEY WORDS: Runoff = Rainwater that flows off fields, carrying fertilizer and soil with it.; Aquifer = A layer of underground rock or sand that holds water.; Salinization = Salt building up in soil from evaporating irrigation water.
QUICK CHECK: Fertilizer washing off fields into rivers is called… → runoff (Runoff carries nitrogen and phosphorus into waterways, where it feeds algae blooms.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE limitation of using this model to understand agriculture in a less developed country today.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One limitation is that the model assumes commercial farmers who sell everything to a single market city, which does not describe many farmers in less developed countries. E2- In rural parts of countries like Ethiopia or Bolivia, many families practice subsistence agriculture, growing maize, teff, or potatoes mainly to feed themselves, often on steep hillsides or communal land rather than a flat plain with one market. A3- Because those farmers are not choosing crops based on transport cost to a central city, distance to the market does not sort their land use into rings, so the model would predict the wrong pattern and miss what actually shapes their farming, such as rainfall, terrain, and land ownership.
RUBRIC: Names an assumption that doesn't fit: subsistence (not commercial) farming, no single market / many small markets, export crops for ports, rugged terrain or varied climate, land tenure (communal land, plantations, landless farmers) / Explains why that makes the model misleading there
MINI-LESSON (When the rings don't form):
 - A model works only where its assumptions are roughly true. Von Thünen assumed commercial farmers, one market, flat land with uniform soil, and farmers who own land and chase profit.
 - In many less developed countries, millions of farmers practice subsistence agriculture: they grow food to eat, so distance to a city isn't what decides their crops.
 - Many regions have dozens of small village markets or sell export crops (coffee, cocoa, cotton) that go to a port and overseas, not to a nearby city.
 - Mountains, deserts, and uneven rainfall matter more than distance, and land tenure (who owns or controls the land, including plantations and communal land) shapes what gets grown.
 - A good limitation names the assumption that fails AND says what conclusion goes wrong because of it.
KEY WORDS: Subsistence agriculture = Growing food mainly to feed your own family, not to sell.; Land tenure = The rules about who owns or has the right to use land.; Model = A simplified picture of reality built on assumptions.
QUICK CHECK: Which assumption of the von Thünen model is LEAST likely to hold in a rural subsistence-farming region? → Farmers sell all their products to one central market (Subsistence farmers grow food for their own families, so there's no single market pulling their land use into rings.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The von Thünen model
   - Johann Heinrich von Thünen was a German landowner who published The Isolated State in 1826 to explain why farms near a city grow different things than farms far away.
   - He imagined one market city on a flat plain with the same soil and climate everywhere, no rivers or roads, and farmers hauling goods by wagon.
   - His rings, from the center out: market gardening and dairy, forest (for firewood and lumber), grains and field crops, then livestock ranching.
   - The big idea: the farther you are from the market, the more it costs to get your product there, so land use changes with distance.
   - Real places never match the rings exactly, but the logic of distance and transport cost still shows up everywhere.
• Bid-rent & transport cost
   - Bid-rent is the most a user is willing to pay for land at a given distance from the market.
   - Land near the city is expensive because many people want it; land far away is cheap.
   - A farmer whose product is perishable (milk, lettuce) or bulky (timber) saves the most money by being close, so they can outbid others for near-city land.
   - A rancher's cattle can walk or ride to market cheaply, so the rancher can't outbid anyone near the city and ends up on cheap distant land.
   - Put together, those bidding wars create the rings.
• Global food supply chains
   - A commodity chain is every step a product takes from the farm to your plate: growing, processing, shipping, selling.
   - Refrigerated trucks and containers (the “cold chain”) keep food fresh for thousands of miles, so perishables no longer have to be grown nearby.
   - Interstate highways, container ships, and air freight make long-distance shipping cheap and fast.
   - That's why a U.S. grocery store sells Chilean grapes in January and Mexican tomatoes year-round.
   - Large agribusiness companies often own several steps of the chain, which is called vertical integration.
• The Green Revolution
   - Starting in the 1940s–1960s, scientists like Norman Borlaug bred high-yield varieties of wheat and rice.
   - Those seeds only produce big harvests with lots of chemical fertilizer, pesticides, and irrigation water.
   - Mexico, India, and the Philippines sharply raised their food output, and famine deaths dropped.
   - Costs: polluted water, drained aquifers, lost crop diversity, and debt for poor farmers who couldn't afford the inputs.
   - Sub-Saharan Africa benefited least, partly because its main crops and dry climates didn't fit the new seeds.
• Farming's environmental costs
   - Fertilizer runoff carries nitrogen and phosphorus into rivers, feeding algae blooms that create oxygen-poor “dead zones,” like the one in the Gulf of Mexico fed by the Mississippi River.
   - Pumping groundwater for irrigation faster than rain refills it depletes aquifers, such as the Ogallala Aquifer under the U.S. Great Plains.
   - Irrigating in dry climates leaves salt behind as water evaporates (salinization), which can ruin soil.
   - Plowing and bare fields speed up soil erosion; the 1930s Dust Bowl is the classic example.
   - Clearing forest for cattle pasture and soybeans drives deforestation in the Amazon.
• Intensive vs. extensive, commercial vs. subsistence
   - Intensive agriculture puts lots of labor or money into a small piece of land: dairy farms, vegetable farms, rice paddies.
   - Extensive agriculture uses a lot of land with little labor per acre: cattle ranching, shifting cultivation, nomadic herding.
   - Commercial farmers grow crops to sell; subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families.
   - Most farms in wealthy countries are commercial; many farms in less developed countries are still subsistence or a mix.
   - Von Thünen's model assumes commercial farmers selling to one market, which is why it fits some places better than others.
