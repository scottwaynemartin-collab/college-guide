Unit 1 · Mapping the Storm (Practice FRQ · No stimulus)
======================================================================
STIMULUS: (no stimulus)
You're interning at a Gulf Coast county's emergency-management office before hurricane season. Your supervisor wants you to master the geographer's toolkit: maps, locations, regions, and how people and the environment shape each other.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of region that “the South” in the United States is an example of.
MODEL ANSWER: “The South” is a perceptual (vernacular) region: it exists in people's minds, so its borders are fuzzy and change depending on who you ask.
RUBRIC: Names it: a perceptual (vernacular) region / Describes it: exists in people's minds, with fuzzy borders
MINI-LESSON (The three types of regions):
 - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana.
 - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a central node, like a city's commuter zone.
 - A perceptual (vernacular) region is based on what people believe and feel, so its borders are fuzzy, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
 - Clue: if people argue about its edges and it's tied to identity, it's perceptual.
KEY WORDS: Perceptual (vernacular) region = A region based on shared identity and feelings, with fuzzy borders.; Formal region = A region with an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is a PERCEPTUAL region? → “The Midwest” (The Midwest's borders depend on who you ask. Ohio is formal; a delivery zone is functional.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe how the Mercator projection distorts the size of Greenland compared with the size of Africa.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Mercator projection stretches the area of places near the poles, so they look much bigger than they really are. E2- On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Greenland is about 2.2 million km² while Africa is about 30 million km², roughly 14 times larger. A3- Because Greenland is near the North Pole and Africa straddles the equator, people who use only Mercator maps can badly underestimate Africa's true size.
RUBRIC: Describes the distortion: Greenland looks about as big as Africa / Gives the truth or the reason: Africa is about 14 times bigger, or Mercator stretches areas near the poles
MINI-LESSON (Why Mercator makes Greenland huge):
 - Every map projection distorts shape, area, distance, or direction when it flattens the globe.
 - Gerardus Mercator made his map in 1569 for sailors: it keeps compass directions and local shapes correct.
 - To do that, it stretches places more and more toward the poles, so Greenland looks as big as Africa, which is really about 14 times larger.
 - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes; Robinson compromises, with a little distortion everywhere.
KEY WORDS: Map projection = A way of showing the curved Earth on a flat map.; Distortion = A change in shape, area, distance, or direction caused by flattening the globe.
QUICK CHECK: Which projection keeps the AREA of every country correct? → Gall-Peters (Gall-Peters is an equal-area projection. Mercator keeps shape and direction; Robinson compromises.)

--- Part C (Describe) ---
Q: Describe the difference between a city's site and its situation, using New Orleans or another real city as your example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A city's site is the physical land it sits on, while its situation is its location compared to other places. E2- New Orleans's site is low, swampy land in a bend of the Mississippi River, and about half the city is below sea level. Its situation is near the river's mouth on the Gulf of Mexico, which makes it a port linking the central United States to world trade. A3- The site explains New Orleans's flood risk, while the situation explains why people built a major city there anyway.
RUBRIC: Describes SITE: the physical features of the place itself / Describes SITUATION: location relative to other places and its connections
MINI-LESSON (Site vs. situation):
 - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
 - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: nearby rivers, roads, trade routes, and cities.
 - The French founded New Orleans in 1718 on a bend of the Mississippi surrounded by swamps, so its site is low and flood-prone.
 - Its situation is excellent: near the river's mouth, it became a port shipping goods from the middle of the country to the world.
KEY WORDS: Site = The physical features of a place's exact location.; Situation = A place's location relative to other places, and its connections to them.
QUICK CHECK: “Chicago grew because canals and railroads connected it to the rest of the country.” This describes Chicago's… → situation (Connections to other places are situation. Site would be the flat lakeshore land itself.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how a county government could use remote sensing to respond to a hurricane after it makes landfall.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A county could use remote sensing, which collects images of Earth from satellites, planes, or drones, to see storm damage without sending people into danger. E2- After a hurricane, before-and-after images show flooded neighborhoods, destroyed homes, and blocked roads; NOAA, for example, flies planes over the coast to photograph hurricane damage. A3- Knowing where the worst damage is lets the county send rescue crews, water, and generators there first, saving time and lives.
RUBRIC: Says what remote sensing collects: images from satellites, aircraft, or drones / Explains the response: finding flooding or damage, then sending rescue, supplies, or repairs there
MINI-LESSON (Remote sensing, GPS, and GIS):
 - Remote sensing collects information about Earth from a distance, usually with satellites, airplanes, or drones.
 - Before-and-after images show where floods, fires, or storms did damage, even where roads are cut off.
 - GPS is different: it pinpoints the exact location of a device, like a rescue boat.
 - GIS is software that layers map data, like damage images on top of road maps.
KEY WORDS: Remote sensing = Collecting information about Earth's surface from a distance.; GPS = A satellite system that gives a device's exact location.; GIS = Software that layers and analyzes map data.
QUICK CHECK: A rescue boat's screen shows its exact latitude and longitude. Which tool is that? → GPS (GPS pinpoints a device's exact location. Remote sensing gathers images from a distance.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason a functional region, such as a hospital's service area or a city's commuter zone, can change in size over time.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A functional region can grow when new transportation makes its node faster to reach. E2- For example, when Washington, D.C.'s Metro Silver Line was extended to Dulles Airport and Loudoun County, Virginia, in 2022, people in those outer suburbs could ride a train into the city. A3- Because a functional region is defined by connections to its node, faster connections let people come from farther away, so the commuter zone's edge moves outward and the region grows.
RUBRIC: Names a cause: new transportation, a competing node, or new technology / Explains how the edge moves: people come from farther (it grows) or switch to a closer node (it shrinks)
MINI-LESSON (Functional regions on the move):
 - A functional (nodal) region is held together by connections to a central node, like commuters going downtown.
 - Its edge is wherever those connections fade out, not a line set by law.
 - New highways or train lines let people reach the node from farther away, so the region grows.
 - A new competing node, like a new hospital in the next town, pulls people away, so the old region shrinks.
KEY WORDS: Node = The central point a functional region is organized around.; Functional (nodal) region = An area tied together by connections to a node.
QUICK CHECK: What holds a functional region together? → Connections to a central node (Official borders mean formal; shared feelings mean perceptual.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE difference between environmental determinism and possibilism, using a real-world example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Environmental determinism says the environment controls how people live and develop, while possibilism says it only sets limits and people choose how to respond. E2- For example, about a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, but the Dutch built dikes, pumps, and polders (land drained from the sea) to farm and live there. A3- A determinist would expect such flood-prone land to hold a society back, but the Netherlands became one of the world's wealthiest countries, which supports possibilism.
RUBRIC: Describes determinism: the environment controls what people and societies can do / Describes possibilism with an example: people choose how to adapt, often with technology
MINI-LESSON (Who's in charge: nature or people?):
 - Environmental determinism claims climate and landforms control people's behavior and development.
 - Geographers rejected it by the mid-1900s: it ignored culture and choice and was used to justify colonialism.
 - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to use it, like the Dutch farming land below sea level.
 - Sustainability adds a warning: changing nature has costs, so meet today's needs without ruining the environment for the future.
KEY WORDS: Environmental determinism = The idea that the environment controls human behavior and development.; Possibilism = The idea that nature sets limits, but people choose how to adapt.; Sustainability = Meeting today's needs without harming future generations.
QUICK CHECK: “Hot climates make people lazy, so tropical countries can't develop.” This claim is an example of… → environmental determinism (Claiming climate controls behavior is determinism, which geographers reject.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE limitation of using only quantitative data, such as census numbers, to understand a place after a hurricane, and how qualitative data could help.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One limitation of quantitative data is that numbers show how many people were affected, but not why they made their choices. E2- For example, the 2010 census counted about 140,000 fewer people in New Orleans than in 2000, after Hurricane Katrina, but it can't show why some families returned and others never did. A3- Qualitative data, like interviews with residents and field observation, could reveal reasons such as rebuilding costs or ties to family and church, helping leaders plan a better recovery.
RUBRIC: Names a limitation: numbers don't show people's reasons, feelings, or experiences / Explains what qualitative data adds: interviews, field observation, or stories
MINI-LESSON (Two kinds of data):
 - Quantitative data are numbers: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
 - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and oral histories.
 - Field observation means going to a place in person to look, listen, and take notes or photos.
 - Numbers show the pattern; qualitative data explain the reasons and human experience behind it.
KEY WORDS: Quantitative data = Information in numbers that can be counted or measured.; Qualitative data = Descriptive information, like interviews, observations, and stories.; Field observation = Studying a place in person.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is QUALITATIVE data? → An interview with a family about why they rebuilt (Interviews describe experiences and reasons. Counts and averages are quantitative.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Map projections & distortion
   - Every map projection, a way of drawing the round Earth on flat paper, distorts shape, area, distance, or direction.
   - Mercator (1569) keeps shapes and compass directions true for sailors, but stretches areas more and more toward the poles.
   - On a Mercator map, Greenland (about 2.2 million km²) looks about as big as Africa (about 30 million km²), though Africa is about 14 times larger.
   - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes; Robinson compromises, distorting everything a little and nothing a lot.
• Formal, functional & perceptual regions
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana or the Corn Belt.
   - A functional (nodal) region is tied together by connections to a central node, like a TV station's viewing area or a city's commuter zone.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
   - New Orleans is in all three: Louisiana (formal), its own commuter zone (functional), and “the South” (perceptual).
• Location, site & situation
   - Absolute location is a place's exact spot, given by latitude and longitude: New Orleans is at about 30°N, 90°W.
   - Relative location describes a place compared to other places: New Orleans is near the mouth of the Mississippi River.
   - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
   - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: its connections to rivers, roads, trade routes, and other cities.
• Remote sensing vs. GPS vs. GIS
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites, airplanes, or drones.
   - Landsat satellites have photographed Earth since 1972, so before-and-after images can reveal floods, burned forests, or growing cities.
   - GPS uses satellite signals to find the exact location of a device. GIS is software that layers and analyzes map data.
   - Remote sensing takes the picture, GPS finds the spot, and GIS puts it all together.
• Determinism, possibilism & sustainability
   - Environmental determinism is the old, rejected idea that the environment, especially climate, controls how people behave and develop.
   - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to respond through culture and technology.
   - It goes both ways: levees protect New Orleans but stop the Mississippi from spreading new mud on its delta, one reason coastal Louisiana has lost about 2,000 square miles of land since the 1930s.
   - Sustainability means meeting today's needs without ruining the environment for future generations, like restoring coastal wetlands that soften storm surges.
• Quantitative vs. qualitative data
   - Quantitative data are numbers you can count or measure: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
   - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and people's stories.
   - Field observation means visiting a place in person to look, listen, and record what you find.
   - Numbers show how much and where; qualitative data help explain why.

--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of thematic map used in the county-scale panel of the stimulus.
MODEL ANSWER: It is a choropleth map: each county is shaded one color based on which percent class it falls in.
RUBRIC: Names it: a choropleth map / Describes the method: areas shaded or colored by data class
MINI-LESSON (Spotting a choropleth):
 - A choropleth map fills in whole areas (countries, states, counties) with a color based on their data value.
 - The values are grouped into classes, and the legend tells you which color means which range.
 - It's great for rates and percents, like percent without internet, because it compares places fairly regardless of their size.
 - Its weakness: a whole county gets one color, even if conditions vary a lot inside it.
KEY WORDS: Thematic map = A map about one topic, like income or internet access.; Choropleth map = Areas shaded by data class.; Class = A range of values that share one color on the legend.
QUICK CHECK: Which map type shades whole areas by data class? → Choropleth (Choropleth maps color areas by value class. Dot density uses dots; graduated symbols use sized circles.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Using the map, describe how the pattern of households without home internet changes between the state scale and the county scale.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- At the state scale there is no pattern to see, because the whole state is one shade with a single value of 18%. E2- At the county scale the same data range from 6% in Calder City and 9% in Westbrook, clustered around the capital, up to 41% in Northfork and 44% in Dry Creek along the western and northern edges. A3- Zooming in reveals a clustered pattern of rural counties where more than a third of households lack internet, which the single state number completely hides.
RUBRIC: Describes the state scale as one value or one shade (18%) for the whole state / Describes the county-scale pattern with evidence: a wide range (6% to 44%), low near the capital or metro, high in rural or edge counties
MINI-LESSON (Reading a pattern at two scales):
 - Scale of analysis is the size of the units the data are divided into. Here: 1 state vs. 12 counties.
 - At the bigger unit, everything is averaged into one number, so you can't see a pattern at all.
 - At the smaller unit, you can describe the pattern: clustered (grouped together) or dispersed (spread out), and which places are high or low.
 - A good description names the range (lowest to highest), where the highs and lows sit, and what word fits the arrangement, like 'clustered around the capital.'
KEY WORDS: Scale of analysis = The size of the units you study: national, state, county, local.; Clustered = Grouped close together.; Dispersed = Spread out evenly.
QUICK CHECK: A pattern that looks uniform at the state scale but clustered at the county scale is an example of… → how scale of analysis changes what you see (Changing the size of the units (the scale of analysis) can reveal or hide patterns.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason rural counties in Calderon have higher rates of households without home internet than the counties around the capital.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Rural counties have higher rates of households without internet because their low population density makes internet infrastructure very expensive per customer. E2- In Dry Creek (44%) homes are spread miles apart, so a company must lay far more fiber-optic cable to reach each household than it does in dense Calder City (6%), where one line serves hundreds of homes. A3- Because the company earns less for every mile of cable in a rural county, it builds there last or not at all, so many rural households are left without service and the rate stays high.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: low population density / homes spread far apart, high cost of infrastructure per customer, distance from the city, or lower incomes / Explains the cause-and-effect chain to the higher rate
MINI-LESSON (Density, distance, and the cost of a connection):
 - Population density is the number of people per unit of land area, for example per square mile. Rural counties have low density: homes sit far apart.
 - Internet service needs physical infrastructure, like fiber-optic cable or cell towers, and it costs money for every mile built.
 - In a dense city, one mile of cable can serve hundreds of paying customers. In a rural county it might serve five, so the company earns far less per mile.
 - Companies build where they make money first, so rural areas get service later, slower, or never. Distance decay plays a role too: places far from the metro interact with it less and get new services later.
 - Lower rural incomes can add to it: even where service exists, fewer families can afford it.
KEY WORDS: Population density = People per unit of land.; Infrastructure = The physical systems a place needs, like roads, pipes, and cables.; Distance decay = Interaction weakens as distance grows.
QUICK CHECK: Why does one mile of internet cable cost MORE per customer in a rural county? → Fewer homes are spread along that mile (Low population density means each mile of cable reaches fewer paying customers.)

Q: Using the map, explain ONE limitation of using only the state-level data to make decisions about improving internet access in Calderon.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One limitation is that a single state-level number is an average that hides the differences inside the state, so planners can't see where the problem actually is. E2- The map shows Calderon at 18%, but at the county scale Dry Creek is 44% and Northfork is 41% while Calder City is only 6%, and eight of the 12 counties are above the state figure. MINI-LESSON (How averages hide the extremes):
 - A state-level figure is a population-weighted average: counties with more people count more, so the crowded metro counties pull Calderon down to 18%.
 - Eight of the 12 counties are above 18%, and two are above 40%. The average doesn't describe any real county well.
 - If leaders only see 18%, they may think the problem is mild or spread money evenly, when it's concentrated in rural counties.

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how a state government agency could use a geographic information system (GIS) to decide where to build new broadband internet infrastructure.

E2- For example, it could overlay this county map of households without internet with layers showing existing fiber-optic lines, population density, household income, and the locations of schools and clinics.  - The agency could stack the 'percent without internet' layer with 'existing fiber lines' and 'population density' to find counties that are both unconnected and reachable.

--- Part F (Describe) ---
Q: Describe ONE way a dot density map or a graduated symbol map of the same data would show the pattern differently from the county choropleth.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A dot density map would show the number of households without internet rather than the percent in each county. E2- If each dot stood for 500 households and the dots were placed where people live, Calder City might have many dots even though its rate is only 6%, while Dry Creek at 44% might have only a few dots because so few people live there. A3- This matters because it shows where the largest total number of unconnected households is, which can be a different place from where the rate is highest, and the dots can hint at clustering inside a county instead of coloring the whole county one shade.
RUBRIC: Names the map type: dot density or graduated/proportional symbol / Describes a real difference: it shows counts (number of households) instead of a rate, so metro counties could look bigger; or dots show where people cluster inside a county
MINI-LESSON (Rate vs. count: why the map type matters):
 - A choropleth map is best for rates and percents, because it compares places fairly no matter how big or crowded they are.
 - A dot density map places one dot per set number of things (say, 500 households). Dots pile up where there are more people and can show clustering inside a county.
 - A graduated (proportional) symbol map puts a circle on each county and sizes it by the number, so the biggest circle goes to the county with the most unconnected households.
 - On the Calderon map, Calder City has the lowest percent (6%) but the most people, so it could have lots of dots or a large circle, while rural Dry Creek (44%) might look small.
 - Always ask: does this map show how many, or what share?
KEY WORDS: Dot density map = One dot for each set number of things, placed where they are.; Graduated symbol map = Symbols sized by the data value.; Rate = A share or percent, not a raw count.
QUICK CHECK: Which map type is usually used to show COUNTS placed where things actually are? → Dot density (Dot density maps put one dot per set number of things. Choropleths shade areas by rate.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how improved internet access in Calderon's rural counties is an example of time-space compression, and explain ONE effect it could have on a rural community.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Better internet is an example of time-space compression because it shrinks the time it takes to connect a rural place with distant ones, so distance matters less. E2- A family in Dry Creek that once drove two hours to see a specialist in Calder City could have a video visit in minutes, and a student there could take an online course from a university anywhere. A3- One effect is that rural residents can reach jobs, health care, and education without leaving, which could help young people stay in the community and let local businesses sell to customers far away, slowing rural population loss.
RUBRIC: Connects to time-space compression: distance matters less because places are connected faster or instantly / Explains one effect on the rural community (remote jobs, telehealth, online school, online businesses, people staying rather than leaving)
MINI-LESSON (Time-space compression, explained with Dry Creek):
 - Time-space compression is the idea that new transportation and communication technology cuts the time it takes to interact across distance, so the world feels smaller.
 - It doesn't change the miles. It changes relative distance: how connected places feel.
 - Without broadband, a Dry Creek family is far from everything: doctors, colleges, jobs, and customers. With it, those things are a click away.
 - Possible effects: remote work and online classes, telehealth, local businesses selling nationwide, and young people staying instead of leaving.
 - It can cut both ways: local stores may lose shoppers to big online companies.
KEY WORDS: Time-space compression = Technology shrinking the time it takes to connect distant places.; Relative distance = How far apart places feel based on time, cost, or connection, not miles.; Telehealth = Seeing a doctor by video or phone.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Types of thematic maps
   - A thematic map shows one topic (a theme), like income or internet access, instead of just roads and borders.
   - A choropleth map shades whole areas, like counties or countries, by class: darker or hotter colors usually mean higher values. It works best with rates and percents.
   - A dot density map places one dot for every set number of things (say, 500 households), so you see counts and where they cluster inside an area.
   - A graduated (proportional) symbol map puts a circle on each place and makes the circle bigger when the number is bigger.
   - An isoline map connects points of equal value (like elevation or temperature), and a cartogram stretches or shrinks places by a data value instead of land area.
• Why patterns change with scale
   - Scale of analysis is the size of the units you look at: one country, 50 states, 3,000 counties, or individual neighborhoods.
   - When data for many places are rolled into one bigger unit, the values get averaged, and places with lots of people pull the average toward their number.
   - That is why a state can look 'fine' at 18% while some of its counties are over 40%: the crowded metro counties with low rates outweigh the thinly populated rural ones.
   - Zooming in to a smaller unit reveals clusters, gaps, and outliers that the bigger unit hides. Zooming out smooths them away.
   - On the AP exam, always ask: at what scale is this data, and what could be hiding inside each unit?
• Distance decay & population density
   - Distance decay is the idea that interaction between places drops off as the distance between them grows. Farther away usually means fewer connections and fewer services.
   - Population density is the number of people per unit of land. Rural counties have low density: homes are spread far apart.
   - Networks like roads, water pipes, and fiber-optic internet cost money per mile. In a dense city one mile of cable can serve hundreds of homes; in a rural county it may serve five.
   - Because companies earn less per mile in low-density places, they build there last or not at all, which leaves rural areas with weaker service.
   - Lower average incomes in many rural areas can add to the problem, since fewer households can afford a monthly plan even where service exists.
• GIS, GPS & remote sensing
   - GIS (geographic information system) is computer software that stores map data in layers, like clear sheets stacked on a light table, so you can combine and analyze them.
   - A city might stack layers for roads, population density, income, flood zones, and existing utilities, then ask the software to find spots where several conditions overlap.
   - GPS (global positioning system) uses satellites to give the exact location of a device. It collects points; GIS analyzes them.
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites or aircraft, like images of crops, forests, or city growth.
   - Governments use these tools to plan services and respond to disasters; businesses use them to choose store locations and plan delivery routes.
• Time-space compression
   - Time-space compression is the idea that new transportation and communication technology shrinks the time it takes to connect distant places, so the world feels smaller.
   - The telegraph, jet planes, and the internet each made far-away places easier and faster to reach.
   - It does not shrink actual distance; it reduces the 'friction' of distance. Geographers call this a change in relative distance between places.
   - Places with weak connections, like rural areas without broadband, are left out of this shrinking world, which is a form of spatial inequality.
   - Better connections can let people in remote places reach jobs, doctors, and schools without moving, but they can also pull local shoppers toward big online companies.
• Three types of regions
   - A region is an area that shares one or more features. Geographers sort regions into three kinds.
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or a shared measurable trait everywhere inside it, like a county, a state, or the French-speaking part of Canada.
   - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a center, or node, like a metro area tied to its downtown, a pizza delivery zone, or the area a radio station reaches.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like 'the South' or 'upstate.'
   - On the Calderon map, each county is a formal region, while the capital and the suburbs it serves form a functional region.
