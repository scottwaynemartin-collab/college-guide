Unit 1 · Mapping the Storm (Practice FRQ · No stimulus)

STIMULUS: undefined
You're interning at a Gulf Coast county's emergency-management office before hurricane season. Your supervisor wants you to master the geographer's toolkit: maps, locations, regions, and how people and the environment shape each other.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify the type of region that “the South” in the United States is an example of.
MODEL ANSWER: “The South” is a perceptual (vernacular) region: it exists in people's minds, so its borders are fuzzy and change depending on who you ask.
RUBRIC: Names it: a perceptual (vernacular) region / Describes it: exists in people's minds, with fuzzy borders
MINI-LESSON (The three types of regions):
 - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana.
 - A functional (nodal) region is organized around a central node, like a city's commuter zone.
 - A perceptual (vernacular) region is based on what people believe and feel, so its borders are fuzzy, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
 - Clue: if people argue about its edges and it's tied to identity, it's perceptual.
KEY WORDS: Perceptual (vernacular) region = A region based on shared identity and feelings, with fuzzy borders.; Formal region = A region with an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is a PERCEPTUAL region? → “The Midwest” (The Midwest's borders depend on who you ask. Ohio is formal; a delivery zone is functional.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe how the Mercator projection distorts the size of Greenland compared with the size of Africa.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- The Mercator projection stretches the area of places near the poles, so they look much bigger than they really are. E2- On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Greenland is about 2.2 million km² while Africa is about 30 million km², roughly 14 times larger. A3- Because Greenland is near the North Pole and Africa straddles the equator, people who use only Mercator maps can badly underestimate Africa's true size.
RUBRIC: Describes the distortion: Greenland looks about as big as Africa / Gives the truth or the reason: Africa is about 14 times bigger, or Mercator stretches areas near the poles
MINI-LESSON (Why Mercator makes Greenland huge):
 - Every map projection distorts shape, area, distance, or direction when it flattens the globe.
 - Gerardus Mercator made his map in 1569 for sailors: it keeps compass directions and local shapes correct.
 - To do that, it stretches places more and more toward the poles, so Greenland looks as big as Africa, which is really about 14 times larger.
 - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes; Robinson compromises, with a little distortion everywhere.
KEY WORDS: Map projection = A way of showing the curved Earth on a flat map.; Distortion = A change in shape, area, distance, or direction caused by flattening the globe.
QUICK CHECK: Which projection keeps the AREA of every country correct? → Gall-Peters (Gall-Peters is an equal-area projection. Mercator keeps shape and direction; Robinson compromises.)

--- Part C (Describe) ---
Q: Describe the difference between a city's site and its situation, using New Orleans or another real city as your example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A city's site is the physical land it sits on, while its situation is its location compared to other places. E2- New Orleans's site is low, swampy land in a bend of the Mississippi River, and about half the city is below sea level. Its situation is near the river's mouth on the Gulf of Mexico, which makes it a port linking the central United States to world trade. A3- The site explains New Orleans's flood risk, while the situation explains why people built a major city there anyway.
RUBRIC: Describes SITE: the physical features of the place itself / Describes SITUATION: location relative to other places and its connections
MINI-LESSON (Site vs. situation):
 - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
 - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: nearby rivers, roads, trade routes, and cities.
 - The French founded New Orleans in 1718 on a bend of the Mississippi surrounded by swamps, so its site is low and flood-prone.
 - Its situation is excellent: near the river's mouth, it became a port shipping goods from the middle of the country to the world.
KEY WORDS: Site = The physical features of a place's exact location.; Situation = A place's location relative to other places, and its connections to them.
QUICK CHECK: “Chicago grew because canals and railroads connected it to the rest of the country.” This describes Chicago's… → situation (Connections to other places are situation. Site would be the flat lakeshore land itself.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how a county government could use remote sensing to respond to a hurricane after it makes landfall.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A county could use remote sensing, which collects images of Earth from satellites, planes, or drones, to see storm damage without sending people into danger. E2- After a hurricane, before-and-after images show flooded neighborhoods, destroyed homes, and blocked roads; NOAA, for example, flies planes over the coast to photograph hurricane damage. A3- Knowing where the worst damage is lets the county send rescue crews, water, and generators there first, saving time and lives.
RUBRIC: Says what remote sensing collects: images from satellites, aircraft, or drones / Explains the response: finding flooding or damage, then sending rescue, supplies, or repairs there
MINI-LESSON (Remote sensing, GPS, and GIS):
 - Remote sensing collects information about Earth from a distance, usually with satellites, airplanes, or drones.
 - Before-and-after images show where floods, fires, or storms did damage, even where roads are cut off.
 - GPS is different: it pinpoints the exact location of a device, like a rescue boat.
 - GIS is software that layers map data, like damage images on top of road maps.
KEY WORDS: Remote sensing = Collecting information about Earth's surface from a distance.; GPS = A satellite system that gives a device's exact location.; GIS = Software that layers and analyzes map data.
QUICK CHECK: A rescue boat's screen shows its exact latitude and longitude. Which tool is that? → GPS (GPS pinpoints a device's exact location. Remote sensing gathers images from a distance.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE reason a functional region, such as a hospital's service area or a city's commuter zone, can change in size over time.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A functional region can grow when new transportation makes its node faster to reach. E2- For example, when Washington, D.C.'s Metro Silver Line was extended to Dulles Airport and Loudoun County, Virginia, in 2022, people in those outer suburbs could ride a train into the city. A3- Because a functional region is defined by connections to its node, faster connections let people come from farther away, so the commuter zone's edge moves outward and the region grows.
RUBRIC: Names a cause: new transportation, a competing node, or new technology / Explains how the edge moves: people come from farther (it grows) or switch to a closer node (it shrinks)
MINI-LESSON (Functional regions on the move):
 - A functional (nodal) region is held together by connections to a central node, like commuters going downtown.
 - Its edge is wherever those connections fade out, not a line set by law.
 - New highways or train lines let people reach the node from farther away, so the region grows.
 - A new competing node, like a new hospital in the next town, pulls people away, so the old region shrinks.
KEY WORDS: Node = The central point a functional region is organized around.; Functional (nodal) region = An area tied together by connections to a node.
QUICK CHECK: What holds a functional region together? → Connections to a central node (Official borders mean formal; shared feelings mean perceptual.)

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE difference between environmental determinism and possibilism, using a real-world example.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Environmental determinism says the environment controls how people live and develop, while possibilism says it only sets limits and people choose how to respond. E2- For example, about a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, but the Dutch built dikes, pumps, and polders (land drained from the sea) to farm and live there. A3- A determinist would expect such flood-prone land to hold a society back, but the Netherlands became one of the world's wealthiest countries, which supports possibilism.
RUBRIC: Describes determinism: the environment controls or limits what people and societies can do / Describes possibilism with an example: people choose how to adapt, often with technology
MINI-LESSON (Who's in charge: nature or people?):
 - Environmental determinism claims climate and landforms control people's behavior and development.
 - Geographers rejected it by the mid-1900s: it ignored culture and choice and was used to justify colonialism.
 - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to use it, like the Dutch farming land below sea level.
 - Sustainability adds a warning: changing nature has costs, so meet today's needs without ruining the environment for the future.
KEY WORDS: Environmental determinism = The idea that the environment controls human behavior and development.; Possibilism = The idea that nature sets limits, but people choose how to adapt.; Sustainability = Meeting today's needs without harming future generations.
QUICK CHECK: “Hot climates make people lazy, so tropical countries can't develop.” This claim is an example of… → environmental determinism (Claiming climate controls behavior is determinism, which geographers reject.)

--- Part G (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE limitation of using only quantitative data, such as census numbers, to understand a place after a hurricane, and how qualitative data could help.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One limitation of quantitative data is that numbers show how many people were affected, but not why they made their choices. E2- For example, the 2010 census counted about 140,000 fewer people in New Orleans than in 2000, after Hurricane Katrina, but it can't show why some families returned and others never did. A3- Qualitative data, like interviews with residents and field observation, could reveal reasons such as rebuilding costs or ties to family and church, helping leaders plan a better recovery.
RUBRIC: Names a limitation: numbers don't show people's reasons, feelings, or experiences / Explains what qualitative data adds: interviews, field observation, or stories
MINI-LESSON (Two kinds of data):
 - Quantitative data are numbers: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
 - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and oral histories.
 - Field observation means going to a place in person to look, listen, and take notes or photos.
 - Numbers show the pattern; qualitative data explain the reasons and human experience behind it.
KEY WORDS: Quantitative data = Information in numbers that can be counted or measured.; Qualitative data = Descriptive information, like interviews, observations, and stories.; Field observation = Studying a place in person.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is QUALITATIVE data? → An interview with a family about why they rebuilt (Interviews describe experiences and reasons. Counts and averages are quantitative.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Map projections & distortion
   - Every map projection, a way of drawing the round Earth on flat paper, distorts shape, area, distance, or direction.
   - Mercator (1569) keeps shapes and compass directions true for sailors, but stretches areas more and more toward the poles.
   - On a Mercator map, Greenland (about 2.2 million km²) looks about as big as Africa (about 30 million km²), though Africa is about 14 times larger.
   - Gall-Peters keeps area correct but stretches shapes; Robinson compromises, distorting everything a little and nothing a lot.
• Formal, functional & perceptual regions
   - A formal (uniform) region has an official boundary or one shared, measurable trait throughout, like the state of Louisiana or the Corn Belt.
   - A functional (nodal) region is tied together by connections to a central node, like a TV station's viewing area or a city's commuter zone.
   - A perceptual (vernacular) region exists in people's minds and has fuzzy borders, like “the South” or “the Midwest.”
   - New Orleans is in all three: Louisiana (formal), its own commuter zone (functional), and “the South” (perceptual).
• Location, site & situation
   - Absolute location is a place's exact spot, given by latitude and longitude: New Orleans is at about 30°N, 90°W.
   - Relative location describes a place compared to other places: New Orleans is near the mouth of the Mississippi River.
   - Site is the physical character of the place itself: landforms, water, soil, elevation, and climate.
   - Situation is the place's location relative to other places: its connections to rivers, roads, trade routes, and other cities.
• Remote sensing vs. GPS vs. GIS
   - Remote sensing collects information about Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites, airplanes, or drones.
   - Landsat satellites have photographed Earth since 1972, so before-and-after images can reveal floods, burned forests, or growing cities.
   - GPS uses satellite signals to find the exact location of a device. GIS is software that layers and analyzes map data.
   - Remote sensing takes the picture, GPS finds the spot, and GIS puts it all together.
• Determinism, possibilism & sustainability
   - Environmental determinism is the old, rejected idea that the environment, especially climate, controls how people behave and develop.
   - Possibilism says the environment sets limits, but people choose how to respond through culture and technology.
   - It goes both ways: levees protect New Orleans but stop the Mississippi from spreading new mud on its delta, one reason coastal Louisiana has lost about 2,000 square miles of land since the 1930s.
   - Sustainability means meeting today's needs without ruining the environment for future generations, like restoring coastal wetlands that soften storm surges.
• Quantitative vs. qualitative data
   - Quantitative data are numbers you can count or measure: population, income, rainfall, or the number of damaged homes.
   - Qualitative data are descriptions: interviews, field notes, photos, and people's stories.
   - Field observation means visiting a place in person to look, listen, and record what you find.
   - Numbers show how much and where; qualitative data help explain why.

--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify ONE environmental push factor described in Stimulus 2.
MODEL ANSWER: A drought that lasted three years and killed the maize on Marisol's family farm.
RUBRIC: Drought / lack of rain / Crop failure on the family farm
MINI-LESSON (Push and pull factors):
 - A push factor makes people want to leave a place. A pull factor attracts them to a new one.
 - They can be economic, environmental, political, or social.
 - Environmental pushes include drought, floods, hurricanes, and worn-out soil.
 - On a farm, drought is also an economic push: no rain, no harvest, no income.
KEY WORDS: Push factor = A reason people leave a place.; Pull factor = A reason people are drawn to a new place.; Drought = A long period with much less rain than normal.
QUICK CHECK: Which of these is an ENVIRONMENTAL push factor? → A long drought that ruins crops (Drought comes from nature. Higher wages is an economic pull; jailing critics is a political push.)

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Using Stimulus 1, describe the pattern of migration from Tavira and how it reflects the role of distance in migration.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Most migrants from Tavira moved a short distance to a neighboring country, and far fewer made the long trip overseas. E2- In 2023, 410,000 people went next door to Montaro, while only 150,000 crossed the ocean to Eldmark, about 6,000 km away, even though both are high-income countries. A3- This fits Ravenstein's law that most migrants move short distances, an example of distance decay: long moves cost more and feel riskier, so fewer people make them.
RUBRIC: Describes the pattern: most went a short distance to neighboring Montaro / Uses map evidence: numbers or arrow widths (410,000 vs. 150,000) / Connects to distance: distance decay or Ravenstein
MINI-LESSON (Ravenstein and distance decay):
 - In the 1880s, geographer E. G. Ravenstein wrote “laws of migration.” The first: most migrants move only a short distance.
 - That's distance decay: the farther apart two places are, the less they interact.
 - Long moves cost more, are riskier, and take people farther from family.
 - Distance isn't everything: Pelora is next door too but got only 40,000, because Montaro's higher wages pull harder.
KEY WORDS: Distance decay = The farther away a place is, the less interaction (trade, travel, migration) it has with you.; Ravenstein's laws = 1880s rules about migration, like “most migrants move short distances.”
QUICK CHECK: “The farther away a place is, the fewer people move there” describes… → distance decay (Interaction drops as distance grows. Ravenstein saw it in migration.)

--- Part C (Explain) ---
Q: Using Stimulus 2, explain how Marisol's move is an example of chain migration.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Marisol's move is chain migration because she followed a family member who had already settled in Montaro. E2- Her cousin Elena had lived there for six years and found Marisol a job at the fruit-packing plant where she works. A3- Because a relative and a job were waiting, the move was less risky and Montaro was the obvious choice, and now Marisol can help the next relative follow, so the chain keeps growing.
RUBRIC: Shows what chain migration is: following family or people from home who already moved / Uses the passage: cousin Elena already lived there and found her a job / Explains how the link helped cause the move
MINI-LESSON (Chain migration):
 - Chain migration happens when people move somewhere because relatives or people from their hometown already live there.
 - Earlier migrants share information, help pay for the trip, and help newcomers find jobs and housing.
 - That makes the move cheaper and less risky, so new migrants pick the same destination.
 - Over time, this can build ethnic enclaves in the destination.
KEY WORDS: Chain migration = Migration that follows family or friends who moved earlier.; Ethnic enclave = A neighborhood where many people share the same ethnic background.
QUICK CHECK: Which is the best example of chain migration? → A man moves to a city where his brother already works (Chain migration follows family or friends who already moved there.)

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how ONE intervening obstacle described in Stimulus 2 affects migration from Tavira to Montaro.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One intervening obstacle is the Brava River, a physical barrier between Tavira and Montaro. E2- Marisol says her group crossed it at night, and people drown there every year. A3- Because the crossing is so dangerous, the river discourages some Tavirans from leaving at all and makes the trip deadly for others, so it slows and reduces migration even though Montaro is right next door.
RUBRIC: Names an obstacle from the passage: the Brava River or the border wall / Explains its effect (dangerous, costly, fewer people go)
MINI-LESSON (Intervening obstacles):
 - An intervening obstacle is a barrier between a migrant's home and destination that makes the move harder.
 - They can be physical (rivers, deserts, oceans), political (walls, visas, laws), or economic (the trip's cost).
 - Obstacles can stop some migrants, push others onto riskier routes, and even cost lives.
 - The opposite is an intervening opportunity: a job or safe place found along the way that makes a migrant stop early.
KEY WORDS: Intervening obstacle = A barrier between origin and destination that makes migration harder.; Intervening opportunity = A chance found along the way that convinces a migrant to stop there instead.
QUICK CHECK: A desert that migrants must cross to reach a new country is… → an intervening obstacle (It sits between home and the destination and makes the trip harder.)

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Push and pull factors
   - Push factors make people want to leave a place. Pull factors attract them to a new one.
   - They come in four main kinds: economic (jobs, wages), environmental (drought, floods), political (war, persecution), and social or cultural (family, religion, discrimination).
   - Economic factors are the most common reason people migrate.
   - Most moves combine both: a push at home AND a pull somewhere else.
• Ravenstein's laws of migration
   - In the 1880s, geographer E. G. Ravenstein studied British census records and wrote “laws of migration.”
   - Most migrants move only a short distance, so the number of migrants drops as distance grows: distance decay.
   - Long-distance migrants usually head for big cities and job centers.
   - Many people move in steps (step migration), like village → town → city.
   - Every migration flow produces a counterflow of people returning home.
• Chain migration
   - Chain migration happens when people move somewhere because relatives or neighbors from home already live there.
   - Earlier migrants send back news and money and help newcomers find jobs and housing.
   - Each newcomer can help the next one, so the “chain” keeps growing.
   - Over time this can build ethnic enclaves, like Little Havana in Miami.
• Intervening obstacles & opportunities
   - An intervening obstacle is a barrier between a migrant's origin and destination that makes the move harder.
   - It can be physical (rivers, oceans, deserts, mountains), political (border walls, visas, immigration limits), or economic (the cost of the trip).
   - As transportation improves, political obstacles like visas and walls matter more than physical ones.
   - An intervening opportunity is the opposite: a job or safe place found along the way that convinces a migrant to stop there instead.
• Remittances
   - Remittances are money that migrants send back to family in their home country.
   - Migrants send more than $600 billion a year to low- and middle-income countries, more than those countries receive in foreign aid.
   - In Nepal, Honduras, and Tajikistan, remittances equal more than a fifth of GDP.
   - Families spend the money on food, school, health care, and housing, which boosts local businesses.
   - Migrants who keep close ties to home like this are called transnational migrants.
• Voluntary vs. forced migration
   - Voluntary migration is a choice. Forced migration is caused by violence, persecution, disaster, or slavery.
   - A refugee has crossed an international border to escape persecution for their race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or social group.
   - An asylum seeker has asked another country for protection and is still waiting for a decision.
   - An internally displaced person (IDP) was forced to flee but stayed inside their own country.
   - A slow drought, like Tavira's, blurs the line between the two.
• Migration, age, and sex
   - Most migrants are young adults, roughly ages 20 to 35.
   - When many leave, the origin's population pyramid gets “pinched” in the young-adult bars, leaving children and older people behind.
   - That raises the dependency ratio and can lower the birth rate.
   - Destinations gain young workers: in Qatar and the UAE, so many male guest workers have arrived that men far outnumber women.
   - When skilled people like doctors and engineers leave, the origin suffers brain drain.

--- Part A (Identify) ---

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Gerrymandering: packing & cracking
   - Gerrymandering is drawing district lines on purpose to help one party or group win more seats.
   - It's named for Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry, who in 1812 signed a map with a salamander-shaped district.
   - Packing crowds the other side's voters into a few districts, which they win by huge margins.
   - Cracking splits the other side's voters across many districts so they're a minority in each. Both tricks waste the other side's votes.
• Census, reapportionment & redistricting
   - The U.S. Constitution requires a census, a count of every person, every ten years.
   - After each census, the House's 435 seats are reapportioned among the states by population. After 2020, Texas gained 2 seats and New York lost 1.
   - Each state then redistricts: it redraws its lines so every district has about the same population (“one person, one vote”).
   - Some states, like Texas, have also redrawn maps mid-decade.
• Voting Rights Act & majority-minority districts
   - The Voting Rights Act of 1965 outlawed racial discrimination in voting, such as literacy tests used to keep Black Southerners from voting.
   - It also bans maps that dilute minority voting power, for example by cracking a minority community.
   - Starting in the 1980s, courts used it to require some majority-minority districts, where a minority group makes up most of the voters.
   - Courts have also ruled that race can't be the main factor in drawing lines, so these cases are still argued.
• Commissions, compactness & contiguity
   - In most states, the legislature draws congressional lines, so the party in control can help itself.
   - An independent redistricting commission is a separate panel that draws the lines instead, as in Arizona, Colorado, and Michigan.
   - Compactness means a tight, simple shape. Contiguity means every part of a district touches the rest.
• States: federal vs. unitary
   - In AP Human Geography, a state is an independent country with sovereignty. U.S. states like Westmoor are units inside one country.
   - A nation is a people with a shared culture. A nation-state is mostly one nation (Japan), a multinational state holds several (the United Kingdom), and a stateless nation (the Kurds) has no state.
   - A federal state splits power between national and regional governments (the U.S., Germany, India); a unitary state keeps it national (France, China).
   - Because the U.S. is federal, each state runs its own redistricting, so the rules differ from state to state.
• Safe seats, turnout & polarization
   - A safe seat is a district one party is almost certain to win. A swing district could go either way.
   - Voters who always lose may feel their votes don't count, which can lower turnout.
   - In safe seats, the real race is often the party primary, which can pull candidates toward the extremes and add to polarization.
• Centrifugal & centripetal forces
   - Centrifugal forces divide a country: conflict between groups, unequal treatment, economic gaps, or distrust of government.
   - Centripetal forces unify it: a shared identity, national symbols, and trusted institutions like fair elections.
   - Strong centrifugal forces can lead to devolution, where power shifts to regions, as when Scotland got its own parliament inside the United Kingdom.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• The Green Revolution
   - The Green Revolution spread high-yield seeds, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation, and farm machines to developing countries in the mid-1900s.
   - It began in Mexico in the 1940s, where American plant scientist Norman Borlaug bred short, sturdy, high-yield wheat.
   - In the 1960s it reached India and Pakistan, and in 1966 a research institute in the Philippines released IR8, nicknamed “miracle rice.”
   - Borlaug won the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize because the extra food helped prevent famines.
• The three agricultural revolutions
   - First (Neolithic), about 10,000 years ago: people domesticated plants and animals in hearths like the Fertile Crescent and settled in villages.
   - Second, 1700s–1800s, alongside the Industrial Revolution: the seed drill, new crop rotations, and better transportation let fewer farmers feed more people, freeing workers for factories.
   - Third, from the mid-1900s: the Green Revolution's seeds, chemicals, irrigation, and machines, later joined by GMOs.
• Agribusiness & vertical integration
   - Agribusiness is farming run as a big business, from seeds and chemicals to processing, shipping, and selling.
   - Vertical integration means one company controls several steps of the same supply chain.
   - Tyson Foods, for example, supplies chicks and feed to contract farmers, then processes and sells the chicken under its own brand.
   - Green Revolution farmers came to depend on buying fertilizer and pesticides from these companies.
• GMOs
   - A GMO (genetically modified organism) has DNA changed in a lab, often by adding a gene from another species.
   - Bt corn makes its own insect-killing protein, and Golden Rice was engineered to provide vitamin A.
   - Supporters say GMOs raise yields; critics worry about corporate control of patented seeds.
   - GMO crops spread from the mid-1990s, after the original Green Revolution.
• Subsistence farming & staple crops
   - Subsistence farmers grow food mainly to feed their own families, not to sell.
   - Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn): clear and burn a rainforest plot, farm it a few years, then move on so it can recover, as in the Amazon and Central Africa.
   - Pastoral nomadism: moving herds of camels, goats, or cattle across dry lands like the Sahel and Mongolia.
   - In much of sub-Saharan Africa, small rain-fed farms grow staples like cassava, yams, sorghum, and millet, not the irrigated wheat and rice the Green Revolution was built for.
• Environmental costs of high-input farming
   - Irrigating thirsty high-yield crops can pump groundwater faster than rain refills it; in India's Punjab the water table has been falling for decades.
   - In dry places, irrigation water evaporates and leaves salt in the soil (salinization), which can ruin fields.
   - Heavy pesticide use can poison water and breed resistant pests.
   - Replacing thousands of local varieties with a few high-yield ones (monoculture) shrinks crop diversity, so one disease can wipe out more.
• Sustainable farming practices
   - Sustainable agriculture grows food today while protecting the soil, water, and wildlife farms need tomorrow.
   - Soil savers: no-till, cover crops, contour plowing, and terracing, like the centuries-old rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras.
   - Crop rotation and organic farming rebuild soil with legumes, compost, and manure instead of synthetic fertilizer.
   - Community-supported agriculture (CSA) and farmers' markets shorten the distance food travels and support small farms.
• Food deserts & food insecurity
   - Food insecurity means lacking reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food, and it happens in rich and poor countries alike.
   - A food desert is an area, often low-income, where residents live far from a supermarket and healthy food is hard to get.
   - In cities, many supermarket chains closed stores in poorer neighborhoods and moved to the suburbs, a pattern tied to disinvestment and redlining.
   - In rural areas, like the Mississippi Delta or the Navajo Nation, too few customers live nearby to support a big store, so families drive far for groceries.

Every factory sits where it does for a reason: close to its raw materials, its customers, or cheap workers. When those costs change, whole industries move, and the places they leave behind have to find a new way to make a living.


--- Part A (Identify) ---
Q: Identify ONE of the three location factors in Alfred Weber's least-cost theory of industrial location.
MODEL ANSWER: Transportation costs: the cost of moving raw materials to the factory and finished products to the market.

MINI-LESSON (Weber's least-cost theory):
 - Alfred Weber said in 1909 that a factory locates where its total costs are lowest.
 - Transportation (moving raw materials in and goods out) usually matters most, but much cheaper labor elsewhere can pull a factory away.
 - Agglomeration means firms save money by clustering near similar firms, like tech companies in Silicon Valley.

--- Part B (Describe) ---
Q: Describe why a bulk-reducing (weight-losing) industry, such as copper smelting, usually locates near its source of raw materials.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- A bulk-reducing industry locates near its raw materials because its finished product weighs much less than its inputs. E2- Copper ore is usually about 1 percent copper or less, so mills and smelters near mines in places like Arizona and Chile remove tons of waste rock to get a little metal. A3- Processing near the mine means only the lighter copper gets shipped, not heavy waste rock, which keeps transportation costs low.

MINI-LESSON (Bulk-reducing vs. bulk-gaining):
 - A bulk-reducing industry makes a product that weighs less than its raw materials, so it locates near them. Copper ore is about 1 percent copper or less.
 - A bulk-gaining industry does the opposite: soft-drink bottlers add water, so they locate near customers (the market).
 - Either way, the goal is to keep transportation costs low.
KEY WORDS: Bulk-reducing industry = An industry whose final product weighs less than its inputs, so it locates near raw materials.; Bulk-gaining industry = An industry whose final product weighs more than its inputs, so it locates near the market.
QUICK CHECK: Which industry is bulk-GAINING and usually locates near its market? → Soft-drink bottling (Bottlers add water, making the product heavier, so they locate near customers.)

A3- Because fuel and metal were cheap and close by, British owners could build steam-powered factories before any other country.
RUBRIC: Names a reason: coal, iron, capital, colonies, rivers/canals, workers, inventions, or political stability / Explains how it helped industry start: powered machines, paid for factories, or moved goods cheaply
MINI-LESSON (Why Britain went first):
 - Britain had coal and iron ore: coal fueled steam engines and iron built the machines.
 - Colonies and trade supplied raw cotton, profits to invest (capital), and markets; rivers and canals made shipping cheap.
 - The Agricultural Revolution pushed rural workers off the land and into factory jobs.
KEY WORDS: Industrial Revolution = The shift from making goods by hand to making them with machines and new power sources like steam.; Capital = Money saved up and invested to build businesses, like factories and machines.

--- Part D (Explain) ---
Q: Explain ONE cause of deindustrialization in the Rust Belt of the United States since the 1970s.

E2- After NAFTA (1994) and China's entry into the World Trade Organization (2001), many makers of auto parts and appliances opened plants in Mexico and China, where wages were far lower than in Ohio or Michigan. A3- Because the same products could be made more cheaply abroad, companies closed or shrank Rust Belt plants, which led to the loss of hundreds of thousands of factory jobs.
RUBRIC: Names a cause: automation, offshoring to lower-wage countries, or foreign competition / Explains how it cut Rust Belt jobs: lower costs elsewhere, fewer workers needed, plants closed or moved
MINI-LESSON (Why the factory jobs left):
 - Automation: robots do many factory tasks, so U.S. factories make far more than in 1980 with millions fewer workers.
 - Offshoring: companies moved production to lower-wage countries, like Mexico after NAFTA (1994) and China after it joined the WTO (2001).
 - Global competition: in the 1970s and 1980s, Japanese cars and cheaper foreign steel took customers from U.S. automakers and mills.
KEY WORDS: Deindustrialization = The loss of manufacturing jobs and factories in a region.; Automation = Using machines, robots, and computers to do work people used to do.; Offshoring = Moving production or other work to another country.
QUICK CHECK: Replacing assembly-line workers with robots is an example of… → automation (Automation means machines do work people used to do, so plants need fewer workers.)

--- Part E (Explain) ---
Q: Using ONE real Rust Belt city as an example, explain ONE economic or social effect of deindustrialization on that city.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- One effect of deindustrialization on Detroit, Michigan, was a huge loss of population, which shrank the city's tax base. E2- As auto plants closed or moved away, workers lost their jobs and left: Detroit fell from about 1.85 million people in 1950 to about 640,000 in 2020, leaving tens of thousands of houses vacant. A3- Because far fewer people and businesses paid taxes, the city struggled to pay for streetlights and police, which helped push Detroit into bankruptcy in 2013.

MINI-LESSON (After the factories close):
 - Economic effects: unemployment and a shrinking tax base, so the city has less money for schools, police, and streetlights.
 - Social effects: people move away, leaving vacant houses, polluted old factory sites (brownfields), and higher poverty. Youngstown, Ohio, shrank from about 170,000 people in 1950 to about 60,000.
 - Some cities reinvent themselves: Pittsburgh replaced steel jobs with service and quaternary jobs in hospitals, universities, and tech.

--- Part F (Explain) ---
Q: Explain how microloans can promote economic development, especially for women in developing countries.
MODEL ANSWER: T1- Microloans promote development by lending small amounts to poor people, especially women, who are shut out of regular banks because they have no collateral. E2- Bangladesh's Grameen Bank lends mostly to women, who use the money to buy a milk cow, a sewing machine, or a mobile phone to rent to neighbors, then repay from their sales. A3- Because she now earns her own income, a borrower can spend more on her children's food, health, and schooling and gains a bigger voice in family decisions, which reduces poverty and empowers women.
RUBRIC: Says what microloans do: small loans that let poor women shut out of banks start small businesses / Explains the payoff: income for her family, children's schooling and health, or empowerment
MINI-LESSON (Microloans: small money, big change):
 - Banks usually want collateral (property they can take if you don't pay), and poor women often own little or none. A microloan skips that.
 - Grameen Bank in Bangladesh lends mostly to women (about 97 percent of borrowers); it and its founder, Muhammad Yunus, won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
 - Women tend to spend earnings on their children's food, health, and schooling, so the benefits reach the next generation.

BACKGROUND POP-OUTS:
• Weber's least-cost theory
   - Alfred Weber's least-cost theory (1909) says a factory locates where its total costs are lowest.
   - His three factors: transportation (moving raw materials in and products out), labor (wages), and agglomeration (savings from clustering near similar firms).
   - Transportation usually matters most. Weber drew it as a triangle between two raw-material sources and one market.
• Bulk-reducing, bulk-gaining & break-of-bulk
   - A bulk-reducing (weight-losing) industry makes a product lighter than its inputs, so it locates near its raw materials, like copper smelting.
   - A bulk-gaining industry makes a product heavier, so it locates near its market, like soft-drink bottling, which adds water.
   - A break-of-bulk point, like a seaport, is where goods switch from one kind of transportation to another. Factories locate there to avoid loading and unloading twice.
• The Industrial Revolution
   - The Industrial Revolution, the shift from hand tools to machines powered by water and steam, began in Great Britain in the late 1700s.
   - Britain had coal and iron, capital from trade and colonies, rivers and canals, and workers pushed off farms by the Agricultural Revolution.
   - It spread to Western Europe and the U.S. in the early 1800s, Japan after 1868, and after World War II to newly industrialized countries like South Korea, then China.
• Deindustrialization & the Rust Belt
   - Deindustrialization is the loss of factory jobs in a region that once depended on them, like the U.S. Rust Belt around the Great Lakes.
   - U.S. factory jobs peaked at about 19.5 million in 1979, then fell by about a third because of automation, offshoring, and foreign competition.
   - Some Rust Belt cities, like Pittsburgh, shifted to service and quaternary jobs in hospitals, universities, and tech.
• The new international division of labor
   - The new international division of labor (NIDL) splits the steps of making one product among different countries.
   - Design, research, and marketing usually stay in wealthy core countries, while assembly moves to lower-wage countries.
   - Nike designs shoes in Oregon but has them made in Vietnam, Indonesia, and China, through outsourcing (hiring other companies) and offshoring (moving work abroad).
• Microloans
   - A microloan is a very small loan, often a few hundred dollars or less, for people too poor to borrow from regular banks.
   - Muhammad Yunus founded Grameen Bank in Bangladesh in 1983. About 97 percent of its borrowers are women, and he and the bank won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
   - Critics warn about high interest rates and tiny businesses that never grow, so microloans can't end poverty alone.
• Ecotourism
   - Ecotourism is travel to natural areas that aims to protect the environment and benefit local people.
   - Costa Rica protects about a quarter of its land in parks and reserves. Rwanda charges $1,500 for a mountain gorilla permit and shares park revenue with nearby villages.
   - Drawbacks: crowds damage fragile places, flights add carbon emissions, and profits can leak away to foreign-owned companies.
• The UN Sustainable Development Goals
   - Sustainable development means meeting today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
   - In 2015, UN members adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for 2030, like no poverty (Goal 1), gender equality (Goal 5), decent work (Goal 8), and life on land (Goal 15).
   - Microloans to women support Goals 1 and 5; well-run ecotourism supports Goals 8 and 15.
