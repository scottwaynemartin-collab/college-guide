### Source P1 (questions U2-P01–U2-P03)
> … [The Emperor] makes [his money] after this fashion. He makes them take of the bark of a certain tree, in fact of the Mulberry Tree … What they take is a certain fine white bast or skin which lies between the wood of the tree and the thick outer bark, and this they make into something resembling sheets of paper, but black. When these sheets have been prepared they are cut up into pieces of different sizes. … All these pieces of paper are issued with as much solemnity and authority as if they were of pure gold or silver … Any one forging it would be punished with death. …
> With these pieces of paper, made as I have described, he causes all payments on his own account to be made; and he makes them to pass current universally over all his kingdoms and provinces and territories, and whithersoever his power and sovereignty extends. And nobody, however important he may think himself, dares to refuse them on pain of death. And indeed everybody takes them readily, for wheresoever a person may go throughout the Great Kaan's* dominions he shall find these pieces of paper current, and shall be able to transact all sales and purchases of goods by means of them just as well as if they were coins of pure gold. And all the while they are so light that ten bezants' worth does not weigh one golden bezant.
> Furthermore all merchants arriving from India or other countries, and bringing with them gold or silver or gems and pearls, are prohibited from selling to any one but the Emperor. … the Emperor then pays a liberal price for them in those pieces of paper. … And with this paper-money they can buy what they like anywhere over the Empire, whilst it is also vastly lighter to carry about on their journeys. …
> Now you have heard the ways and means whereby the Great Kaan may have, and in fact has, more treasure than all the Kings in the World …
> * The Great Kaan (Khan) is Khubilai Khan, Great Khan of the Mongols from 1260 to 1294, who completed the Mongol conquest of China in 1279.
— Marco Polo, Venetian merchant who lived in China under Mongol rule from 1275 to 1292, describing the paper money of the Great Khan; his book was written down by Rustichello of Pisa in 1298 (Translated by Henry Yule, revised by Henri Cordier (1903); adapted)

**U2-P01** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 3.A]  Polo's main point in the passage is that the Great Khan
- (A) paid for his wars with the gold and silver taken from the many cities he conquered
- (B) issued paper money in his capital while the provinces kept using coins
- (C) switched to silver coins because paper money was too easy to forge
- (D) grew very rich by issuing paper money that people across his lands had to accept
KEY: D. Explanation: Polo explains how the Khan made money out of tree bark. Everyone had to accept it, and merchants had to trade their gold and gems for it. That is how Polo says the Khan got "more treasure than all the Kings in the World."
  - why not A: Polo does not talk about war loot. He says the Khan's treasure came from his paper money and the gold and gems he bought with it.
  - why not B: Polo says the paper money passed "over all his kingdoms and provinces and territories," not just in the capital.
  - why not C: Polo says the Khan did use paper, and forgers were "punished with death." He does not say the Khan switched to coins.
  - hint: Read the last paragraph. What does Polo say the Khan gained from this system?

**U2-P02** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 4.A · Continuity and Change]  The paper money Polo describes is best understood in the context of
- (A) the Mongols' invention of printing in China in the 1200s
- (B) Khubilai Khan's copying of paper money first issued by the Ilkhans of Persia
- (C) the use of paper money in China since the Song dynasty
- (D) a decline in trade within China under Mongol rule
KEY: C. Explanation: Paper money was not a Mongol idea. Song China used it in the 1000s and 1100s. The Mongols kept the system going after they conquered China.
  - why not A: Printing was much older than the Mongols. Chinese printers were carving wooden blocks by the 800s.
  - why not B: It went the other way. The Ilkhans of Persia copied Chinese paper money in 1294, long after China first used it.
  - why not D: Polo describes busy trade. Merchants could buy what they liked "anywhere over the Empire."
  - hint: Was paper money new in China when Polo arrived? Think about the dynasty before the Mongols.

> Now you must know that from this city of Cambaluc [Beijing] proceed many roads and highways leading to a variety of provinces … And the messengers of the Emperor in travelling from Cambaluc, be the road whichsoever they will, find at every twenty-five miles of the journey a station which they call Yamb, or, as we should say, the "Horse-Post-House." … At some of these stations, moreover, there shall be posted some four hundred horses standing ready for the use of the messengers …
> … For it is a fact that on all these posts taken together there are more than 300,000 horses kept up, specially for the use of the messengers. And the great buildings that I have mentioned are more than 10,000 in number, all richly furnished, as I told you. …
> … Moreover, there are also at those stations other men … who are employed for expresses when there is a call for great haste in sending despatches to any governor of a province, or to give news when any Baron has revolted, or in other such emergencies; and these men travel a good two hundred or two hundred and fifty miles in the day, and as much in the night. … And each of them carries with him a gerfalcon tablet, in sign that he is bound on an urgent express; so that if perchance his horse break down, or he meet with other mishap, whomsoever he may fall in with on the road, he is empowered to make him dismount and give up his horse. …

Thus from Damascus he carried away with him all the weavers of that city, those who worked at the silk looms. Further the bow-makers who produce those cross-bows which are so famous: likewise armourers: also the craftsmen in glass and porcelain, who are known to be the best in all the world. From Turkey he had brought … all men of other crafts wheresoever he found them, such as the silver-smiths and the masons. … Lastly hemp and flax had been sown and grown for the purpose in the Samarqand lands, where never before this crop had been cultivated.
> … The markets of Samarqand further are amply stored with merchandise imported from distant and foreign countries. From Russia and Tartary come leathers and linens, from Cathay silk stuffs that are the finest in the whole world … Thence too is brought musk which is found in no other land but Cathay, with balas rubies and diamonds …, also pearls, lastly rhubarb with many other spiceries. The goods that are imported to Samarqand from Cathay indeed are of the richest and most precious of all those brought thither from foreign parts, for the craftsmen of Cathay are reputed to be the most skilful by far beyond those of any other nation …
> From India there are brought to Samarqand the lesser spiceries, which indeed are the most costly of the kind, such as nutmegs and cloves and mace with cinnamon both in the flower and as bark, with ginger and manna: all these with many other kinds that are never to be found in the markets of Alexandria. …
> … During the month of June of this year, immediately before the date of our coming to Samarqand, there had arrived a caravan of eight hundred camels bringing merchandise from China. …
> * Timur (also called Tamerlane) was a conqueror from Central Asia who ruled from Samarkand from 1370 to 1405. His armies took Damascus, in Syria, in 1401.
— Ruy González de Clavijo, ambassador sent by King Henry III of Castile (in Spain) to the court of Timur, describing Samarkand, which he visited in 1404 (Translated by Guy Le Strange, Clavijo: Embassy to Tamerlane, 1403–1406 (1928); adapted)

**U2-E23** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 3.A]  Clavijo's description best supports which claim about Samarkand around 1404?
- (A) It drew goods mainly from China, India, and the steppe.
- (B) Its markets were filled with goods made in western Europe.
- (C) Its rulers had cut off trade with China after Mongol rule ended.
- (D) Its wealth came from silver mines near the city.
KEY: A. Explanation: Samarkand sat on the Silk Roads between China, India, Persia, and the steppe. Timur made it richer by encouraging trade and by forcing captured craftsmen to move there.
  - A: Correct. Clavijo lists silk and musk from Cathay, spices from India, and leather and linen from Russia and Tartary.
  - B: Incorrect. Clavijo names Cathay, India, Russia, and Tartary. He does not say European goods filled the markets.
  - C: Incorrect. A caravan of 800 camels had just arrived from China.
  - D: Incorrect. Clavijo stresses trade and crafts. He does not mention silver mines.

**U2-E24** [Topic 2.2 · LO D · Skill 5.A · Comparison]  Timur's practice of moving skilled workers to Samarkand, described in the first paragraph, was most similar to the practice of
- (A) Ming emperors who sent fleets to bring foreign rulers into their tribute system
- (B) Mali's rulers who welcomed Muslim scholars and builders to their courts
- (C) Mongol khans who moved captured craftsmen to Karakorum and other capitals
- (D) Abbasid caliphs who paid scholars to translate Greek works in Baghdad
KEY: C. Explanation: Timur, who claimed ties to Chinggis Khan's family, followed a Mongol habit. Conquerors carried off the best craftsmen by force to build up their own cities.
  - A: Incorrect. The Ming fleets used gifts and force to win tribute from foreign rulers. They did not move captured workers to China.
  - B: Incorrect. Mali's rulers invited scholars and builders, who came by choice and were paid. Timur took his craftsmen by force.
  - C: Correct. The Mongol khans also took skilled captives to work in their capitals. Some came from China and some from eastern Europe.
  - D: Incorrect. The Abbasids paid scholars to translate books. That supported learning, but it did not move captured workers.

**U2-E25** [Topic 2.1 · LO A · Skill 5.A · Continuity and Change]  The last paragraph of the passage best supports which conclusion about the decades after the Mongol Empire broke apart?
- (A) Overland trade between China and Central Asia continued under new rulers.
- (B) Sea trade had replaced the overland routes between China and Central Asia.
- (C) The Ming rulers of China had closed the country's borders to caravans.
- (D) Caravans from China now traveled straight through to the Black Sea ports.
KEY: A. Explanation: The Mongol Empire had split apart, and the Ming had ruled China since 1368. Yet caravans still crossed Central Asia. Trade on the Silk Roads changed and sometimes slowed, but it did not stop.
  - A: Correct. In 1404, decades after Mongol rule in China ended, a caravan of eight hundred camels arrived in Samarkand from China.
  - B: Incorrect. Sea trade grew, but the caravan of eight hundred camels shows that overland trade with China went on.
  - C: Incorrect. The Ming limited some foreign contacts, but a caravan bringing goods from China had just reached Samarkand.
  - D: Incorrect. Clavijo says the caravan came to Samarkand. He says nothing about caravans going on to the Black Sea.
