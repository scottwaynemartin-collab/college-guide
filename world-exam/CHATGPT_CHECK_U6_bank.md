### Source P1 (questions U6-P01–U6-P03)
> You will see that my view—and I think it may be called the scientific view of a nation—is that of an organized whole, kept up to a high pitch of internal efficiency by insuring that its numbers are substantially recruited from the better stocks, and kept up to a high pitch of external efficiency by contest, chiefly by way of war with inferior races,* and with equal races by the struggle for trade-routes and for the sources of raw material and of food supply. This is the natural history view of mankind, and I do not think you can in its main features subvert it. …
> I say it is possible for a small rural community to stand apart from the world-contest and to stagnate, if no more powerful nation wants its possessions. But are we such a community? Is it not a fact that the daily bread of our millions of workers depends on their having somebody to work for? that if we give up the contest for trade-routes and for free markets and for waste lands, we indirectly give up our food-supply? Is it not a fact that our strength depends on these and upon our colonies, and that our colonies have been won by the ejection of inferior races, and are maintained against equal races only by respect for their and our present power? …
> … Mankind as a whole, like the individual man, advances through pain and suffering only. The path of progress is strewn with the wreck of nations; traces are everywhere to be seen of the hecatombs of inferior races, and of victims who found not the narrow way to the greater perfection. Yet these dead peoples are, in very truth, the stepping-stones on which mankind has arisen to the higher intellectual and deeper emotional life of to-day.
> * Pearson's ranking of "inferior" and "equal" races reflects racist ideas that were common among European scientists of his time. Scientists today reject these ideas.
— Karl Pearson, British mathematician and scientist, National Life from the Standpoint of Science, a lecture given in Newcastle, England, in 1900 and published in 1901 (Adapted)

**U6-P01** [Topic 6.1 · LO A · Skill 1.B]  Pearson's ideas in the passage are best described as an example of
- (A) the civilizing mission, which claimed that Europeans had a duty to improve the peoples they ruled
- (B) Marxism, which explained history as a struggle between social classes inside each society
- (C) Social Darwinism, which applied ideas about natural selection to struggles among nations and races
- (D) liberalism, which held that free trade among nations would make wars less likely
KEY: C. Explanation: Social Darwinists used Darwin's ideas about nature to explain human groups. They said stronger groups win and weaker ones lose. Pearson says a nation stays strong through "war with inferior races." He says progress comes through "pain and suffering."
  - why not A: Pearson does not talk about helping or improving other peoples. He says nations get ahead by defeating and pushing out "inferior races."
  - why not B: Marx wrote about a struggle between classes. Pearson writes about a struggle between races and nations.
  - why not D: Pearson does not expect trade to bring peace. He calls trade one more "contest" between nations.
  - hint: Pearson says nations rise through struggle and that weaker peoples are pushed aside. Which set of ideas fits that view?

The natives of Central Africa are very desirous of trading, but their only traffic is at present in slaves, of which the poorer people have an unmitigated horror: it is therefore most desirable to encourage the former principle, and thus open a way for the consumption of free productions, and the introduction of Christianity and commerce. ### Source P3 (questions U6-P07–U6-P09)
> [The heads of state of Britain, Germany, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, Spain, the United States, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Sweden-Norway, and the Ottoman Empire,] wishing, in a spirit of good and mutual accord, to regulate the conditions most favourable to the development of trade and civilization in certain regions of Africa, and to assure to all nations the advantages of free navigation on the two chief rivers of Africa flowing into the Atlantic Ocean; being desirous, on the other hand, to obviate the misunderstanding and disputes which might in future arise from new acts of occupation … on the coast of Africa; and concerned, at the same time, as to the means of furthering the moral and material well-being of the native populations, have resolved … to meet for those purposes in Conference at Berlin …
> Article VI. All the Powers exercising sovereign rights or influence in the aforesaid territories* bind themselves to watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and to care for the improvement of the conditions of their moral and material well-being, and to help in suppressing slavery, and especially the slave trade. They shall, without distinction of creed or nation, protect and favour all religions, scientific, or charitable institutions, and undertakings created and organized for the above ends, or which aim at instructing the natives and bringing home to them the blessings of civilization. … Christian missionaries, scientists and explorers, with their followers, property, and collections, shall likewise be the objects of especial protection. …
> Article XXXIV. Any Power which henceforth takes possession of a tract of land on the coasts of the African Continent outside of its present possessions, or which, being hitherto without such possessions, shall acquire them, as well as the Power which assumes a Protectorate there, shall accompany the respective act with a notification thereof, addressed to the other Signatory Powers of the present Act, in order to enable them, if need be, to make good any claims of their own.
> Article XXXV. The Signatory Powers of the present Act recognize the obligation to insure the establishment of authority in regions occupied by them on the coasts of the African Continent sufficient to protect existing rights, and, as the case may be, freedom of trade and of transit under the conditions agreed upon.
> * Article VI applied to the Congo River basin and the nearby lands named earlier in the treaty.
— General Act of the Berlin Conference, signed by representatives of fourteen states, February 26, 1885 (English text as printed in Edward Hertslet, The Map of Africa by Treaty, 3rd ed., vol. 2 (London, 1909); adapted)

**U6-P07** [Topic 6.2 · LO B · Skill 2.B]  Articles XXXIV and XXXV were intended mainly to

- (B) divide Africa into fixed borders that each European power agreed to accept
- (C) set rules so that European powers could claim African land without fighting one another
- (D) require each European power to win the written consent of local African rulers before claiming their land
KEY: C. Explanation: The powers wanted to avoid "disputes" over new land claims. So Article XXXIV said a power must tell the others about a claim. Article XXXV said it must set up real control there.
  - why not A: The articles explain how to make claims official. They do not ban moving inland, and the powers soon pushed deep into the interior.
  - why not B: The conference did not draw a map of Africa. These articles set rules for future claims, and most borders were settled later in separate treaties.
  - why not D: The articles say a power must notify "the other Signatory Powers," not African rulers.
  - hint: To whom must a power send its "notification"? Why would that matter?
