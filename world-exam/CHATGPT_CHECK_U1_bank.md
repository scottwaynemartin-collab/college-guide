> … you arrive at the most noble city of Kinsay, a name which is as much as to say in our tongue "The City of Heaven" … for the city is beyond dispute the finest and the noblest in the world. In this we shall speak according to the written statement which the Queen of this Realm* sent to Bayan the conqueror of the country for transmission to the Great Kaan**, in order that he might be aware of the surpassing grandeur of the city and might be moved to save it from destruction or injury. …
> The document aforesaid also went on to state that there were in this city twelve guilds of the different crafts, and that each guild had 12,000 houses in the occupation of its workmen. … And yet all these craftsmen had full occupation, for many other cities of the kingdom are supplied from this city with what they require. The document aforesaid also stated that the number and wealth of the merchants, and the amount of goods that passed through their hands, was so enormous that no man could form a just estimate thereof. …
> … The waters of the latter [the river] fill a number of canals of all sizes which run through the different quarters of the city … Both streets and canals are so wide and spacious that carts on the one and boats on the other can readily pass to and fro, conveying necessary supplies to the inhabitants. … In each of the squares is held a market three days in the week, frequented by 40,000 or 50,000 persons, who bring thither for sale every possible necessary of life …
> * the Song empress dowager, grandmother of the boy emperor; she surrendered the city to the Mongol general Bayan in 1276
> ** Khubilai Khan, the Mongol ruler of China
— Marco Polo, Venetian merchant who lived in China under Mongol rule from 1275 to 1292, describing Hangzhou, which had been the capital of the Song dynasty; his book was written down by the author Rustichello of Pisa in 1298 (Translated by Henry Yule, revised by Henri Cordier (1903); adapted)

**U1-P01** [Topic 1.1 · LO C · Skill 3.B]  Polo's description of the city's crafts and merchants best supports which claim about Song China?
- (A) Its economy depended mostly on the labor of enslaved workers in its cities.
- (B) Its cities were centers of large-scale manufacturing and trade.
- (C) Its merchants were forbidden to sell goods to other cities.
- (D) Its craftsmen made goods mainly for the emperor and his officials at court.
KEY: B. Explanation: Polo describes guilds of craftsmen, very rich merchants, and busy markets. His numbers may be exaggerated, but his account shows that making and selling goods were central to Song city life.
  - why not A: Polo describes guilds of workmen and very wealthy merchants, not enslaved workers.
  - why not C: Polo says the city supplied "many other cities of the kingdom."
  - why not D: The goods were sold widely to other cities and in huge markets, not made just for the emperor.
  - hint: Look at what the guilds made and where their goods went.

**U1-P02** [Topic 1.1 · LO C · Skill 2.C]  Historians should be careful with the numbers in the second paragraph mainly because
- (A) Polo counted the houses himself and made mistakes in his arithmetic
- (B) Mongol officials kept no records of the city's population or trade
- (C) they come from a plea asking a conqueror to spare the city
- (D) the numbers describe Venice, which Polo was comparing to Hangzhou
KEY: C. Explanation: The figures came from a statement written to the Mongol general. Its goal was to make him spare the city. A writer with that goal had reason to make the city sound as grand as possible.
  - why not A: Polo says the numbers came from "the document aforesaid," not from his own count.
  - why not B: Polo does not say whether the Mongols kept records, and that is not why the numbers are doubtful.
  - why not D: The numbers describe Kinsay (Hangzhou), not Venice.
  - hint: Read the end of the first paragraph. Why was the document written?

> May it please your Majesty, I am informed that in consequence of the recent birth of a princess, a demand has been made on the Treasury for no less than 8,000 pieces of silk. Now the rigour of winter is just at its height, and the wretched workmen of the Dyeing Department, forced to break ice before they can get water, will suffer unspeakable hardships in supplying the amount required. > … The fact is that the Imperial bounty is too lavishly bestowed, and that extravagance is rife in the palace. Your Majesty suffers thereby: the State suffers thereby; and it is my duty to speak, trusting that your Majesty will take immediate steps to rectify these abuses.

— Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072), Chinese scholar-official who passed the highest civil service examination, memorial (formal letter of advice) to the Song emperor, written in the mid-1000s while he held a post that Giles translates as "Censor" (Translated by Herbert A. **U1-P04** [Topic 1.1 · LO A · Skill 1.B]  The memorial best illustrates which feature of Song government?

- (B) Scholar-officials were expected to advise and correct the emperor.
- (C) Buddhist monks controlled the treasury and decided how silk was used.
- (D) Wealthy merchants bought government posts and set the empire's tax rates.
KEY: B. Explanation: Song emperors relied on scholar-officials. Many earned their posts by passing exams on Confucian texts. Censors were expected to report wrongdoing and criticize bad decisions, even the emperor's.
  - why not A: The writer is a scholar-official serving as a censor, not a general.
  - why not C: The memorial does not mention Buddhist monks; the Treasury and the emperor decide spending.
  - why not D: Ouyang Xiu earned his post by passing the civil service exams, not by buying it.
  - hint: What was Ouyang Xiu's job, and what did it require him to do?

**Source 1**
> We maintain that the business of philosophy is nothing other than to look into creation and to ponder over it in order to be guided to the Creator … The Law* encourages and exhorts us to observe creation. … But the Law urges us to observe creation by means of reason and demands the knowledge thereof through reason. …
> … if before us, somebody has enquired into it, we should derive help from what he has said. It is quite immaterial whether that man is our co-religionist or not … By these latter we mean those Ancients who investigated these things before the advent of Islam. … All that is required of us is that we should go back to their books and see what they have said in this connection. > * the Sharia, the religious law of Islam, based on the Quran and the Sunnah (the Prophet Muhammad's teachings and example)
— Ibn Rushd (known in Europe as Averroes, 1126–1198), Muslim judge and philosopher in al-Andalus (Muslim-ruled Spain), The Decisive Treatise, about 1180 (Translated by Mohammad Jamil-ur-Rehman, The Philosophy and Theology of Averroes (1921); adapted)
**Source 2**
> … It was necessary for man's salvation that there should be a knowledge revealed by God, besides philosophical science built up by human reason. … it was necessary for the salvation of man that certain truths which exceed human reason should be made known to him by divine revelation. …

Since therefore grace does not destroy nature, but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith … Hence sacred doctrine makes use also of the authority of philosophers in those questions in which they were able to know the truth by natural reason …
